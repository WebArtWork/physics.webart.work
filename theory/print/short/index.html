Short theories

1. What Physics Studies: Phenomena, Models and Laws

Physics investigates matter, energy, motion and interactions through observations, controlled experiments and mathematical models. A model keeps essential features and leaves out others. A 5 m long car can be treated as a point when studying a 100 km trip. It cannot be treated as a point when parking in a 5.2 m space. A law describes a reliable relationship, while a theory explains a connected set of observations. Every conclusion has a domain where its assumptions hold.

2. Physical Quantities and SI Units

Every physical measurement consists of a number and a unit. The International System of Units uses seven base units, including metre (m), kilogram (kg) and second (s), so that measurements can be compared worldwide. For example, 72 km/h equals 20 m/s because 72 · 1000 / 3600 = 20. A result written as 20 without a unit is incomplete, and units must be converted before substitution into equations.

3. Measurement Uncertainty and Significant Figures

No measurement gives an infinitely exact number. An instrument with 1 mm markings cannot reliably distinguish every possible fraction of a millimetre, and repeated readings may differ. For a measured length 12.4 ± 0.1 cm, the uncertainty is 0.1 cm and the relative uncertainty is approximately 0.8%. Report enough digits to reflect the evidence, not the number of digits on a calculator.

4. Dimensional Analysis and Estimation

Dimensional analysis checks whether an equation uses compatible kinds of quantities. You may add 3 m to 2 m, but not to 2 s. The period of a simple pendulum can depend on length l and gravity g as √(l / g), which has the unit second. A quick estimate then tests whether a numerical answer is plausible. Matching dimensions is necessary but does not prove a formula physically correct.

5. Scalars and Vectors

A scalar has size only, while a vector has both magnitude and direction. Mass 3 kg is a scalar, but a displacement 3 m east is a vector. Walking 3 m east and then 4 m north gives a 5 m displacement northeast, not 7 m, although the path length is 7 m. Vector directions must be included whenever motion or forces are combined.

6. Graphs in Physics

Graphs turn numerical measurements into patterns you can read. On a distance-time graph a straight line that rises 30 m over 10 s has slope 3 m/s; on a speed-time graph a rectangle 3 m/s high and 10 s wide represents 30 m travelled. Always read the axis labels before interpreting the slope or area. A straight line does not automatically mean that a body moves at constant speed.

7. Mechanical Motion and Frames of Reference

Mechanical motion means a body changes position relative to a chosen reference body as time passes. A passenger seated in a moving train is at rest relative to the carriage but moving relative to the platform. To describe the trip you need a reference body, coordinates and a clock, together called a frame of reference. The train may be treated as a material point for a long trip, but not for checking whether it fits beside a platform.

8. Trajectory, Path Length and Displacement

A trajectory is the line traced by a moving body. Path length is the total distance travelled along that line; displacement is the directed straight segment from the initial point to the final point. If you walk 40 m east and return 40 m west, your path is 80 m but displacement is zero. Path can never be negative, and displacement depends on the chosen coordinate directions.

9. Average and Instantaneous Velocity

Average speed compares total path with total time; average velocity compares displacement with total time and includes direction. A runner who completes a 400 m lap in 100 s has average speed 4.0 m/s but average velocity zero. Instantaneous velocity describes motion at one moment and points along the trajectory. On trips with different speeds, you must divide the whole path by the whole time rather than blindly averaging speed readings.

10. Uniform Straight-Line Motion

Uniform straight-line motion means velocity stays constant in magnitude and direction. Position then follows x = x₀ + vt. A bus at x₀ = 20 m moving at 5 m/s reaches x = 70 m after 10 s. Its position-time graph is a straight line and its velocity-time graph is horizontal. The rule does not describe an accelerating bus or a vehicle turning a corner.

11. Acceleration

Acceleration measures how quickly velocity changes, including changes in direction. A car that increases velocity from 4 m/s to 16 m/s in 3 s has average acceleration (16 − 4) / 3 = 4 m/s². Positive acceleration along an axis does not always mean a body speeds up: if velocity points in the negative direction, a positive acceleration may slow it down.

12. Uniformly Accelerated Straight-Line Motion

With constant acceleration in a straight line, velocity changes by equal amounts during equal times. Position follows x = x₀ + v₀t + at² / 2. A car starting from rest with a = 2.0 m/s² reaches 10 m/s after 5 s and travels 25 m. Braking distance increases with the square of starting speed when deceleration has the same magnitude, so doubling speed can quadruple braking distance.

13. Motion Graphs

Position, velocity and acceleration graphs offer three views of the same motion. The slope of an x–t graph is velocity, the slope of a v–t graph is acceleration, and the signed area under a v–t graph is displacement. For a velocity increasing linearly from 0 to 8 m/s in 4 s, acceleration is 2 m/s² and displacement is the triangle area 16 m. Check whether plotted velocity is negative before interpreting area as distance.

14. Relative Motion and Velocity Addition

Velocity depends on the observer. A passenger walks forward at 2 m/s inside a train that travels at 20 m/s relative to the ground; the walking passenger moves at 22 m/s relative to the ground if both velocities align. Velocities add as vectors, so a boat crossing a river can drift sideways. These Galilean rules work well at everyday speeds, but not when speeds approach that of light.

15. Free Fall and Vertical Throw

In free fall without air resistance, every body near Earth accelerates downward at approximately g = 9.8 m/s² regardless of its mass. A ball dropped from rest falls about 4.9 m in the first second. A ball thrown vertically upward slows, stops momentarily at its highest point, and then falls. The upward and downward times are equal only when it returns to the same height and air resistance is neglected.

16. Horizontal Launch

An object launched horizontally keeps its horizontal velocity while gravity increases its downward speed, provided air drag is negligible. A ball rolling off a 19.6 m high platform at 5.0 m/s takes 2.0 s to fall and lands 10 m horizontally from the edge. Its path is a parabola. The time of fall depends on height, not on horizontal launch speed, within this ideal model.

17. Projectile Launched at an Angle

A projectile launched at an angle combines steady horizontal motion with vertically accelerated motion under gravity. At 20 m/s and 30° above the horizontal, the initial vertical component is 10 m/s. For equal launch and landing heights without air resistance, the ideal range is v₀² sin(2θ)/g and is greatest at 45°. This rule does not apply automatically to a ball landing on a different level or moving through strong air resistance.

18. Uniform Circular Motion: Period, Frequency and Angular Velocity

In uniform circular motion, a body travels around a circle at constant speed but continually changes velocity direction. One revolution takes a period T, and the number of revolutions per second is frequency f = 1/T. A wheel making 2 revolutions each second has angular velocity ω = 4π rad/s. Points at larger radii on the same rigid wheel have greater linear speeds, though they share the same angular velocity.

19. Centripetal Acceleration

A body moving in a circle needs acceleration directed toward the centre, even if its speed stays constant. For a car moving around a 50 m radius curve at 10 m/s, centripetal acceleration is a = v²/R = 2.0 m/s². A greater speed increases this acceleration quadratically, so doubling speed quadruples the required value. It is a change of velocity direction, not an inward speed.

20. Newton's First Law and Inertial Frames

Newton’s first law states that a body remains at rest or moves in a straight line with constant velocity when the net external force is zero, provided the reference frame is inertial. A passenger lurches forward when a bus brakes because the passenger’s body tends to keep moving. A seat belt supplies the force needed to slow the passenger safely. The law does not say that motion requires a continuous forward force.

21. Mass and Density

Mass measures inertia, while density compares mass with volume: ρ = m / V. A 2.0 kg piece of material occupying 0.001 m³ has density 2000 kg/m³. Mass does not change when the object is moved to the Moon, although its weight changes. The density formula works for a body only when its measured mass and volume refer to the same sample.

22. Force and Net Force

A force describes an interaction that can change a body’s velocity or shape. Forces are vectors, so 8 N to the right and 3 N to the left produce a net force of 5 N to the right, not 11 N. A spring dynamometer measures force. Add force vectors acting on the same body before deciding how it moves.

23. Newton's Second Law

Newton’s second law links net force and acceleration: ΣF = ma for a constant-mass body in an inertial frame. A 3.0 kg trolley acted on by a net 12 N accelerates at 4.0 m/s² in the direction of the force. A stronger net force gives more acceleration; a larger mass gives less acceleration under the same net force.

24. Newton's Third Law

When one body pushes a second, the second simultaneously pushes the first with an equally large opposite force. A person pushes a wall with 40 N, and the wall pushes the person back with 40 N. These forces do not cancel because they act on different bodies. This is Newton’s third law, essential for walking, swimming and rocket motion.

25. Free-Body Diagrams and Solving Dynamics Problems

A free-body diagram separates one object from its surroundings and shows every external force acting on it. For a 4.0 kg block on a horizontal floor, gravity is 39.2 N down and the normal force is 39.2 N up when there is no vertical acceleration. This diagram prevents force omissions when applying Newton’s second law.

26. Law of Universal Gravitation

Any two masses attract gravitationally. Newton’s law gives F = Gm₁m₂ / r², so doubling the separation cuts the force to one quarter. Gravity holds satellites in orbit and contributes to the falling of objects. For spherical bodies you measure r between their centres; the inverse-square form is not a universal formula for arbitrary irregular nearby masses.

27. Gravity, Weight, Weightlessness and Overload

Gravity is the force mg exerted by Earth, whereas weight is the force a body exerts on its support or suspension. A 60 kg person at rest has weight about 588 N. In an elevator accelerating upward at 2.0 m/s², the scale reads 708 N. In free fall a scale reads zero even though gravity still acts.

28. Elastic Force and Hooke's Law

A spring resists stretching or compression. Within its elastic range, Hooke’s law says restoring force is proportional to displacement: F = −kx. A spring with k = 200 N/m stretched by 0.050 m pulls back with magnitude 10 N. Beyond its elastic limit the proportionality may fail and permanent deformation can occur.

29. Friction Forces

Friction resists relative sliding or the tendency to slide between surfaces. Static friction adjusts up to a limit, while kinetic friction approximately obeys Fₖ = μₖN. On level ground a 10 kg sliding box with μₖ = 0.20 experiences about 19.6 N of friction. Friction makes walking and braking possible, but it also wastes energy as heat.

30. Motion on an Inclined Plane

On an inclined plane gravity splits into a component mg sin α down the slope and mg cos α perpendicular to it. A frictionless 5.0 kg sled on a 30° slope accelerates down at 4.9 m/s². Friction reduces this acceleration and may prevent sliding if the maximum static friction is sufficient.

31. Connected Bodies: Strings and Pulleys

Connected bodies share motion constraints imposed by strings and pulleys. For two hanging masses of 3.0 kg and 2.0 kg joined over an ideal pulley, the heavier side descends with acceleration 1.96 m/s². Both masses have the same acceleration magnitude, and an ideal massless string transmits the same tension throughout.

32. Dynamics of Circular Motion

Circular motion needs a net force toward the centre: ΣF_radial = mv²/R. A 1000 kg car turning at 10 m/s on a level curve of radius 50 m requires 2000 N of inward force, usually supplied by tyre-road friction. “Centripetal force” names the resultant radial force, not an extra force added to the diagram.

33. Satellite Motion and Cosmic Velocities

A satellite can remain in orbit because gravity continuously bends its path. For a circular orbit of radius r around a spherical planet, speed is v = √(GM/r). Near Earth’s surface the ideal circular-orbit speed is about 7.9 km/s; Earth’s escape speed at the same location is about 11.2 km/s without air resistance. Real low orbits lie above the atmosphere.

34. Drag Forces and Terminal Velocity

Drag opposes motion through air or water and usually grows as speed rises. A falling person speeds up until downward gravity and upward drag balance; then acceleration becomes zero and terminal speed is reached. A parachute increases effective drag area, lowering terminal speed, but the exact result depends on body shape and airflow.

35. Non-Inertial Frames and Inertial Forces

An accelerating or rotating reference frame is non-inertial. A passenger in a bus accelerating forward appears to be pushed backward relative to the bus, although no real backward interaction is required. Introducing an inertial or fictitious force makes Newton-like equations usable in the accelerating frame, provided its origin is clearly identified.

36. Moment of Force and Conditions of Equilibrium

For a body to remain at rest without turning, both its net force and net torque must be zero. Torque measures the turning tendency of a force: τ = Fd, where d is the perpendicular lever arm. A 20 N force applied 0.30 m from a hinge gives torque magnitude 6.0 N·m, provided it is perpendicular to the lever.

37. Center of Mass and Types of Equilibrium

The centre of mass is the mass-weighted average position of a body or system. A 2.0 kg mass at x = 0 m and a 1.0 kg mass at x = 3.0 m have centre of mass at x = 1.0 m. A standing object tips when the vertical line through its centre of mass passes beyond its base of support.

38. Simple Machines and the Golden Rule of Mechanics

Simple machines exchange force for distance. A lever, pulley or inclined plane can make a heavy load easier to move, but an ideal machine does not reduce the total work. With a twofold mechanical advantage, pulling 4.0 m moves a load only 2.0 m. Real machines need extra input because friction and deformation reduce efficiency.

39. Rotation of a Rigid Body and Moment of Inertia

Rotational dynamics uses angular speed, angular acceleration and moment of inertia. A solid disk has I = MR²/2 about its central axis, so a 2.0 kg disk of radius 0.50 m has I = 0.25 kg·m². For fixed-axis rotation the net torque obeys τ = Iα, linking turning effect to angular acceleration.

40. Angular Momentum and Its Conservation

Angular momentum describes rotational motion and remains conserved if the external net torque is zero. For fixed-axis rotation L = Iω. A skater who pulls their arms inward reduces moment of inertia and spins faster. For example, if I halves while angular momentum stays constant, angular speed doubles.

41. Momentum and Impulse

Momentum describes how difficult it is to stop a moving object: p = mv. A force acting over a time interval produces an impulse J = FΔt = Δp. A 0.20 kg ball moving at 10 m/s carries 2.0 kg·m/s of momentum. Extending the stopping time reduces the average force for the same change in momentum, which is why helmets and airbags matter.

42. Conservation of Momentum

In a system whose net external impulse is zero, total momentum stays constant even when objects collide or push each other. Two skaters initially at rest can move apart with equal and opposite momenta. If a 60 kg skater moves at 2 m/s to the right, a 40 kg skater moves at 3 m/s to the left. Conservation applies separately along each coordinate axis.

43. Jet Propulsion

Jet propulsion works when an object expels matter in one direction and receives momentum in the opposite direction. A rocket can accelerate in a vacuum because it pushes exhaust gases backward, not because it pushes against air. For instance, ejecting 2 kg of gas each second at 500 m/s relative to a rocket creates approximately 1000 N of thrust under steady conditions.

44. Mechanical Work

Mechanical work measures energy transferred when a force acts through a displacement. For a constant force, W = Fs cos α, where α is the angle between force and displacement. Pulling a box 4 m with a 30 N horizontal force does 120 J of work. A force perpendicular to motion, such as the normal force on a level floor, does zero work.

45. Power and Efficiency

Power tells you how quickly work is done or energy is transferred: P = W / t. Efficiency compares useful output with total input: η = W_useful / W_input. A hoist that lifts a 50 kg load through 2 m in 5 s provides about 196 W of useful power. If its efficiency is 80%, the input power must be about 245 W.

46. Kinetic Energy and the Work–Energy Theorem

Kinetic energy is energy due to motion: Eₖ = mv² / 2. The net work done on an object equals its change in kinetic energy. A 1000 kg car travelling at 20 m/s has 200,000 J of kinetic energy. Doubling its speed quadruples this energy, which is why stopping distance tends to grow strongly with speed when braking force is roughly constant.

47. Potential Energy

Potential energy belongs to an interaction or configuration rather than motion. Near Earth, gravitational potential energy can be written U = mgh relative to a chosen zero height. A 2 kg book on a shelf 1.5 m above the floor has 29.4 J of gravitational potential energy relative to the floor. A stretched spring stores U = kx² / 2 if Hooke’s law applies.

48. Conservation of Mechanical Energy

When only conservative forces do work, the sum of kinetic and potential energy stays constant: Eₖ + U = constant. A ball released from a height of 5 m falls faster as its height decreases; ignoring air resistance, its speed just before reaching the ground is about 9.9 m/s. Friction does not destroy energy, but converts mechanical energy into internal energy.

49. Elastic and Inelastic Collisions

Collisions always conserve the total momentum of an isolated system. In an elastic collision, total kinetic energy is also conserved; in an inelastic collision, some of it becomes deformation, heat or sound. Two equal-mass carts moving along one line exchange velocities in a perfectly elastic head-on collision. If carts stick together, they share one final velocity.

50. Pressure

Pressure describes how much perpendicular force acts on each unit area: p = F / A. The same 200 N force produces 20,000 Pa on an area of 0.010 m² but just 2000 Pa on 0.10 m². Sharp knives increase pressure by reducing contact area; wide skis reduce pressure on snow. Pressure is a scalar, unlike force.

51. Hydrostatic Pressure and Communicating Vessels

In a liquid at rest, pressure increases with depth: the extra pressure below the surface is p = ρgh. At 2.0 m depth in fresh water, the gauge pressure is about 19.6 kPa. At the same depth, it is independent of the container’s shape. Connected vessels containing the same liquid and exposed to the same pressure have equal free-surface levels.

52. Pascal's Law and the Hydraulic Press

Pascal’s law says that a change of pressure applied to an enclosed fluid is transmitted throughout that fluid. A hydraulic press uses a small input force on a small piston to create a larger force on a larger piston. If piston areas are 0.002 m² and 0.020 m², a 100 N input produces an ideal 1000 N output. The larger piston moves a shorter distance.

53. Atmospheric Pressure

Air has weight, so the atmosphere exerts pressure on surfaces. At sea level a representative standard atmospheric pressure is 101,325 Pa, or about 760 mmHg. A barometer measures this pressure; weather systems and altitude cause variations. The pressure decreases as you climb because less air lies above you.

54. Archimedes' Principle and Flotation

An immersed object experiences an upward buoyant force equal to the weight of displaced fluid. This is Archimedes’ principle: F_b = ρ_fluid g V_submerged. A block displacing 0.003 m³ of water experiences 29.4 N of buoyancy. An object floats when buoyancy balances its weight; its average density relative to the fluid determines how much is submerged.

55. Fluid Flow and the Continuity Equation

In steady incompressible flow, the same volume of liquid passes each section of a pipe per second. The continuity equation is A₁v₁ = A₂v₂. When a hose narrows from 4 cm² to 1 cm², water moving at 0.5 m/s speeds up to 2.0 m/s. Flow can be smooth and laminar or irregular and turbulent; the same conservation of mass still applies.

56. Bernoulli's Equation and Its Applications

Bernoulli’s equation links fluid pressure, speed and height along a streamline: p + ρv²/2 + ρgh = constant. In a level ideal pipe, faster flow has lower static pressure. If water speeds up from 2 m/s to 4 m/s, static pressure falls by 6000 Pa. This principle helps explain Venturi meters and atomizers, but real fluid friction causes additional losses.

57. Main Ideas of Molecular-Kinetic Theory

Molecular-kinetic theory explains matter using particles. Substances consist of atoms, molecules or ions; these particles move continually and randomly and interact by attraction and repulsion. Perfume spreading across a room illustrates diffusion. Heating usually speeds up molecular motion. The model connects observable temperature, pressure and material properties with events too small to see directly.

58. Amount of Substance and the Avogadro Constant

The mole counts enormous numbers of microscopic particles. One mole contains exactly 6.02214076 · 10²³ specified entities. Amount of substance is ν = m / M = N / N_A. For water with molar mass about 18 g/mol, 36 g contains 2.0 mol and about 1.20 · 10²⁴ molecules. Always state what entities are being counted: atoms, molecules, ions or another specified unit.

59. Brownian Motion and Diffusion

Brownian motion is the irregular jitter of tiny particles suspended in a liquid or gas, caused by countless impacts from surrounding molecules. Diffusion is the net spreading of particles from regions of higher concentration toward lower concentration. A drop of dye spreads through still water even without stirring. Both effects provide evidence of continual molecular motion and often become faster at higher temperatures.

60. Ideal Gas and the Basic Equation of Molecular-Kinetic Theory

An ideal gas is a simplified model whose particles occupy negligible volume and interact mainly in brief elastic collisions. Its pressure comes from momentum transferred when particles hit container walls. The basic molecular-kinetic equation is p = (1/3)n m₀ v_rms² = (2/3)n Ēₖ. At fixed particle concentration, faster molecular motion produces higher pressure.

61. Temperature and Thermal Equilibrium

Temperature tells us whether energy will flow as heat when two bodies touch. When the flow stops, they have reached thermal equilibrium. A room at 20 °C has temperature 293.15 K, while boiling water at ordinary pressure is near 100 °C. Temperature is not the same as the amount of heat or internal energy stored in an object.

62. Temperature and the Kinetic Energy of Molecules

For an ideal monatomic gas, absolute temperature is proportional to the average kinetic energy of random translational motion: Ēₖ = 3kT / 2. At 300 K each molecule has on average about 6.21 · 10⁻²¹ J of translational kinetic energy, irrespective of molecular mass. Lighter molecules have a higher typical speed at the same temperature.

63. Ideal Gas Equation of State

The ideal gas equation connects pressure, volume, amount of gas and absolute temperature: pV = νRT. It predicts how a sealed balloon's pressure changes when warmed and how much gas occupies a given volume. For example, one mole of ideal gas near 300 K and 100 kPa occupies about 0.0249 m³, or 24.9 L.

64. Isoprocesses in Gases

An isoprocess is a gas-state change in which one of pressure, volume or temperature remains constant. Boyle's law says pV is constant at fixed temperature; at fixed pressure, volume grows with absolute temperature; at fixed volume, pressure grows with absolute temperature. Halving a gas's volume at constant temperature doubles its pressure if the ideal-gas model applies.

65. Evaporation, Boiling and Saturated Vapor

Evaporation occurs at a liquid surface at many temperatures, while boiling begins throughout the liquid when its saturated vapour pressure reaches the surrounding pressure. In a closed container saturated vapour is in dynamic equilibrium with liquid. Water boils below 100 °C on a high mountain because ambient pressure is lower, and above 100 °C in a pressure cooker.

66. Air Humidity

Relative humidity states how close air's water-vapour pressure is to saturation at the same temperature. At 20 °C, if vapour pressure is 1.17 kPa and saturation pressure is about 2.34 kPa, relative humidity is 50%. Warm air can have a higher saturation vapour pressure, so warming a room without adding moisture usually lowers relative humidity.

67. Surface Tension

Surface tension makes a liquid surface behave as though it resists being stretched. It helps explain why small drops become nearly spherical and why some insects can rest on water. At room temperature the surface tension of clean water is approximately 0.072 N/m. Soap lowers this value, so water can wet dirty surfaces more readily.

68. Wetting and Capillary Action

Wetting describes how strongly a liquid spreads over a solid. Water wets clean glass and makes a concave meniscus; mercury does not wet glass and makes a convex one. In a narrow clean glass tube, water can rise above the surrounding reservoir because surface tension has an upward component along the wall.

69. Crystalline and Amorphous Solids

A crystal has long-range ordered atomic arrangement; an amorphous solid lacks such periodic order. Table salt forms crystals with repeating structure, while ordinary glass is amorphous. Many crystals melt over a relatively narrow temperature interval, but glasses typically soften over a range rather than having one sharp melting point.

70. Mechanical Properties of Solids

Solids respond to forces by changing shape or size. Small elastic deformation disappears when the load is removed, while plastic deformation remains. A metal wire lengthens under tension, and its stress is the applied force per cross-sectional area. Within the elastic range, stress is approximately proportional to relative strain.

71. Thermal Expansion

Most materials expand when heated because the average spacing between their particles increases. A 10 m steel rail with linear expansion coefficient 12 · 10⁻⁶ K⁻¹ lengthens by 4.8 mm after a 40 °C increase. Expansion joints in bridges and rails prevent harmful stress when temperature changes.

72. Internal Energy and Ways to Change It

Internal energy is the energy associated with particles' microscopic random motion and their interactions. It can increase when you heat an object or do work on it, such as compressing air in a pump. For one mole of an ideal monatomic gas at 300 K, internal energy is approximately 3.74 kJ.

73. Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation

Energy transfers as heat by conduction, convection and radiation. A metal spoon warms by conduction, circulating air carries heat by convection, and sunlight reaches Earth by radiation. A vacuum flask limits conduction, convection and radiation using several different layers; no single insulating material blocks all three processes perfectly.

74. Quantity of Heat and Specific Heat Capacity

Specific heat capacity tells us how much energy is needed to raise the temperature of one kilogram of material by one kelvin without changing its phase. Liquid water has a high specific heat capacity of about 4200 J/(kg·K), so heating 2 kg of water by 10 °C takes roughly 84 kJ. The oceans' heat capacity moderates daily and seasonal temperature changes.

75. Melting, Vaporization and Latent Heat

During melting and boiling, a substance can absorb energy while remaining at nearly constant temperature. This energy changes molecular arrangement or separation rather than simply raising average kinetic energy. Melting 1.0 kg of ice at 0 °C takes approximately 334 kJ, not counting any warming before or after the phase change.

76. Fuel Combustion and Heat of Combustion

Fuel combustion releases chemical energy, part of which can be transferred as heat. The specific heat of combustion gives the energy released by burning one kilogram of a fuel completely. If a fuel releases 40 MJ/kg, burning 0.50 kg releases 20 MJ before losses. Real heaters deliver only a fraction of that energy to the intended object.

77. Heat Balance Equation

In a sufficiently insulated system, heat lost by warmer bodies equals heat gained by colder bodies. This is the heat balance equation, a practical form of energy conservation. Mixing equal masses of water at 20 °C and 60 °C gives 40 °C if there is no heat loss and the container's heat capacity is negligible.

78. Work Done by a Gas

A gas can perform mechanical work when it expands against its surroundings. At constant pressure the work done by the gas equals pressure times the change in volume. Expanding from 0.020 m³ to 0.030 m³ against 100 kPa gives 1000 J of positive work. Compression produces negative work by the gas under this sign convention.

79. First Law of Thermodynamics

The first law of thermodynamics expresses energy conservation for thermal processes: energy supplied as heat changes a system's internal energy and can be spent doing work. If a gas receives 500 J of heat and does 200 J of work, its internal energy increases by 300 J. A machine cannot continually supply more energy than it receives without drawing on some energy store.

80. First Law for Isoprocesses and the Adiabatic Process

The first law takes useful special forms for ideal-gas processes. At constant volume, the gas does no pressure–volume work; at constant temperature, an ideal gas has no change in internal energy; and in an adiabatic process no heat crosses the boundary. A rapidly compressed gas can become hotter because work is done on it.

81. Heat Engines and Their Efficiency

A heat engine repeatedly converts some heat taken from a hot source into useful work while rejecting the remaining heat to a cooler environment. If it absorbs 1000 J, rejects 650 J and returns to its starting state, its work is 350 J and its efficiency is 35%. No cyclic heat engine can convert all absorbed heat into work.

82. The Carnot Cycle and Maximum Efficiency

The Carnot cycle gives the theoretical upper limit on heat-engine efficiency between two fixed absolute temperatures. A reversible engine operating between 600 K and 300 K can reach at most 50% efficiency. Real engines achieve less because friction, finite-temperature heat transfer and other irreversible effects waste useful work.

83. Second Law of Thermodynamics and Entropy

The second law of thermodynamics specifies which energy changes can occur spontaneously. Heat naturally flows from hotter to colder objects; transferring it back requires external work or another compensating change. Entropy quantifies the direction of irreversible processes. In an isolated system total entropy cannot decrease.

84. Refrigerators and Heat Pumps

A refrigerator uses work to move heat from a cooler space into warmer surroundings. A heat pump uses the same principle but values the heat delivered to the warm space. A heat pump delivering 4 kWh of heat for 1 kWh of electrical input has a heating coefficient of performance of 4, not an energy efficiency of 400% in the heat-engine sense.

85. Electric Charge and Its Conservation

Electric charge is a property of matter responsible for electric interactions. Positive and negative charges attract when unlike and repel when alike. Charge is conserved in an isolated system: rubbing a plastic object can move electrons between materials but cannot create net charge. A charge of −3.2 · 10⁻¹⁹ C corresponds to two additional electrons.

86. Coulomb's Law

Coulomb's law gives the magnitude of the electrostatic force between two stationary point charges. It grows with the product of charge magnitudes and decreases as the inverse square of their separation. Two charges of 1 μC each, 0.10 m apart in vacuum, repel with a force of about 0.90 N.

87. Electric Field and Field Strength

An electric field describes the force that a charge would experience at a location without requiring contact with another charge. Field strength E is force per positive test charge. A 2 μC charge in a uniform field of 300 N/C experiences 6.0 · 10⁻⁴ N along the field if it is positive.

88. Field Lines and the Superposition Principle

Electric field lines are a drawing tool: their tangent gives the local direction of the electric field, and a denser pattern represents a stronger field when lines are drawn consistently. Fields from different charges combine by vector superposition. Two equal fields of 100 N/C pointing in opposite directions cancel to zero at the point considered.

89. Conductors and Dielectrics in an Electric Field

A conductor contains mobile charges that move until electrostatic equilibrium is reached. In that equilibrium, the electric field inside the conducting material is zero and excess net charge lies on its surface. Dielectrics instead respond mainly by electric polarisation, changing the field and often increasing capacitor capacitance.

90. Work of the Electric Field, Potential and Voltage

Electric potential describes electric potential energy per unit charge. Voltage is the potential difference between two points and determines the work a static electric field does when charge moves between them. A 2 C positive charge moving through a 3 V potential drop releases 6 J of electric potential energy.

91. Field Strength and Voltage in a Uniform Field

In an approximately uniform electric field, voltage between two points separated along the field direction equals field strength times distance. Therefore E = U/d for a suitable pair of parallel plates. A 120 V potential difference across a 4.0 mm gap gives 30,000 V/m if the field is uniform.

92. Capacitance and Capacitors

Capacitance measures how much charge a capacitor stores per volt across its terminals. A capacitor of 10 μF at 5 V carries charge magnitude 50 μC on each plate. Capacitance depends chiefly on conductor geometry and intervening material, not on the voltage for an ideal linear capacitor.

93. Capacitor Combinations and Electric Field Energy

Capacitors connected in parallel share the same voltage; their capacitances add. Capacitors in series carry the same magnitude of charge and split the total voltage. Stored energy grows with capacitance at fixed voltage. Two equal 10 μF capacitors have 20 μF in parallel but only 5 μF in series.

94. Electric Current and Current Strength

Electric current is directed transport of electric charge. In a metal, mobile electrons drift opposite the conventional positive-current direction. A current of 2 A means 2 C of charge crosses a chosen conductor cross-section each second. Individual electrons drift slowly, while changes in the electric field propagate through a circuit much faster.

95. Electrical Resistance and Resistivity

Electrical resistance characterises how strongly a conductor opposes current under specified conditions. For a uniform wire, resistance grows with length and decreases with cross-sectional area. Doubling its length doubles resistance; doubling its area halves resistance if material and temperature stay the same.

96. Ohm's Law for a Circuit Section

Ohm's law for a circuit section states that current is proportional to voltage when resistance is constant: I = U/R. A 12 V source applied across an ideal 6 Ω resistor gives 2 A. The law is a material-and-condition-dependent relation, not a universal linear rule for every electrical device.

97. Series and Parallel Connections

In a series circuit the same current passes through each resistor and their voltage drops add. In parallel circuits every branch has the same voltage and branch currents add. Two 6 Ω resistors give 12 Ω in series and 3 Ω in parallel. Household devices are generally connected in parallel so each receives its rated supply voltage.

98. Electromotive Force and Ohm's Law for a Complete Circuit

A real battery has an electromotive force (EMF) and internal resistance. EMF is energy supplied to each unit charge by non-electrostatic processes inside the source. When current flows, internal resistance lowers the terminal voltage. A battery with EMF 12 V and internal resistance 1 Ω supplying 2 A has terminal voltage 10 V.

99. Kirchhoff's Rules

Kirchhoff's rules solve electrical networks whose branches cannot be simplified by simple series and parallel combinations. The junction rule expresses conservation of charge: currents entering a node balance currents leaving it. The loop rule expresses conservation of energy: signed voltage changes around a closed path sum to zero.

100. Work and Power of Electric Current

Electric current transfers electrical energy to components. Electrical power is the rate of energy transfer, and the Joule–Lenz law gives resistive heating. A 100 W device operating for 2 hours uses 0.20 kWh of energy. For a resistor, heating increases with the square of current when resistance is fixed.

101. Ammeters, Voltmeters and Multimeters

An ammeter measures current through a circuit element and must be inserted in series. A voltmeter measures the potential difference between two points and connects in parallel. A multimeter can perform both tasks, but its lead sockets and settings must match the measurement. For example, a 6 V drop across a 3 Ω resistor implies 2 A, which is a separate current measurement rather than a voltage setting.

102. Charging and Discharging a Capacitor

An RC circuit contains a resistor and capacitor. Charging begins rapidly but slows as the capacitor voltage approaches the source voltage; discharge follows the opposite exponential trend. Its time constant τ = RC sets the clock: after one τ, charging reaches about 63% of the final voltage, while discharge retains about 37%. Such behavior appears in timing and flash circuits.

103. Current in Metals and Superconductivity

Electrical current in a metal is mainly the directed drift of conduction electrons within a lattice of positive ions. Their random thermal motion does not produce a net current. For many ordinary metals, heating increases resistance because electrons scatter more strongly. Superconductors, by contrast, can have exactly zero DC electrical resistance below critical conditions, making powerful magnets possible.

104. Current in Electrolytes and Electrolysis

In an electrolyte, dissolved or molten substances conduct electric current through positive and negative ions, not through free electrons as in a metal wire. At electrodes, chemical reactions can deposit metal or release gases. Faraday's law links the deposited mass to the charge passed, enabling electroplating and metal refining.

105. Current in Gases and Vacuum

Ordinary gas is usually a poor conductor because it has few free charged particles. When atoms become ionized, electrons and ions carry current; sustained strong ionization can form plasma. Sparks, glowing lamps, corona and arcs are different discharge regimes. In a vacuum tube, electrons emitted from a heated cathode carry current even without gas ions.

106. Semiconductors and Their Conductivity

A semiconductor has conductivity between that of common metals and good insulators, but its key feature is that conductivity can be controlled strongly by temperature, light and small impurity concentrations. Electric current is carried by electrons and holes. Doping silicon with donors creates n-type material; acceptors create p-type material. Semiconductor sensors and electronic chips exploit this control.

107. The p–n Junction, Diodes and Transistors

A p–n junction is the boundary between p-type and n-type semiconductor regions. It conducts readily under forward bias but limits current under reverse bias, so a diode can rectify alternating current. A light-emitting diode converts electrical energy into light. Transistors use semiconductor junctions or electric fields to control a much larger circuit current and to switch digital circuits.

108. Magnetic Field and Magnetic Induction

A magnetic field exists around magnets and electric currents, exerting forces on moving charges and current-carrying wires. Its strength and direction are described by magnetic induction B, a vector measured in teslas. A compass aligns with the local field, including Earth's field, typically tens of microteslas near the surface. Magnetic field lines show direction but are not physical strings.

109. Magnetic Field of an Electric Current

An electric current produces a magnetic field. Around a long straight conductor, field lines are concentric circles; inside an elongated solenoid the field is nearly uniform away from its ends. Reversing current reverses the magnetic-field direction. This lets electrical circuits operate electromagnets, relays and lifting devices.

110. The Ampère Force and Electric Motors

A current-carrying conductor placed in a magnetic field experiences the Ampère force. The force is greatest when the current is perpendicular to the field and vanishes when they are parallel. In electric motors, forces on opposite sides of a current loop produce a turning moment. Speakers similarly use magnetic force to move their cones.

111. The Lorentz Force and Charged Particles in a Magnetic Field

The magnetic part of the Lorentz force acts on a charged particle only while it moves, and is perpendicular to its velocity. It can bend a path without changing the particle's speed in a static magnetic field. When a charged particle enters a uniform field at right angles, it travels in a circle; this helps explain cyclotrons, mass spectrometers and auroras.

112. Magnetic Properties of Matter

Materials respond differently to a magnetic field. Diamagnets weakly oppose the applied field; paramagnets weakly align with it; ferromagnets such as iron can develop strong magnetization and retain part of it. Ferromagnetic domains explain the behavior of permanent magnets and magnetic recording. Above a Curie temperature, many ferromagnets lose spontaneous ferromagnetic order.

113. Magnetic Flux

Magnetic flux measures how much magnetic field passes through a chosen surface, taking its orientation into account. For a flat surface in a uniform field it equals B times area times the cosine of the angle between B and the surface normal. Flux can change if the field strength, surface area or angle changes; changing flux is the key to electromagnetic induction.

114. Electromagnetic Induction and Faraday's Law

A changing magnetic flux through a conducting loop creates an induced electromotive force (EMF). If the loop is closed, it can drive current. Moving a magnet through a coil or switching current in a nearby coil changes flux and induces an EMF even without a battery connected to the detecting loop. This is the principle behind generators and transformers.

115. Lenz's Rule

Lenz's rule tells which way an induced current flows. Its magnetic field opposes the change in magnetic flux that produced the current. When a north pole approaches a conducting loop, the near side of the loop behaves like a north pole and tends to repel the magnet; when the pole moves away, it tends to attract it. This is a consequence of energy conservation, not opposition to all magnetic fields.

116. Induced EMF in a Moving Conductor

A conducting rod moving across magnetic-field lines can develop a voltage between its ends. Magnetic force separates mobile charges until the resulting electric force balances it. The induced EMF depends on magnetic field strength, rod length and the component of velocity perpendicular to both the field and rod. This is motional EMF and can drive current if the rod completes a circuit with rails.

117. Self-Induction, Inductance and Magnetic Field Energy

A changing current in a coil changes its own magnetic flux and produces a self-induced EMF opposing that current change. This is why current through an inductor cannot jump instantly in an ideal circuit. Inductors store energy in magnetic fields and are used in filters, power converters and electromagnets. Interrupting a large inductive current may produce a dangerous voltage spike.

118. Oscillatory Motion: Amplitude, Period and Frequency

Oscillatory motion repeats around an equilibrium position, as in a swinging pendulum or vibrating spring. The amplitude describes the maximum distance from equilibrium, the period the time for one full cycle, and the frequency the number of cycles per second. A pendulum that makes ten oscillations in 20 s has period 2.0 s and frequency 0.50 Hz.

119. Harmonic Oscillations

Harmonic oscillation is the simplest periodic motion: displacement varies as a sine or cosine of time. The acceleration is proportional to displacement but points in the opposite direction, so the system continually moves back toward equilibrium. A spring with ideal linear restoring force produces this motion; the projection of uniform circular motion onto a diameter has the same mathematical form.

120. Spring and Simple Pendulums

An ideal mass–spring oscillator and a simple pendulum have predictable periods. The spring period depends on mass and spring stiffness, while the pendulum period depends on length and gravitational acceleration, not mass. A 1.0 m pendulum near Earth's surface has a period of about 2.0 s, provided the swing angle is small and damping is weak.

121. Energy in Oscillations and Damping

A pendulum exchanges kinetic energy with potential energy as it swings. At the lowest point its speed is greatest; at its turning points it momentarily stops. Real oscillations fade because friction and air resistance convert mechanical energy to internal energy.

122. Forced Oscillations and Resonance

A periodic external force can keep an oscillator moving despite friction. When the forcing frequency is near the natural frequency, the response can become especially large: this is resonance. Resonance helps tune radios and musical instruments but excessive vibrations can damage machines and bridges.

123. Mechanical Waves

A mechanical wave transports a disturbance and energy through a material medium without carrying the medium as a whole along its route. A wave on a string is transverse because the string moves across the direction of travel. Sound in air is longitudinal because the air compresses and expands along that direction.

124. Wavelength, Frequency and Wave Speed

A periodic wave repeats after one period in time and after one wavelength in space. Its frequency measures cycles per second, while speed measures how quickly its phase propagates. The relation v = λf links all three: if sound travels at 340 m/s with frequency 170 Hz, its wavelength is 2.0 m.

125. Interference, Diffraction and Standing Waves

When waves overlap, their displacements add. Coherent waves can reinforce or cancel each other, producing interference. Waves also bend and spread around obstacles, an effect called diffraction. Two opposing waves of the same frequency and amplitude can form a standing wave with fixed nodes and antinodes.

126. Sound Waves: Pitch, Loudness and Timbre

Sound is a mechanical pressure wave. Its frequency mainly controls pitch, its intensity contributes to loudness, and its waveform and spectrum contribute to timbre. Two instruments can play the same note but sound different because their harmonics and envelopes differ. Sound cannot cross an empty vacuum.

127. The Doppler Effect

The Doppler effect is a change in the observed wave frequency caused by relative motion between source and observer. An approaching ambulance sounds higher-pitched and a receding one lower-pitched. The wave speed in stationary air does not change just because the source moves; the wavelength changes.

128. The LC Circuit and Electromagnetic Oscillations

An ideal LC circuit exchanges energy between the electric field of a capacitor and the magnetic field of an inductor. As capacitor charge falls, current rises; after the capacitor empties, current continues and charges it with opposite polarity. The oscillation is analogous to an ideal frictionless spring.

129. Alternating Current and Generators

Alternating current changes direction periodically. In a generator, a rotating coil experiences a changing magnetic flux and develops an alternating electromotive force. Many electrical grids operate near 50 Hz, meaning the voltage completes fifty cycles each second. An AC source can conveniently feed transformers.

130. Effective Values of Current and Voltage

The effective or root-mean-square value of alternating voltage is the DC voltage that would produce the same average heating in a resistor. For a sinusoidal voltage the RMS value is the peak divided by √2. Thus a nominal 230 V household supply has a peak near 325 V, not 230 V.

131. Resistors, Capacitors and Inductors in AC Circuits

In an AC circuit, resistors dissipate energy while ideal capacitors and inductors store and return it each cycle. A capacitor lets higher frequencies pass more readily; an inductor opposes higher-frequency currents more strongly. Their effects create a phase difference between voltage and current, and together they can produce resonance.

132. Transformers and Power Transmission

A transformer changes alternating voltage using electromagnetic induction between coils linked by a magnetic core. Ideally, the ratio of voltages equals the ratio of turns. Power grids raise voltage for long-distance transmission because the same power at higher voltage needs less current and therefore loses less energy as heat in wires.

133. Electromagnetic Waves

Electromagnetic waves consist of coupled changing electric and magnetic fields. Unlike sound, they travel through empty space. They are transverse, carry energy and momentum, and undergo reflection, refraction, interference and diffraction. Radio signals and visible light are members of the same family.

134. Principles of Radio Communication

Radio communication sends information by altering a high-frequency electromagnetic carrier wave. A transmitter modulates the carrier and sends it through an antenna; a receiver selects and demodulates the signal to recover information. Modern mobile networks encode digital data using more elaborate forms of modulation.

135. The Electromagnetic Spectrum

The electromagnetic spectrum orders waves by frequency or wavelength: radio, microwave, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays. They all travel at the same speed in vacuum but interact differently with matter. Visible wavelengths are roughly 380–750 nm; shorter-wavelength radiation generally carries more energy per photon.

136. Light: Its Nature and Speed

Light is electromagnetic radiation that can be described by waves and by photons. In a vacuum its speed is exactly 299 792 458 m/s. Sunlight needs about eight minutes to reach Earth, showing that even extremely fast light takes time to cross astronomical distances.

137. Rectilinear Propagation of Light, Shadows and Eclipses

In a uniform transparent medium, light travels along approximately straight rays. An opaque body blocks some rays, producing a shadow. An extended light source also produces a partially illuminated penumbra. Solar and lunar eclipses follow from the relative geometry of the Sun, Earth and Moon.

138. Reflection of Light and Plane Mirrors

When light strikes a smooth surface, it reflects with an angle equal to its angle of incidence, measured from the normal. A plane mirror creates an upright virtual image at the same distance behind the mirror as the object stands in front of it. It seems left-right reversed because front and back directions are exchanged relative to the viewer.

139. Spherical Mirrors

Spherical mirrors have reflecting surfaces that form part of a sphere. Concave mirrors can concentrate parallel rays near a focus and form real or virtual images. Convex mirrors spread reflected rays and form upright, reduced virtual images, making them useful for wide fields of view.

140. Refraction of Light and Snell's Law

Refraction is the change of light direction when it passes obliquely between media with different refractive indices. Light generally bends toward the normal when entering a medium with larger refractive index, and away from the normal when entering a smaller-index medium. Its frequency remains unchanged across a stationary boundary.

141. Total Internal Reflection

Total internal reflection occurs when light travels from a larger refractive index to a smaller one and strikes the boundary at an angle greater than the critical angle. No propagating refracted ray then carries energy into the second medium. This guides signals through optical fibres.

142. Lenses and Optical Power

A lens redirects light by refraction. A converging lens brings paraxial parallel rays toward a real focus, while a diverging lens spreads them as if they came from a virtual focus. Optical power describes how strongly a thin lens bends light and is measured in dioptres.

143. Image Formation by Thin Lenses

A thin lens forms an image where refracted rays meet or where their backward extensions appear to meet. Depending on object distance and lens type, the image may be real or virtual, enlarged or reduced, upright or inverted. Three principal rays help locate it without measuring every ray.

144. The Eye and Vision Correction

The eye focuses light on the retina using its cornea and adjustable lens. Accommodation changes the optical power so objects at different distances appear sharp. Myopia makes distant objects blur; hyperopia makes near objects difficult to focus. Corrective lenses change the incoming wavefront before it reaches the eye.

145. Optical Instruments

Optical instruments control how images appear to the eye or to a sensor. A magnifier increases angular size, a microscope reveals tiny nearby structures, and a telescope gathers light from distant objects. A camera projects a real image onto a sensor; a projector creates a real enlarged image on a screen.

146. Dispersion of Light and Color

White light separates into colors in a prism because a transparent material generally refracts different wavelengths by different amounts. This is dispersion. An object's color depends on its illumination and on which wavelengths it reflects or transmits. Red, green and blue screen lights can combine to create many perceived colors.

147. Interference of Light

Light waves interfere when their electric fields overlap. With stable phase difference, some positions become brighter and others darker because wave amplitudes add. Young's double-slit experiment showed alternating fringes, providing key evidence for the wave nature of light. Soap films show related colors.

148. Diffraction of Light and the Diffraction Grating

Diffraction is the spreading of light near edges or through openings, most noticeable when their size approaches a wavelength. A diffraction grating has many evenly spaced lines; their light waves interfere to produce sharp maxima at wavelength-dependent angles. This makes it possible to measure spectra.

149. Polarization of Light

Polarization describes the orientation of light's transverse electric-field oscillations. Ordinary sunlight is largely unpolarized; a polarizing filter transmits one selected field direction. Polarized sunglasses reduce glare from many reflective surfaces, and crossed filters can strongly darken transmitted light.

150. Postulates of Special Relativity

Special relativity describes space and time in inertial frames when speeds approach the speed of light. Einstein's two postulates state that physical laws have the same form in all inertial frames and that light in vacuum has the same speed c for all inertial observers. Events simultaneous in one frame need not be simultaneous in another.

151. Time Dilation and Length Contraction

Moving clocks run more slowly relative to an inertial observer when compared through correctly defined events, and a moving object's length parallel to its motion is smaller than its proper length. These are frame-dependent effects, not mechanical damage to clocks or rods. Fast muons surviving to Earth's surface provide evidence.

152. Relativistic Velocity Addition

Speeds measured in different inertial frames do not simply add at relativistic speeds. Special relativity combines collinear velocities through a fraction that keeps the result below c for material objects. At everyday speeds it agrees almost exactly with ordinary addition.

153. Mass–Energy Equivalence

Mass and energy are equivalent: a body at rest has energy even without motion. The rest energy of one kilogram is enormous because it is multiplied by the square of light speed. Nuclear reactions can release energy because the total rest mass of the products differs from that of the reactants.

154. Thermal Radiation and Planck's Hypothesis

Every object with a temperature above absolute zero emits thermal electromagnetic radiation. An ideal blackbody absorbs all incident radiation and has a temperature-dependent spectrum. Classical physics failed to explain the measured short-wavelength spectrum; Planck's energy quantization solved this problem.

155. Photons

Light is emitted and absorbed in photons, discrete quanta of electromagnetic radiation. A photon has energy proportional to frequency and momentum inversely proportional to wavelength. It has zero rest mass yet carries energy and momentum, so light can exert pressure on a surface.

156. The Photoelectric Effect

In the external photoelectric effect, light can eject electrons from a material if each photon has enough energy to overcome the work function. Increasing light intensity below the threshold frequency does not eject electrons from a clean metal under ordinary single-photon conditions. Above threshold, greater frequency raises the maximum electron kinetic energy.

157. Wave–Particle Duality and de Broglie Waves

Particles such as electrons can produce interference and diffraction, behavior previously associated with waves. De Broglie proposed that every particle with momentum has an associated wavelength. The wavelength becomes tiny for everyday objects but is measurable for electrons, helping electron microscopes resolve very small structures.

158. The Uncertainty Principle

Quantum mechanics imposes a fundamental lower limit on how sharply position and momentum can simultaneously be specified for one state. A sharply localized particle has a broad spread of possible momentum measurements. This is not merely an error caused by a careless observer or an imperfect instrument.

159. Rutherford's Nuclear Model of the Atom

Most of an atom's mass and nearly all its positive charge are concentrated in a tiny nucleus. Rutherford's scattering experiment demonstrated this when some alpha particles passed through thin gold foil almost undeflected but a small fraction scattered through large angles. The atom is mostly empty space on the scale of its nucleus.

160. Bohr's Postulates and Energy Levels

Atoms emit and absorb light at specific frequencies because their electrons occupy discrete energy states. Bohr proposed that an electron in a stationary atomic state does not continuously radiate energy, but a transition between states can emit or absorb a photon. This model predicts hydrogen's simplest spectral lines.

161. Emission and Absorption Spectra

When atoms or molecules exchange energy with light, they produce characteristic spectra. A hot dense source often gives a continuous rainbow; a thin excited gas gives bright lines; cooler gas in front of a bright source leaves dark absorption lines. Hydrogen emits a visible red line near 656 nm. Comparing line positions lets astronomers identify elements in stars, but line brightness also depends on temperature and conditions.

162. Lasers

A laser produces light through stimulated emission inside an optical gain medium. Its beam can have narrow wavelength range, strong directional focus and high coherence. A red laser pointer may operate near 650 nm, but its bright-looking spot does not reveal whether it is eye-safe. Lasers serve in fibre communication, scanners, manufacturing and medicine. Never look into a laser beam or its mirror reflection.

163. Structure of the Atomic Nucleus and Isotopes

An atomic nucleus consists of protons and neutrons, collectively called nucleons. Proton number Z determines the chemical element; mass number A counts all nucleons, so neutron number N = A − Z. Carbon-12 has Z = 6, A = 12 and N = 6, while carbon-14 has N = 8. These are isotopes of the same element. The nucleus occupies a tiny region compared with the atom.

164. Nuclear Forces, Mass Defect and Binding Energy

Nuclear binding energy is the energy needed to separate a nucleus into free protons and neutrons. A bound nucleus has slightly less mass than its separated nucleons: the mass defect Δm corresponds to energy E = Δmc². For example, a mass defect of 0.010 u represents about 9.31 MeV. Binding energy per nucleon helps explain why both light-nucleus fusion and heavy-nucleus fission can release energy.

165. Radioactivity: Alpha, Beta and Gamma Radiation

Radioactive nuclei may transform while emitting alpha particles, beta radiation or gamma photons. An alpha particle is a helium-4 nucleus; beta radiation consists of electrons or positrons; gamma radiation consists of high-energy photons. Their penetrating abilities differ, so protection must match the source. For example, alpha radiation is easily stopped externally but may be harmful if alpha-emitting material enters the body.

166. The Law of Radioactive Decay and Half-Life

Radioactive decay is random for an individual nucleus but predictable for very large collections. The half-life T is the time in which the expected number of undecayed nuclei falls by half. A sample starting with 80 mg of one radionuclide has 20 mg of that radionuclide after two half-lives. This law enables isotope dating and activity estimates; it does not reveal exactly when one particular nucleus will decay.

167. Nuclear Reactions

A nuclear reaction changes one or more nuclei through interaction with particles or other nuclei. The total electric charge and total nucleon number are conserved in ordinary school-level nuclear equations. A reaction may release energy if the products have less total rest mass than the reactants. Conservation alone does not prove that a reaction is likely or even energetically allowed.

168. Nuclear Fission and Nuclear Reactors

Nuclear fission splits a heavy nucleus into lighter fragments and releases energy, typically together with neutrons. Neutrons can induce further fissions, allowing a chain reaction. A power reactor regulates this process while transferring released heat to an electricity-generating system. Nuclear energy produces little direct carbon dioxide during operation, but it requires strict safety, cooling and radioactive-waste management.

169. Nuclear Fusion

Nuclear fusion joins light nuclei into a more tightly bound nucleus and can release energy. Fusion powers the Sun, where extreme temperature and gravitational confinement support reactions over long times. On Earth researchers investigate magnetic confinement in tokamaks and other approaches. The challenge is not just to make fusion occur, but to obtain reliable net useful energy while managing hot plasma and materials.

170. Ionizing Radiation: Dose and Protection

Ionizing radiation transfers enough energy to remove electrons from atoms and can damage living tissue. Absorbed dose describes energy deposited per kilogram and is measured in grays (Gy). Equivalent dose accounts for radiation type and is measured in sieverts (Sv). For example, depositing 0.002 J uniformly in 1 kg gives an absorbed dose of 0.002 Gy. Protection uses reduced exposure time, greater distance and suitable shielding.

171. Elementary Particles and Fundamental Interactions

Matter is built from elementary particles such as quarks and leptons. Quarks form protons and neutrons; the electron is a lepton. The four fundamental interactions are strong, electromagnetic, weak and gravitational. The Standard Model describes the first three through quantum fields and particles, but it does not include a complete quantum theory of gravity. Discovery of the Higgs boson in 2012 supported a central part of this model.
