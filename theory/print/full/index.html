1. What Physics Studies: Phenomena, Models and Laws

Origin

Ideas about nature were discussed in antiquity, but the combination of quantitative experiments and mathematical reasoning became central in the work of Galileo in the early seventeenth century and Newton later that century. Earlier observations remained important, and modern physics still revises models as better instruments reveal new evidence.

Core idea

A phenomenon is an observable event, such as a falling ball or a rainbow. You first identify a question, propose a testable hypothesis, choose measurable quantities, predict an outcome and compare it with observations. A law, such as a relationship between distance and time, summarizes a pattern. A theory supplies a wider explanation and predictions. The terms do not represent an upgrade ladder: a well-supported theory does not turn into a law.

Key terms and formulas

Write measurable predictions with quantities and SI units. For a constant-speed idealization, s = vt, where distance s is in metres (m), speed v in metres per second (m/s) and time t in seconds (s). This simple law applies only when speed remains constant along the measured route. Record measured values and an uncertainty rather than treating all digits as exact.

How to apply it

For a moving object, specify what will be ignored: its size, friction, air drag or changes in speed. Measure distance and time several times. Calculate the predicted value with the proposed model, then compare the difference with measurement uncertainty. If the discrepancy is systematic, revise the assumptions rather than changing the measurements to fit your prediction.

Worked example

A cyclist covers 120 m in 20 s on a straight road. The constant-speed model predicts v = s / t = 120 m / 20 s = 6.0 m/s. At that speed, the next 30 s would cover s = 6.0 m/s · 30 s = 180 m, provided the cyclist does not accelerate. Try it: at 6.0 m/s, how far in 5 s? (Answer: 30 m.)

Limits and common mistakes

Physics models are approximations, not photographs of reality. The point-particle model cannot describe parking clearance or rotation. A single successful measurement does not establish a universal law; repeat trials and seek independent evidence. Avoid treating a hypothesis as a guess with no test or assuming a scientific theory is merely an opinion.

2. Physical Quantities and SI Units

Origin

Metric units emerged from eighteenth-century efforts to standardize measurements. The International System of Units was established in 1960; the mole became a seventh base unit in 1971. Since 2019 the units have been defined using fixed numerical values of fundamental constants rather than a physical kilogram artifact.

Core idea

A physical quantity expresses a measurable property. A base quantity is assigned one of the seven base units; derived units combine them by multiplication and division. Prefixes rescale a unit without changing the quantity. Scientific notation keeps very large and small numbers readable. A unit is not an optional decoration: it tells you which quantity a number represents.

Key terms and formulas

The seven SI base units are metre (m) for length, kilogram (kg) for mass, second (s) for time, ampere (A) for electric current, kelvin (K) for temperature, mole (mol) for amount of substance and candela (cd) for luminous intensity. Derived examples: speed m/s, acceleration m/s², force newton (N = kg·m/s²). Prefixes: nano n = 10⁻⁹, micro µ = 10⁻⁶, milli m = 10⁻³, centi c = 10⁻², kilo k = 10³, mega M = 10⁶ and giga G = 10⁹.

How to apply it

Write the given quantity with its unit; identify the target SI unit; multiply by conversion factors whose units cancel. Convert every value before evaluating a physical law. For a squared unit, square the conversion factor too: 1 cm² = 10⁻⁴ m². Express extreme values in powers of ten and check that the final dimension matches the requested quantity.

Worked example

A bus travels at 72 km/h. Since 1 km = 1000 m and 1 h = 3600 s, v = 72 · 1000 m / 3600 s = 20 m/s. In 15 s at unchanged speed it travels s = vt = 20 m/s · 15 s = 300 m. Try it: convert 54 km/h to m/s. (Answer: 15 m/s.)

Limits and common mistakes

SI is a convention for communicating measurements, not a separate physical law. Never use 72 directly in s = vt when time is in seconds and distance is needed in metres. Distinguish lowercase m for milli from uppercase M for mega, and never mistake kg for a derived unit by attaching another prefix to it directly.

3. Measurement Uncertainty and Significant Figures

Origin

Metrologists developed statistical treatment of measurement errors as astronomy and surveying advanced in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. Gauss and Legendre helped formalize least-squares methods. Modern practice prefers the word uncertainty because a measurement can be uncertain even when no definite error is known.

Core idea

Random variation makes repeated results scatter, whereas systematic effects shift them consistently, as with an incorrectly zeroed scale. Resolution is the smallest division or increment that an instrument displays. Absolute uncertainty expresses a range in the measured unit; relative uncertainty compares that range with the measurement. Repeating trials helps estimate random uncertainty but does not automatically fix calibration bias.

Key terms and formulas

Report x = x̄ ± Δx in the unit of x. The arithmetic mean is x̄ = (x₁ + … + xₙ) / n. Relative uncertainty δ = Δx / |x̄| and percentage uncertainty is 100δ%. For a simple classroom estimate with an analogue scale, half the smallest division may be used if reading conditions permit. The choice is an approximation, not a universal rule. Round uncertainty to one or two significant digits and round x̄ to the same decimal place.

How to apply it

Inspect the instrument zero and scale before measuring. Repeat the observation without changing the measured object, note the spread and check for systematic shifts. Choose an uncertainty justified by resolution and repeatability; state the convention used. Calculate the percentage uncertainty after converting both values to the same unit. Keep extra digits during calculations and round at the end.

Worked example

The measured desk length is 12.3 cm, 12.5 cm and 12.4 cm. The mean is x̄ = (12.3 + 12.5 + 12.4) cm / 3 = 12.4 cm. With an estimated uncertainty of 0.1 cm, report (12.4 ± 0.1) cm and δ = 0.1 / 12.4 · 100% ≈ 0.8%. Try it: what is the percentage uncertainty of (20.0 ± 0.2) cm? (Answer: 1.0%.)

Limits and common mistakes

Uncertainty is not the same as a mistake or the difference from a textbook value. The example estimate of 0.1 cm is not a full statistical confidence interval. Do not average away a faulty zero; recalibrate. Do not report a value as 12.400000 cm when the uncertainty is 0.1 cm, and do not confuse percentage uncertainty with percentage error against a known reference.

4. Dimensional Analysis and Estimation

Origin

The systematic use of dimensions grew in nineteenth-century mechanics and fluid physics. Fourier emphasized dimensional consistency, and later Rayleigh and Buckingham developed methods for finding relationships among physical variables. Fermi popularized order-of-magnitude estimation for problems with incomplete information.

Core idea

Every quantity has a dimension, such as length L, mass M or time T. An equation must have the same dimension on both sides, and terms being added must share a dimension. Ratios of like quantities have dimension one. Dimensional reasoning can suggest how variables combine but cannot find every numerical coefficient or distinguish some physically different mechanisms.

Key terms and formulas

Dimensions: [v] = LT⁻¹, [a] = LT⁻², [F] = MLT⁻² and [g] = LT⁻². For a pendulum, [l/g] = L / (LT⁻²) = T², so √(l/g) has dimension T. The actual small-angle period is Tₚ = 2π√(l/g); the dimensionless 2π cannot be predicted from units alone. For estimates, powers of ten such as 10² or 10⁶ are often sufficient.

How to apply it

First identify the desired output dimension. Replace each variable in a candidate formula with L, M and T; simplify powers. Reject any expression with mismatched sides. If data are incomplete, estimate independent factors, calculate their product and compare it with familiar scales. Check extreme cases: doubling a length must not cause a physically implausible change.

Worked example

Estimate the period of a 1.0 m pendulum for small swings using g = 9.8 m/s². Tₚ = 2π√(1.0 m / 9.8 m/s²) = 2π√(0.102 s²) ≈ 2.01 s. The units inside the square root reduce to s². Try it: how does the period change if length becomes 4.0 m? (Answer: it doubles, to about 4.01 s.)

Limits and common mistakes

Correct dimensions cannot detect a missing coefficient, wrong sign or wrong physical assumption. The pendulum expression above needs small angles and negligible damping. Never add incompatible units or treat a dimensionless angle in radians as a length. An estimate should state its assumptions and sensible precision rather than claiming exactness.

5. Scalars and Vectors

Origin

Geometric methods for directed quantities developed over centuries. In the nineteenth century Hamilton, Grassmann and Gibbs helped establish the vector methods now used in physics. The familiar arrow representation makes directional quantities easier to compare and combine.

Core idea

Vectors are drawn as arrows: length represents magnitude and the arrowhead direction. You can move an arrow parallel to itself without changing the represented free vector. Add vectors head-to-tail or by the parallelogram rule; reverse an arrow for subtraction. Components show how much of a vector lies along chosen perpendicular axes. A negative component indicates direction, not negative magnitude.

Key terms and formulas

For perpendicular components Aₓ and Aᵧ, magnitude A = √(Aₓ² + Aᵧ²). In a plane, Aₓ = A cos θ and Aᵧ = A sin θ if θ is measured from the positive x-axis. For a sum, Rₓ = Aₓ + Bₓ and Rᵧ = Aᵧ + Bᵧ. Magnitude of the sum is R = √(Rₓ² + Rᵧ²). Each component has the same SI unit as its parent vector: metres for displacement or newtons for force.

How to apply it

Draw coordinate axes before drawing arrows. Mark the direction of each vector and decompose angled vectors along axes. Add like components, then calculate the resultant magnitude and direction. Compare the magnitude with the sum of individual magnitudes: a resultant cannot exceed that sum. When you subtract vectors, add the reversed second vector.

Worked example

A walker goes 3.0 m east and 4.0 m north. Resultant components are Rₓ = 3.0 m and Rᵧ = 4.0 m. Magnitude R = √((3.0 m)² + (4.0 m)²) = 5.0 m; direction θ = arctan(4/3) ≈ 53° north of east. Try it: two perpendicular displacements of 5 m and 12 m give what resultant? (Answer: 13 m.)

Limits and common mistakes

The triangle rule applies to vectors of the same physical type. You cannot meaningfully add 3 N to 4 m. Do not confuse total path with displacement magnitude, or the sign of x-component with the overall direction. For nonperpendicular vectors, using Pythagoras without resolving components gives a wrong result.

6. Graphs in Physics

Origin

Plots became indispensable as scientists began recording repeatable quantitative experiments. Descartes developed analytic geometry in the seventeenth century, linking algebra with coordinates. Later experimental physics used fitted curves to extract physical laws despite measurement scatter.

Core idea

An independent variable is usually on the horizontal axis, and the measured dependent variable on the vertical. The slope tells you how much the vertical quantity changes per unit change of the horizontal quantity. Area under a curve has the product of the two axis units, but its physical meaning depends on the quantities being plotted. A proportional relation has a straight graph through the origin; a general linear relation may not.

Key terms and formulas

For points (x₁, y₁) and (x₂, y₂) on a straight line, slope k = (y₂ − y₁) / (x₂ − x₁), and y = kx + b. On an x–t plot k has unit m/s; on a v–t plot k has unit m/s²; area beneath v(t) has unit (m/s)·s = m and gives signed displacement. A curve y = ax² becomes linear when plotted against x².

How to apply it

Identify axes, units, scale and whether the relation is linear before calculating anything. For a straight graph take well-separated points on the best-fit line, not necessarily two noisy data points. Calculate slope with units. To find area, divide the region into rectangles or triangles. If a curve is nonlinear, consider a transformation predicted by the physical model.

Worked example

A position-time best-fit line passes through (2 s, 7 m) and (8 s, 25 m). Its slope is k = (25 − 7) m / (8 − 2) s = 18 m / 6 s = 3.0 m/s. Extrapolating the line to t = 0 s gives b = 1 m. Try it: what signed displacement corresponds to 4 m/s maintained for 5 s on a v–t graph? (Answer: 20 m.)

Limits and common mistakes

Slope and area are not interchangeable, and neither has a fixed physical meaning without axis labels. A line on an acceleration-time graph means something different from a line on a position-time graph. Extrapolation beyond measured data is a prediction, not a measurement. Avoid joining noisy points into a zigzag when a best-fit trend is appropriate.

7. Mechanical Motion and Frames of Reference

Origin

Ideas about relative motion appear in early astronomy and mechanics. Galileo described how motion within a smoothly moving ship is observed in the seventeenth century. Later Newton formulated laws in inertial frames, establishing the need to specify the frame when describing motion.

Core idea

Motion is not a property stated without comparison: a coordinate changes relative to a reference body. A coordinate system gives position; a clock assigns time to each event. Translational motion means every line rigidly attached to an object keeps its direction as it moves, although individual parts may follow different paths. A material point preserves mass while neglecting body size and shape when those dimensions do not affect the task.

Key terms and formulas

In one dimension, position is x(t) in metres (m) at time t in seconds (s), measured from a chosen origin. A change in position is Δx = x₂ − x₁, also in metres. Two frames may assign different coordinates and velocities to the same object. There is no universal numerical coordinate until the origin, direction and time reference are specified.

How to apply it

Choose what counts as stationary for this problem, such as road, vehicle or laboratory bench. Set an origin and positive axis; select the time origin. Mark positions at known instants and decide whether treating the body as a point is justified. State relative to which frame any conclusion about rest or motion is made.

Worked example

A seated passenger is 4.0 m from the back of a carriage throughout a 10 s interval. Relative to the carriage, Δx = 4.0 m − 4.0 m = 0 m. If the train travels steadily at 15 m/s relative to the ground, its ground displacement is Δx = 15 m/s · 10 s = 150 m. Try it: how far does the train move in 4 s? (Answer: 60 m.)

Limits and common mistakes

Do not claim that a body is absolutely at rest or absolutely moving. Earth-based frames are often adequate for short classroom experiments but not perfectly inertial for precision work. Treating a long vehicle as a point fails when its dimensions or rotation matter. Avoid changing the reference body halfway through a calculation.

8. Trajectory, Path Length and Displacement

Origin

The mathematical description of paths grew with geometry and seventeenth-century mechanics. Galileo investigated trajectories of falling and thrown bodies. Modern kinematics distinguishes distance travelled from the vector connecting starting and ending positions.

Core idea

The trajectory tells you which positions a body visits; it may be a line, circle or curve. Path length accumulates motion along every portion of the trajectory. Displacement ignores detours and connects only start and finish, so its magnitude is never larger than the path. Along a single axis a displacement may have a positive or negative projection, even though its magnitude is nonnegative.

Key terms and formulas

For an interval, Δx = x₂ − x₁ in metres (m). In a plane, Δrₓ = x₂ − x₁ and Δrᵧ = y₂ − y₁, with displacement magnitude |Δr| = √(Δrₓ² + Δrᵧ²), in metres. Path length s ≥ |Δr|, with equality for motion along a straight segment without reversing. For several segments, total path is s = s₁ + s₂ + … .

How to apply it

Mark the starting point, endpoint and actual route. Add the lengths of all route sections to obtain path. To obtain displacement, subtract initial coordinates from final coordinates and, for a plane, use the Pythagorean theorem on components. Include a direction or signed projection when describing displacement. Check whether the body returns to its starting position.

Worked example

A runner travels 30 m east and then 40 m north. The path is s = 30 m + 40 m = 70 m. Displacement components are 30 m east and 40 m north, so |Δr| = √((30 m)² + (40 m)²) = 50 m. Try it: a person walks 10 m east and 10 m west; what are the path and displacement? (Answer: 20 m and 0 m.)

Limits and common mistakes

Displacement magnitude equals path only for straight movement in one direction. For a curved route the path is usually longer. Do not confuse the signed x-projection with the vector magnitude, or sum vector displacement magnitudes in place of route lengths. The numerical trajectory itself changes with the reference frame.

9. Average and Instantaneous Velocity

Origin

Galileo developed quantitative descriptions of speeds in the early seventeenth century. The idea of instantaneous velocity became precise with calculus, developed by Newton and Leibniz later that century. Modern measurement estimates instantaneous velocity from very short time intervals.

Core idea

Average speed answers how quickly a route was covered. Average velocity answers how quickly position changed and in which direction. Instantaneous velocity is the limit of average velocity over a shrinking time interval; its magnitude is instantaneous speed, the number shown on a vehicle speedometer. A curved path can have changing velocity even when instantaneous speed is constant.

Key terms and formulas

Average speed v̄ₛ = s_total / Δt in m/s. Average velocity along x is v̄ₓ = Δx / Δt in m/s; in two dimensions use the displacement vector divided by time. Instantaneous vₓ = dx/dt and speed is the magnitude |v|. For equal distances d travelled at speeds v₁ and v₂, the average speed is 2v₁v₂ / (v₁ + v₂), not generally (v₁ + v₂) / 2.

How to apply it

Draw the route and label initial and final positions. Sum the total distance and elapsed times, including stops when they count in the trip duration. For average velocity, calculate signed displacement instead. For equal-distance legs calculate each travel time d/v before taking an average. Use the tangent direction to describe instantaneous velocity on a curved route.

Worked example

A cyclist rides 60 m at 3.0 m/s and then another 60 m at 6.0 m/s, always forward. Times are t₁ = 60 m / 3.0 m/s = 20 s and t₂ = 60 m / 6.0 m/s = 10 s. Average speed is 120 m / 30 s = 4.0 m/s, not 4.5 m/s. Try it: a 400 m track lap in 80 s gives what average velocity? (Answer: zero.)

Limits and common mistakes

Average speed is nonnegative, whereas a velocity component can be negative. Never compute average speed by averaging two speed numbers unless time spent at each speed is equal. A speedometer gives the speed at that moment, not average velocity. Do not substitute path for displacement in velocity calculations.

10. Uniform Straight-Line Motion

Origin

Galileo analyzed uniform motion as part of his seventeenth-century studies of mechanics. The Cartesian coordinate system made it possible to represent the same motion by an equation and a graph. Newton later used uniform motion in his statement of inertia.

Core idea

Equal displacement in equal time intervals is the defining feature of uniform rectilinear motion. The direction matters: negative velocity means movement opposite to the chosen positive axis. An object’s coordinate may increase, decrease or remain fixed, depending on its velocity. Two objects meet when their coordinates in the same frame become equal at the same time.

Key terms and formulas

For constant velocity projection vₓ, x = x₀ + vₓt where x and x₀ are metres, vₓ is m/s and t is seconds measured from the chosen starting instant. Displacement Δx = vₓt. The x–t slope is vₓ; the vₓ–t graph is a horizontal line at vₓ. For two bodies, solve x₁₀ + v₁t = x₂₀ + v₂t using the same time origin.

How to apply it

Select one axis and a single clock for all moving bodies. Record initial positions and signed velocities, then write a separate coordinate equation for each. To find a meeting, set the equations equal and solve for t. Check that t is nonnegative and the coordinates agree. Use a graph to visualize which body catches the other.

Worked example

At t = 0 s, car A is at x = 0 m and moves at 12 m/s; car B is at x = 100 m and moves in the same direction at 7 m/s. Their positions are x_A = 12t and x_B = 100 + 7t in metres. Meeting: 12t = 100 + 7t, so t = 20 s and x = 240 m. Try it: where is A after 5 s? (Answer: 60 m.)

Limits and common mistakes

This model needs a straight path and constant velocity, not merely a constant speed during turns. The position-time graph slope can be negative. Do not confuse the initial coordinate x₀ with distance travelled, and avoid mixing km/h with metres and seconds. Two moving objects can have equal coordinates only at the same chosen instant.

11. Acceleration

Origin

Galileo established a mathematical description of accelerated motion on inclined planes in the seventeenth century. Newton later related acceleration to force. Calculus provides the precise definition of acceleration at a single instant.

Core idea

Velocity is a vector, so acceleration describes changes of magnitude, direction or both. A positive acceleration component means the velocity component is increasing, not necessarily that speed is increasing. When velocity and acceleration point in the same direction, speed increases; when opposite, it decreases. Circular motion can have acceleration even at constant speed because the direction changes.

Key terms and formulas

Average acceleration ā = Δv / Δt, with unit m/s², where Δv is change of velocity in m/s and Δt is seconds. Along x, āₓ = (v₂ₓ − v₁ₓ) / Δt. Instantaneous aₓ = dvₓ/dt. Under constant acceleration, vₓ = v₀ₓ + aₓt. A typical near-surface free-fall acceleration has magnitude g ≈ 9.8 m/s² downward.

How to apply it

Choose a positive direction and write velocity projections with signs. Subtract initial velocity from final velocity, then divide by elapsed time in seconds. Compare the signs of a and v to decide whether the body is speeding up. On a v–t graph, acceleration is the slope. If a changing direction matters, work with vector components rather than speed alone.

Worked example

A car accelerates from 4.0 m/s to 16.0 m/s in 3.0 s in a straight line. ā = (16.0 − 4.0) m/s / 3.0 s = 4.0 m/s² forward. If this acceleration remains constant for another 2.0 s, v = 16.0 + 4.0·2.0 = 24.0 m/s. Try it: a car slows from 10 m/s to 4 m/s in 3 s. What is acceleration along its forward axis? (Answer: −2 m/s².)

Limits and common mistakes

Average acceleration equals instantaneous acceleration only when acceleration is constant. A negative acceleration component does not automatically mean slowing down; compare its direction with velocity. Do not divide a change in speed by time when velocity direction also changes, and do not label m/s² as m/s.

12. Uniformly Accelerated Straight-Line Motion

Origin

Galileo described uniformly accelerated motion using experiments and geometric reasoning in the seventeenth century. His work established that distance from rest grows as the square of time under constant acceleration, a principle later incorporated into Newtonian mechanics.

Core idea

Acceleration is a constant change of velocity per second. The velocity-time graph is a straight line; its area gives displacement. The position-time graph is a parabola. Starting from rest, displacements in successive equal time intervals are in the ratio 1 : 3 : 5 : 7. Braking is the same model with acceleration opposite to velocity, provided the deceleration remains nearly constant.

Key terms and formulas

For one-dimensional constant a: v = v₀ + at, x = x₀ + v₀t + at² / 2 and v² − v₀² = 2a(x − x₀). Mean velocity during the interval is (v₀ + v)/2, so Δx = (v₀ + v)t/2. Positions x are in m, velocities in m/s, acceleration in m/s² and time in s. Stopping distance at deceleration magnitude b is d = v₀²/(2b), when final speed is zero.

How to apply it

Choose the axis and write the signs of v₀ and a. Pick an equation containing the unknown and the supplied data, avoiding time if it is not given. If the question is about stopping, set v = 0 and check the resulting displacement is in the initial travel direction. Verify with the triangular or trapezoidal area beneath v(t).

Worked example

A car starts from rest and accelerates steadily at 2.0 m/s² for 5.0 s. v = 0 + 2.0 m/s² · 5.0 s = 10 m/s. Displacement is Δx = 0 + (2.0 m/s²)(5.0 s)²/2 = 25 m. Try it: from rest with the same acceleration, how far in 3.0 s? (Answer: 9.0 m.)

Limits and common mistakes

The equations need constant acceleration and a straight path, not arbitrary car motion. The squared-speed equation uses displacement with sign, not always total path. When a moving car brakes, acceleration is negative only if the positive axis points forward. Do not apply the 1 : 3 : 5 rule to bodies with nonzero initial velocity.

13. Motion Graphs

Origin

Graph-based kinematics grew from seventeenth-century mathematical descriptions of motion. Galileo used geometric area arguments for uniformly accelerated motion, and calculus later formalized slopes as derivatives and accumulated areas as integrals.

Core idea

An x–t graph shows where the body is; its tangent slope gives instantaneous velocity. A v–t graph shows directional motion; its slope tells how acceleration changes speed or direction along the axis. An a–t graph records acceleration; the signed area beneath it equals velocity change. Negative velocity produces negative signed area on v–t, even though the path length for that interval is positive.

Key terms and formulas

vₓ = dx/dt in m/s, aₓ = dvₓ/dt in m/s². Displacement Δx = area under vₓ(t) with unit m, and velocity change Δvₓ = area under aₓ(t) with unit m/s. For a straight v–t segment, acceleration a = (v₂ − v₁)/(t₂ − t₁). For a uniform acceleration segment, Δx = (v₁ + v₂)Δt/2.

How to apply it

Read axis titles and scale. Identify intervals with positive, zero or negative slopes. Differentiate the x–t description conceptually to sketch v–t, and differentiate v–t to sketch a–t. To reconstruct position, accumulate signed areas and add the known starting coordinate. For total path, add absolute areas for intervals that have opposite velocity signs.

Worked example

A body accelerates uniformly from 0 m/s to 8.0 m/s in 4.0 s. The v–t slope is a = (8.0 − 0) m/s / 4.0 s = 2.0 m/s². Displacement is the triangle area Δx = (4.0 s)(8.0 m/s)/2 = 16 m. Try it: what displacement does a constant velocity −3.0 m/s give in 2.0 s? (Answer: −6.0 m.)

Limits and common mistakes

Area below the time axis is negative displacement, not negative distance. A horizontal x–t graph means rest; a horizontal v–t graph means zero acceleration, not zero velocity. Sharp corners may indicate discontinuous idealized velocity or acceleration and need interpretation; real motions often change smoothly.

14. Relative Motion and Velocity Addition

Origin

Galileo developed the principle of relativity for uniform motion in the seventeenth century, using examples of experiments aboard a moving ship. Newtonian mechanics applies the corresponding vector addition of velocities. Einstein later showed that a different law is required near light speed.

Core idea

A frame of reference changes the way a motion is described. The observed velocity equals the object’s velocity relative to the moving frame plus the frame velocity relative to the observer. When motions are perpendicular, the resulting speed follows from vector components rather than simple addition of magnitudes. A boat aimed straight across a river travels sideways with the current relative to the shore.

Key terms and formulas

Galilean addition: v_object,ground = v_object,frame + v_frame,ground, using vectors and m/s. Along x, v_og,x = v_of,x + v_fg,x; along y the components add separately. Relative velocity of A with respect to B is v_A/B = v_A/ground − v_B/ground. The magnitudes add directly only for parallel vectors in the same direction.

How to apply it

Name all three bodies or frames in each velocity notation. Choose horizontal and vertical axes and draw the vectors head-to-tail. Add each component, then calculate magnitude if requested. When finding a crossing time, use only the velocity component across the river. Use the along-river component to determine downstream displacement.

Worked example

A boat moves at 3.0 m/s perpendicular to the bank relative to water, while the river flows at 4.0 m/s downstream relative to the ground. Shore velocity has components 3.0 and 4.0 m/s, with magnitude √(3.0² + 4.0²) = 5.0 m/s. For a 60 m wide river, crossing takes 60 m / 3.0 m/s = 20 s and drift is 4.0 m/s · 20 s = 80 m. Try it: if current is 0 m/s, what is drift? (Answer: 0 m.)

Limits and common mistakes

Galilean addition is an excellent approximation at speeds far below 3.0 · 10⁸ m/s, but not near light speed. Do not add perpendicular speed magnitudes. State whether boat speed is measured relative to water or shore, and do not use the resultant speed when computing the time needed to cross a fixed width.

15. Free Fall and Vertical Throw

Origin

Galileo investigated falling objects and accelerated motion in the early seventeenth century. Newton later explained near-Earth gravitational acceleration through universal gravitation. In evacuated-tube demonstrations a feather and a heavier object fall together because air resistance is removed.

Core idea

Free fall means gravity is the only significant force. Choose upward as positive: acceleration is −g throughout the flight, even at the highest point where velocity is momentarily zero. When an object is launched upward, gravity reduces its upward velocity until it reaches zero, then makes the downward speed increase. Mass does not enter the ideal equations.

Key terms and formulas

Near Earth, g ≈ 9.8 m/s² downward. With upward-positive axis, v = v₀ − gt and y = y₀ + v₀t − gt²/2. Also v² = v₀² − 2g(y − y₀). For upward launch from level y₀, time to maximum height t_top = v₀/g and height gain h = v₀²/(2g). For a return to the starting height, total time = 2v₀/g.

How to apply it

Mark a vertical axis and origin. Record initial height and signed initial velocity; use a = −g. If the highest point is involved, set v = 0 only at that instant. For a return to the launch height solve y = y₀ and discard t = 0 when seeking the later landing. Check whether air drag or a different gravitational field is relevant.

Worked example

A ball is thrown upward at 19.6 m/s from ground level, with g = 9.8 m/s². Time to the top is t = 19.6 m/s / 9.8 m/s² = 2.0 s. Height gain h = (19.6 m/s)²/(2·9.8 m/s²) = 19.6 m. The ideal return time is 4.0 s. Try it: what is its velocity after 1.0 s? (Answer: 9.8 m/s upward.)

Limits and common mistakes

Real feathers fall differently in air because drag matters, not because gravitational acceleration depends strongly on mass. g changes slightly with latitude and altitude. Do not set acceleration to zero at the top; only velocity is zero there. The symmetry of upward and downward times fails if landing height differs or air resistance is significant.

Related theories

Acceleration (#11); Uniformly Accelerated Straight-Line Motion (#12); Horizontal Launch (#16)

16. Horizontal Launch

Origin

Galileo established that horizontal and vertical aspects of projectile motion can be analyzed independently in the seventeenth century. Combining uniform horizontal motion with uniform vertical acceleration explains the parabolic trajectories observed in experiments.

Core idea

At launch, the horizontal velocity component is v₀ and vertical velocity is zero. Gravity acts downward, so the horizontal component is unchanged while the vertical component grows in magnitude. The two motions happen during the same time interval. The landing velocity is the vector sum of its horizontal and vertical components and usually points diagonally downward.

Key terms and formulas

With horizontal x and downward-positive vertical y starting at launch, x = v₀t, y = gt²/2, vₓ = v₀ and vᵧ = gt. From height h, fall time t = √(2h/g); horizontal range L = v₀√(2h/g). Impact speed v = √(v₀² + (gt)²), with angle below horizontal given by tan θ = gt/v₀. Lengths are m, speeds m/s and g = 9.8 m/s².

How to apply it

Sketch the platform, start and landing level. Choose x horizontally and y downward from the launch point. Solve vertical motion first for the common flight time using known height. Substitute time into horizontal equation to obtain range. If asked about impact speed, calculate both components and combine them using Pythagoras.

Worked example

A ball leaves a 19.6 m high platform horizontally at 5.0 m/s. The fall time t = √(2·19.6 m / 9.8 m/s²) = √(4.0 s²) = 2.0 s. Range L = 5.0 m/s · 2.0 s = 10 m. The downward impact component is 9.8 m/s² · 2.0 s = 19.6 m/s. Try it: if horizontal speed doubles, what is the range? (Answer: 20 m.)

Limits and common mistakes

The equations require a nearly uniform gravitational field and negligible air drag. Do not use the total launch speed for vertical motion, because the vertical component initially equals zero. The horizontal speed does not determine fall time when launch height is fixed. The ideal path is parabolic, but wind and drag alter real trajectories.

Related theories

Uniformly Accelerated Straight-Line Motion (#12); Free Fall and Vertical Throw (#15); Projectile Launched at an Angle (#17)

17. Projectile Launched at an Angle

Origin

Galileo described parabolic projectile paths in the seventeenth century. Later mechanics expressed them through independent horizontal and vertical components of motion. The resulting ideal equations have been used for ballistics and sports calculations, while real trajectories require drag corrections.

Core idea

Horizontal speed is unchanged when air resistance is ignored; vertical speed begins upward, falls to zero at the peak and becomes downward. Both components share the same time. For equal start and finish heights, the rise and fall are symmetric. Complementary launch angles such as 30° and 60° produce the same ideal range at equal starting speed, but different maximum heights.

Key terms and formulas

For initial speed v₀ and angle θ, v₀ₓ = v₀ cos θ and v₀ᵧ = v₀ sin θ. x = v₀ cos θ·t and y = y₀ + v₀ sin θ·t − gt²/2. Time to peak t_top = v₀ sin θ/g and maximum rise H = v₀² sin² θ/(2g). For equal-level landing, T = 2v₀ sin θ/g and range R = v₀² sin(2θ)/g. Use SI units and g = 9.8 m/s².

How to apply it

Draw the start, peak and landing level. Split the initial velocity into horizontal and vertical components. Use vᵧ = 0 at the highest point, or y = landing height to find flight time. Substitute that time into x(t) to find range. Apply the equal-height shortcuts only after checking launch and landing levels; confirm that the computed range is positive.

Worked example

A ball is launched at 20.0 m/s and 30° and lands at the same height. Initial vertical speed is 20.0 sin 30° = 10.0 m/s. Time T = 2·10.0 m/s / 9.8 m/s² ≈ 2.04 s. Horizontal speed is 20.0 cos 30° ≈ 17.3 m/s; range R ≈ 17.3 m/s · 2.04 s = 35.3 m. Try it: what launch angle other than 30° gives the same ideal range? (Answer: 60°.)

Limits and common mistakes

The 45° maximum and complementary-angle rule require equal launch and landing elevations and negligible drag. If landing is higher or lower, solve the vertical quadratic equation instead of using T = 2v₀ sin θ/g. Do not confuse launch speed with the vertical component or claim vertical acceleration becomes zero at the peak.

Related theories

Free Fall and Vertical Throw (#15); Horizontal Launch (#16); Uniform Circular Motion: Period, Frequency and Angular Velocity (#18)

18. Uniform Circular Motion: Period, Frequency and Angular Velocity

Origin

Circular motion was studied in ancient astronomy and became quantitative with early modern mechanics. Huygens and Newton analyzed circular motion in the seventeenth century. The radian offers a natural way to relate rotation angle, arc length and angular speed.

Core idea

One revolution corresponds to 2π radians. Period is the time for a full revolution; frequency counts revolutions per second. Angular velocity describes change of angle per second. Linear speed is the distance travelled along the circular path per unit time. On a rigid rotating disk all points complete a turn together, so frequency and angular speed are equal at every radius, while linear speed grows with radius.

Key terms and formulas

Frequency f = 1/T, in hertz (Hz = s⁻¹); angular speed ω = 2π/T = 2πf, in radians per second (rad/s). Arc length s = Rθ for θ in radians, and linear speed v = ωR = 2πR/T, in m/s. Radius R is in m, period T in s, and angular displacement θ in rad. A full turn is 2π rad = 360°.

How to apply it

Identify the radius and how many turns are made in a measured time. Divide turns by elapsed time to obtain frequency or divide time by turns to obtain period. Convert frequency to angular speed using 2π; then multiply by radius to find linear speed at the selected point. Keep angular and linear quantities distinct.

Worked example

A disk rotates at f = 2.0 Hz. Its period T = 1/2.0 Hz = 0.50 s, and angular speed ω = 2π·2.0 s⁻¹ = 4π rad/s ≈ 12.6 rad/s. At R = 0.20 m, linear speed v = 12.6 rad/s · 0.20 m ≈ 2.51 m/s. Try it: what is the speed at R = 0.40 m? (Answer: about 5.03 m/s.)

Limits and common mistakes

Uniform circular motion has constant speed but not constant velocity. Do not confuse frequency with angular speed: they differ by 2π. Radians are dimensionless mathematically, but the label rad clarifies angle. Points on a rotating disk share ω, not v. The model excludes changes of rotation rate and noncircular trajectories.

Related theories

Centripetal Acceleration (#19); Dynamics of Circular Motion (#32); Oscillatory Motion: Amplitude, Period and Frequency (#118)

19. Centripetal Acceleration

Origin

Huygens calculated the acceleration associated with uniform circular motion in the seventeenth century. Newtonian dynamics later explained that a net inward force must produce this acceleration. The idea remains central to vehicle turns, rotating devices and satellite motion.

Core idea

A velocity vector is tangent to the circular path. A moment later, it points in a new tangent direction. The difference between these two velocity vectors points toward the centre in the limit of a short interval. Therefore a body can accelerate without changing its speed. The centripetal acceleration is not a separate kind of force; forces such as friction or tension can cause it.

Key terms and formulas

Centripetal acceleration magnitude a_c = v²/R = ω²R = 4π²R/T², in m/s², where v is tangential speed in m/s, R circle radius in m, ω angular speed in rad/s and T period in s. Direction is always toward the centre. For uniform circular motion the tangential acceleration is zero while the centripetal component remains nonzero.

How to apply it

Draw the circular path, tangent velocity and radius to its centre. Identify the speed and radius in SI units. Use v²/R when speed is known or ω²R when angular speed is given. Mark inward acceleration on the sketch. Check that increasing radius at fixed speed decreases acceleration and that stopping makes it zero.

Worked example

A car rounds a level circular turn of radius R = 50 m at v = 10 m/s. Its centripetal acceleration is a_c = (10 m/s)² / 50 m = 100 m²/s² / 50 m = 2.0 m/s² toward the turn centre. Try it: what acceleration at 20 m/s on the same curve? (Answer: 8.0 m/s².)

Limits and common mistakes

The stated formulas give the normal component of acceleration on a circle. If speed changes, an additional tangential component exists. Do not draw acceleration tangentially when speed is constant, and do not invent an outward centrifugal force in an inertial frame. Centripetal acceleration is not a centrifugal push experienced independently of reference frame.

Related theories

Uniform Circular Motion: Period, Frequency and Angular Velocity (#18); Newton's Second Law (#23); Dynamics of Circular Motion (#32)

20. Newton's First Law and Inertial Frames

Origin

Galileo used inclined-plane reasoning in the early seventeenth century to challenge the idea that continuous motion needs continuous pushing. Newton formulated the first law in his 1687 Principia, providing a basis for defining inertial frames and analyzing the effects of forces.

Core idea

Inertia is a body’s tendency to resist changes in velocity. With zero net external force in an inertial frame, acceleration is zero: the body can stay at rest or move uniformly along a straight line. Friction often stops objects on Earth, creating the misleading impression that movement itself requires force. Inertial frames are those in which the first law holds for force-free bodies.

Key terms and formulas

In an inertial frame, if ΣF = 0 N then a = 0 m/s² and v is constant in m/s. Here ΣF is the vector sum of all external forces; cancellation requires zero in every coordinate direction. A uniform straight motion has x = x₀ + vt. Earth-bound laboratory frames are approximately inertial for many short, ordinary experiments, but Earth rotates and is not perfectly inertial.

How to apply it

Choose a body and draw external forces such as gravity, normal reaction, friction and tension. Add their vector projections. If the sums equal zero in each direction, conclude that velocity does not change relative to an inertial frame. This does not determine whether the velocity is zero without initial conditions. For a braking vehicle, analyze the passenger and vehicle as separate objects.

Worked example

A puck slides at 3.0 m/s across a nearly frictionless horizontal air table. Gravity and the normal force cancel vertically, and horizontal net force is approximately 0 N. Thus aₓ ≈ 0 m/s², and after 4.0 s its displacement is Δx = 3.0 m/s · 4.0 s = 12 m. Try it: how far in 6.0 s under the same conditions? (Answer: 18 m.)

Limits and common mistakes

The first law is stated for inertial frames; an accelerating bus is not one. Earth is only approximately inertial over limited times and distances. Zero net force does not imply zero velocity, and balanced forces act on the same body, unlike the action-reaction pair in Newton’s third law. Seat belts restrain inertia-driven motion, not a mysterious forward force.

Related theories

Force and Net Force (#22); Newton's Second Law (#23); Free-Body Diagrams and Solving Dynamics Problems (#25)

21. Mass and Density

Origin

Newton distinguished mass from force in his seventeenth-century mechanics. Density has been compared using balances and displaced water since ancient times, including the tradition associated with Archimedes. Modern density tables depend on temperature and sometimes pressure.

Core idea

Mass expresses resistance to acceleration in Newtonian mechanics and is measured in kilograms. Density describes how much mass occupies a unit volume. For a uniform substance, doubling volume doubles mass, so density stays unchanged. An object containing empty space may have an average density smaller than that of the solid material forming its walls.

Key terms and formulas

ρ = m / V, m = ρV and V = m / ρ; ρ is in kg/m³, mass m in kg, volume V in m³. The conversion is 1 g/cm³ = 1000 kg/m³. These equations describe mean density; for a nonuniform object, local density may vary with position. Water has density near 1000 kg/m³ around ordinary temperatures.

How to apply it

Measure mass with a balance. For a regular solid, compute the geometrical volume; for an irregular water-resistant solid, measure displaced water. Convert volume to m³ and divide mass by volume. For a hollow object distinguish exterior volume from material volume and state which density you calculate.

Worked example

A metal block has mass 2.7 kg and volume 0.0010 m³. Its density is ρ = 2.7 kg / 0.0010 m³ = 2700 kg/m³. A hollow object with the same exterior volume but half the mass has mean density 1350 kg/m³. Try it: what mass occupies 0.0020 m³ at 2700 kg/m³? (Answer: 5.4 kg.)

Limits and common mistakes

Density changes with temperature and phase; gases are strongly compressible. Do not confuse mass in kg with weight in newtons, or cm³ with m³. For hollow bodies, the exterior volume gives mean overall density, not the density of the wall material.

Related theories

Force and Net Force (#22); Newton's Second Law (#23); Archimedes' Principle and Flotation (#54)

22. Force and Net Force

Origin

Galileo investigated motion under forces; Newton systematically expressed force and dynamics in 1687. Spring scales became practical instruments through the study of elastic deformation and calibration.

Core idea

A force has magnitude, direction and a point of application. Gravity, support reactions, friction and tension are different interactions. The resultant or net force is the vector sum of external forces on one selected body. Two equally large opposing forces on that body can cancel, but forces acting on different bodies cannot be added in one free-body diagram.

Key terms and formulas

ΣF = F₁ + F₂ + … represents vector addition; each force has SI unit newton (N = kg·m/s²). Along x, ΣFₓ = ΣFᵢₓ. For perpendicular components, |ΣF| = √(ΣFₓ² + ΣFᵧ²). A spring dynamometer indicates force through calibrated elastic extension within its operating range.

How to apply it

Isolate the body and draw arrows for each external force, naming its source. Choose right and upward as positive, project vectors on axes, add signed projections, then find the resultant direction and magnitude. Check whether balanced forces act on the same object.

Worked example

Two people pull a crate horizontally: 12 N east and 5 N west. Net force is 12 N − 5 N = 7 N east. If an additional 24 N northward acts, the resultant magnitude is √((7 N)² + (24 N)²) = 25 N. Try it: 9 N right and 9 N left give what net force? (Answer: 0 N.)

Limits and common mistakes

A resultant alone does not reveal which specific interaction caused it. Be careful not to add magnitudes when directions differ or treat a Newton-third-law pair as balanced forces on one body. The point of application matters for rotation even if the resultant force is zero.

Related theories

Newton's Second Law (#23); Newton's Third Law (#24); Free-Body Diagrams and Solving Dynamics Problems (#25)

23. Newton's Second Law

Origin

Newton formulated the mathematical laws of motion in his 1687 Principia, building on Galileo’s studies. The modern constant-mass expression ΣF = ma is a convenient form of the momentum law.

Core idea

Acceleration is the change of the velocity vector per unit time. Its direction follows the vector sum of external forces. A body can move to the right yet accelerate left when braking. Normal force and gravity may cancel vertically, while horizontal friction or a motor force causes horizontal acceleration.

Key terms and formulas

ΣF = ma, a = ΣF / m and m = |ΣF| / |a| when nonzero and parallel; ΣFₓ = maₓ and ΣFᵧ = maᵧ. Force is in N, mass in kg and acceleration in m/s²; 1 N = 1 kg·m/s². This simplified form assumes constant mass and an inertial frame.

How to apply it

Choose the body and an inertial reference frame, draw every external force, and project ΣF = ma onto axes. Identify known acceleration directions, solve simultaneous equations and inspect signs. Check whether halving mass at fixed resultant doubles acceleration.

Worked example

A cart of mass 3.0 kg experiences a 15 N pull right and 3.0 N resistance left. Net force is 15 N − 3.0 N = 12 N; a = 12 N / 3.0 kg = 4.0 m/s² right. Starting from rest it reaches v = 4.0 m/s² · 2.0 s = 8.0 m/s. Try it: same net force on a 6.0 kg cart? (Answer: 2.0 m/s².)

Limits and common mistakes

The equation is for an inertial frame and constant mass; variable-mass systems need the momentum formulation. Do not replace net force with one convenient force, confuse velocity with acceleration, or infer that a body must move in the direction of acceleration at that instant.

Related theories

Force and Net Force (#22); Free-Body Diagrams and Solving Dynamics Problems (#25); Friction Forces (#29)

24. Newton's Third Law

Origin

Newton stated the action-reaction principle in his 1687 Principia. It clarified contact interactions and provided a systematic way to analyze pairs of interacting bodies.

Core idea

Interactions occur in pairs. During a push, body A acts on B, while B acts on A. Forces arise together, are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction, and have the same interaction type. A foot pushes the ground backward; the ground’s friction on the foot pushes the walker forward. The two accelerations need not match because masses differ.

Key terms and formulas

F(A on B) = −F(B on A) in vector form, each in N. The equation compares forces on two distinct bodies in an inertial Newtonian description. Acceleration still follows ΣF = ma separately for each body.

How to apply it

Name both interacting bodies. Draw two separate free-body diagrams rather than one combined arrow picture. Identify each pair by source and receiver. Then compute net force on each body from all forces acting on that particular body.

Worked example

A child pushes a skateboard with a horizontal force of 30 N. The skateboard pushes the child with 30 N in the opposite direction. If the skateboard has mass 5.0 kg and other horizontal forces are negligible, its acceleration is 30 N / 5.0 kg = 6.0 m/s². Try it: if the push becomes 20 N, what is the reaction magnitude? (Answer: 20 N.)

Limits and common mistakes

An action-reaction pair never cancels within a single body’s net force. Equal forces do not imply equal accelerations when masses differ. Weight and the normal force may balance on one body, but they are not generally a third-law pair.

Related theories

Newton's First Law and Inertial Frames (#20); Force and Net Force (#22); Newton's Second Law (#23)

25. Free-Body Diagrams and Solving Dynamics Problems

Origin

Force diagrams became a standard teaching tool as Newtonian mechanics and engineering statics developed. They make Newton’s vector equations easier to organize and check.

Core idea

The diagram replaces the chosen body by a simple shape or point and displays forces exerted on it by other bodies or fields. Typical arrows include gravity mg downward, normal N perpendicular to contact, tension along a string and friction along a surface opposing relative sliding or its tendency.

Key terms and formulas

For each axis, ΣFₓ = maₓ and ΣFᵧ = maᵧ. Gravitational force near Earth is mg, with g = 9.8 m/s²; forces have unit N. On a horizontal support with no vertical acceleration and no other vertical forces, N = mg. These equalities require the stated conditions.

How to apply it

Identify a single body, choose axes and sketch the surface and string directions. Draw only forces acting on that body and label their sources. Resolve oblique forces into components, write an equation for each axis, solve algebraically and test units. If connected bodies appear, create one diagram per body.

Worked example

A 4.0 kg box rests on a horizontal floor and is pulled right with 18 N against 6.0 N of friction. Vertically N = mg = 4.0 kg · 9.8 m/s² = 39.2 N. Horizontally a = (18 N − 6.0 N) / 4.0 kg = 3.0 m/s² right. Try it: friction increases to 10 N; what is a? (Answer: 2.0 m/s² right.)

Limits and common mistakes

Never add the force that the selected body exerts on another object to its own diagram. Normal force is not automatically mg on a slope or in an accelerating elevator. Velocity arrows are not forces, and friction direction must follow relative motion or impending slip.

Related theories

Force and Net Force (#22); Newton's Second Law (#23); Motion on an Inclined Plane (#30)

26. Law of Universal Gravitation

Origin

Newton published the universal-gravitation law in 1687. In 1798 Henry Cavendish measured the tiny attraction between laboratory masses using a torsion balance, enabling determination of Earth’s density and later G.

Core idea

The force of gravity acts along the line joining the mass centres and is always attractive. All masses participate, yet the small numerical value of G makes gravity between ordinary objects difficult to notice. Above a roughly spherical planet, gravitational acceleration decreases as distance from its centre increases.

Key terms and formulas

F = Gm₁m₂ / r², G ≈ 6.67430 · 10⁻¹¹ Н·м²/кг². F is in Н, masses in кг and centre-to-centre distance r in м. g = GM / r² for a spherical planet of mass M; at altitude h, r = R + h, where R is planetary radius. Near Earth’s surface g ≈ 9.8 м/с² (9.8 m/s²).

How to apply it

Draw the centres of both bodies and measure their separation in metres. Identify masses in kg and substitute into the inverse-square law. For an altitude problem use R + h rather than height above the surface alone. Check that doubling r reduces F fourfold.

Worked example

Two 100 kg spherical masses have centres 1.0 m apart. The attraction is F = 6.67430 · 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg² · (100 kg)(100 kg) / (1.0 m)² ≈ 6.67 · 10⁻⁷ N. Try it: what happens to F if r becomes 2.0 m? (Answer: F ≈ 1.67 · 10⁻⁷ N.)

Limits and common mistakes

The simple point-mass formula is exact for spherical symmetric distributions viewed externally and for point masses; arbitrary shapes require integrating their mass. Near intense gravity, general relativity gives a more complete description. Do not use surface-to-surface separation in place of centre-to-centre distance or mix km and m.

Related theories

27. Gravity, Weight, Weightlessness and Overload

Origin

Newtonian mechanics separates gravitational attraction from forces of support. Studies of accelerated motion and orbital flight made apparent weight and weightlessness especially important in modern physics.

Core idea

Gravity acts on a body and produces downward acceleration near Earth. A scale responds to the contact force between body and support, which changes when the support accelerates. In a freely falling spacecraft, objects and the vehicle accelerate together, so support forces can vanish; astronauts are not beyond Earth’s gravity.

Key terms and formulas

F_g = mg (Н), where m is in кг and g ≈ 9.8 m/s². Apparent weight P = m(g + a) for an elevator accelerating upward, P = m(g − a) downward, and P = 0 in ideal free fall (a = g downward). Overload factor n = P / (mg), dimensionless, for cases with nonzero mg.

How to apply it

Draw separate arrows for gravity mg and support force N. Choose upward positive. For the passenger, N − mg = ma. The scale measures N, while the body’s weight on the scale has equal magnitude. Check that a = 0 recovers mg and free fall gives zero scale reading.

Worked example

A 60 kg passenger rides in an elevator accelerating upward at 2.0 m/s². Gravity is mg = 60 kg · 9.8 m/s² = 588 N. Scale reading P = 60 kg · (9.8 + 2.0) m/s² = 708 N. Try it: at downward acceleration 2.0 m/s², what is P? (Answer: 468 N.)

Limits and common mistakes

Do not identify weight with mass or conclude that no gravity acts during weightlessness. P = m(g ± a) assumes a vertical elevator and an ordinary contact scale. In orbit, weightlessness is chiefly shared free fall, not the absence of gravitational attraction.

Related theories

Newton's Second Law (#23); Law of Universal Gravitation (#26); Satellite Motion and Cosmic Velocities (#33)

28. Elastic Force and Hooke's Law

Origin

Robert Hooke reported his elastic-law findings in the seventeenth century. Spring balances and force-measuring instruments exploit the relation between force and deformation.

Core idea

An external pull changes a spring’s length; the spring exerts a restoring force toward its undeformed position. Stiffness k is the force needed per unit extension. For springs side by side, forces add at equal extensions; end-to-end springs experience equal force but their extensions add.

Key terms and formulas

Fₓ = −kx, where displacement x is in м, k in Н/м and F in Н. Magnitude |F| = k|x|. For ideal parallel springs k_eq = k₁ + k₂; for ideal series springs 1/k_eq = 1/k₁ + 1/k₂. The equations assume massless linear springs and operation within elastic limits.

How to apply it

Locate the spring’s natural length, then measure extension or compression x in metres. Multiply by stiffness for the force magnitude and orient the spring force opposite the displacement. When springs combine, determine whether extension or transmitted force is shared. Check that doubling extension doubles force in the linear regime.

Worked example

A spring of stiffness 200 N/m is stretched 5.0 cm = 0.050 m. The restoring force magnitude is |F| = 200 N/m · 0.050 m = 10 N. Two identical springs in parallel have k_eq = 400 N/m. Try it: same original spring stretched 0.030 m? (Answer: 6.0 N.)

Limits and common mistakes

Hooke’s law is a limited approximation: stretching too far can leave permanent deformation. Do not mix cm with m, confuse spring stiffness with force, or forget the minus sign shows the restoring direction rather than a negative force magnitude.

Related theories

Force and Net Force (#22); Simple Machines and the Golden Rule of Mechanics (#38); Potential Energy (#47)

29. Friction Forces

Origin

Leonardo da Vinci and Guillaume Amontons investigated friction empirically; Charles-Augustin de Coulomb developed related studies in the eighteenth century. Modern tribology examines contact surfaces from microscopic to industrial scales.

Core idea

At a contact, microscopic roughness and adhesion impede slipping. Static friction is whatever force is needed for sticking up to its maximum, not automatically μₛN. Once sliding starts, a simple model treats kinetic friction as μₖN. Rolling resistance is usually much smaller and has different physical causes.

Key terms and formulas

0 ≤ Fₛ ≤ μₛN for the magnitude of static friction; maximum Fₛ,max = μₛN. During sliding Fₖ ≈ μₖN. N is normal reaction in Н; μₛ and μₖ are dimensionless coefficients. On a horizontal floor without extra vertical forces N = mg. Coefficients depend on surfaces and conditions.

How to apply it

Draw the body and locate the direction of relative sliding or impending slip. Find N from forces perpendicular to the surface, then check the static-friction inequality. Use μₖN only after establishing sliding. Apply ΣF = ma for the remaining forces and signs.

Worked example

A 10 kg box slides across a horizontal floor with μₖ = 0.20. N = 10 kg · 9.8 m/s² = 98 N. Sliding friction is Fₖ = 0.20 · 98 N = 19.6 N, opposite the sliding direction. Try it: if μₖ is 0.30, find friction. (Answer: 29.4 N.)

Limits and common mistakes

The μN model is approximate and can fail for sticky, wet, lubricated or high-speed contacts. Static friction is not always at its maximum. Do not automatically set N = mg on an incline, and do not assume friction always opposes the body’s overall motion rather than relative slip.

Related theories

30. Motion on an Inclined Plane

Origin

Inclined-plane experiments were central to Galileo’s seventeenth-century study of accelerated motion. The component method follows Newtonian force analysis.

Core idea

The normal force balances the component of gravity perpendicular to a stationary plane when no other perpendicular forces act. The down-slope component attempts to accelerate the body. Static friction can hold a body at rest; kinetic friction opposes sliding and dissipates energy.

Key terms and formulas

F∥ = mg sin α, N = mg cos α and, for frictionless sliding, a = g sin α. For downhill sliding with kinetic friction coefficient μₖ, a = g(sin α − μₖ cos α). The rest condition is tan α ≤ μₛ for a body supported only by gravity and contact.

How to apply it

Place x along the slope and y perpendicular to it. Draw mg vertically, N perpendicular and friction along the slope. Resolve mg, use ΣFᵧ = 0 to find N, then ΣFₓ = ma. Check whether the body is stationary or sliding before choosing the friction law.

Worked example

A 5.0 kg sled slides down a frictionless 30° incline. F∥ = 5.0 kg · 9.8 m/s² · sin 30° = 24.5 N. Thus a = 24.5 N / 5.0 kg = 4.9 m/s². Try it: for the same slope with μₖ = 0.20, a ≈ ? (Answer: 3.20 m/s² downhill.)

Limits and common mistakes

Angle α is measured from the horizontal. Do not interchange sin α and cos α, assume N = mg, or apply kinetic friction to a body that has not started sliding. The simple formulas neglect air resistance and other applied forces.

Related theories

Newton's Second Law (#23); Free-Body Diagrams and Solving Dynamics Problems (#25); Friction Forces (#29)

31. Connected Bodies: Strings and Pulleys

Origin

Pulley systems have been used since antiquity; their quantitative analysis became routine through Newton’s laws and later engineering mechanics.

Core idea

An ideal string is inextensible and massless, while an ideal pulley has negligible mass and friction. These assumptions force connected segments to move with linked accelerations and allow one tension magnitude along a continuous string. Draw the bodies separately because gravity acts differently on each mass.

Key terms and formulas

For a simple Atwood machine with m₁ greater than m₂, a = (m₁ − m₂)g / (m₁ + m₂); T = m₁(g − a) = m₂(g + a). Acceleration a has unit m/s², tension T newtons, masses kilograms; g = 9.8 m/s². These expressions require an ideal pulley and string.

How to apply it

Select a positive direction for each body consistent with the string constraint. Draw gravity and tension on each body. Write m₁g − T = m₁a and T − m₂g = m₂a, then solve simultaneously. Confirm that tension lies between the two weights.

Worked example

For m₁ = 3.0 kg and m₂ = 2.0 kg, a = (3.0 − 2.0) kg · 9.8 m/s² / (5.0 kg) = 1.96 m/s². T = 3.0 kg · (9.8 − 1.96) m/s² = 23.52 N ≈ 23.5 N. Try it: if both masses are 2.0 kg, what is a? (Answer: 0 m/s².)

Limits and common mistakes

Real pulleys have rotational inertia and bearing friction, and strings may stretch. In complicated moving-pulley arrangements, accelerations need not be equal. Do not assume that tension equals either weight while the system accelerates.

Related theories

Newton's Second Law (#23); Newton's Third Law (#24); Free-Body Diagrams and Solving Dynamics Problems (#25)

32. Dynamics of Circular Motion

Origin

Huygens analyzed circular motion in the seventeenth century, and Newton incorporated its dynamics into the laws of motion. Banked tracks, bridges and loop-the-loop motion became standard applications.

Core idea

Even at constant speed, velocity changes direction around a circle and therefore acceleration points inward. On a flat road static friction provides inward force; on a banked road the normal reaction contributes. At a hilltop, gravity can exceed the normal force and provide the inward resultant.

Key terms and formulas

ΣF_toward centre = mv²/R = mω²R. On a flat turn, maximum ideal friction μₛmg gives v_max = √(μₛgR). On a frictionless banked turn tan θ = v²/(Rg). At the top of a vertical loop, mg + N = mv²/R when inward is downward; contact requires N ≥ 0.

How to apply it

Mark the centre and draw real forces only: gravity, normal, friction or string tension. Choose radial positive inward. Project Newton’s law along that axis and separately along any nonaccelerating direction. Determine the contact or friction condition before accepting the computed speed.

Worked example

A 1000 kg car travels around a level curve of radius 50 m at 10 m/s. Required inward force is F = 1000 kg · (10 m/s)² / 50 m = 2000 N. If friction is the only horizontal force, it must supply 2000 N. Try it: what force at 20 m/s on the same curve? (Answer: 8000 N.)

Limits and common mistakes

For an accelerating speed there is also a tangential component of acceleration. Do not invent an outward centrifugal force in an inertial-frame free-body diagram. Tyre grip, road banking and loss of contact all impose limits on real trajectories.

Related theories

Centripetal Acceleration (#19); Newton's Second Law (#23); Friction Forces (#29)

33. Satellite Motion and Cosmic Velocities

Origin

Newton showed how gravity could account for orbital motion, and Kepler’s planetary laws were established earlier in the seventeenth century. Modern artificial-satellite flight began in 1957 with Sputnik 1.

Core idea

A satellite moving sideways fast enough falls around Earth rather than directly into it. Higher circular orbits have lower speeds but longer periods. A geostationary satellite circles above Earth’s equator in the same direction as Earth’s rotation with an orbital period equal to one sidereal day, remaining over nearly the same longitude.

Key terms and formulas

For circular orbits, GMm/r² = mv²/r, v = √(GM/r), T = 2π√(r³/(GM)). Here G ≈ 6.67430 · 10⁻¹¹ Н·м²/кг², M planet mass in кг, r distance from its centre in м, v in м/с and T in с. Escape speed from radius r is v_esc = √(2GM/r) under a two-body model.

How to apply it

Write r = R + h and decide whether the orbit is circular. Balance gravity with the required radial force and solve for speed or period. For escape speed apply energy conservation rather than the circular-orbit formula. State whether atmosphere and planetary rotation are neglected.

Worked example

Using Earth’s surface values g = 9.8 m/s² and R = 6.37 · 10⁶ m, ideal circular speed v = √(gR) = √(9.8 m/s² · 6.37 · 10⁶ m) ≈ 7.90 · 10³ m/s = 7.90 km/s. Try it: escape speed at that radius? (Answer: √2 · 7.90 km/s ≈ 11.2 km/s.)

Limits and common mistakes

A surface-hugging orbit is a mathematical idealization because atmospheric drag and terrain prevent it. The formulas assume spherical gravity and ignore other bodies. Do not use height h instead of orbital radius R + h or confuse escape speed with the speed for a circular orbit.

Related theories

Law of Universal Gravitation (#26); Dynamics of Circular Motion (#32); Conservation of Mechanical Energy (#48)

34. Drag Forces and Terminal Velocity

Origin

Newton discussed resistance in fluids; later fluid mechanics and experimental aerodynamics developed models of drag at different speeds. Terminal speed follows from balancing forces.

Core idea

At low speeds in viscous flow, drag may be approximately proportional to speed. For larger objects moving rapidly through air, drag is often approximately proportional to v². Shape, fluid density, viscosity and cross-sectional area all matter. Terminal velocity is not an inherent constant of a falling object; it depends on conditions.

Key terms and formulas

Linear model F_d = bv, with b in кг/с; quadratic model F_d = ρC_dAv²/2, where fluid density ρ is in кг/м³, drag coefficient C_d is dimensionless, area A in м² and speed v in м/с. If buoyancy is negligible, at terminal speed mg = F_d; hence v_t = √(2mg/(ρC_dA)) for quadratic drag.

How to apply it

Draw weight downward and drag opposite velocity. Select a drag model appropriate to the flow; solve ΣF = ma if acceleration is required. For terminal motion put a = 0 and balance mg against drag, including buoyancy if significant. Check that larger area decreases terminal speed.

Worked example

A 2.0 kg object falls in air where its quadratic drag is F_d = (0.50 kg/m)v². At terminal motion, mg = 2.0 kg · 9.8 m/s² = 19.6 N = (0.50 kg/m)v_t². Thus v_t = √(39.2 m²/s²) ≈ 6.26 m/s. Try it: if drag coefficient in this model doubles, what happens to v_t? (Answer: it becomes 6.26/√2 ≈ 4.43 m/s.)

Limits and common mistakes

The linear and quadratic laws are approximations for different flow regimes. Real parachutes change shape and airflow; a changing drag force generally requires numerical integration for the full fall. Do not call terminal motion force-free: forces still act but balance.

Related theories

Newton's Second Law (#23); Friction Forces (#29); Fluid Flow and the Continuity Equation (#55)

35. Non-Inertial Frames and Inertial Forces

Origin

Newton distinguished inertial motion from acceleration. Later mechanics formalized inertial forces in translating and rotating frames, including centrifugal and Coriolis effects.

Core idea

From the road, a passenger tends to maintain velocity until the seat accelerates the body. From the bus, an apparent backward force must be introduced to explain the observed relative acceleration. In a rotating frame, centrifugal inertial force points outward, while Coriolis force depends on velocity relative to the frame. These are frame-dependent, not new interactions.

Key terms and formulas

For a frame translating with acceleration a_frame, F_inertial = −ma_frame and ΣF_real + F_inertial = ma_relative. In a rotating frame with angular speed ω, centrifugal force magnitude mω²r points away from the axis; Coriolis force has vector form F_C = −2m(ω × v_relative). Mass m in кг, acceleration in м/с² and force in Н.

How to apply it

First choose the reference frame. If it accelerates, identify its translational or rotational acceleration. Draw real forces; add inertial terms only for a non-inertial frame and apply the correct signs. Compare with a solution in an approximately inertial road or laboratory frame.

Worked example

A 60 kg passenger is at rest relative to a bus accelerating east at 2.0 m/s². In the bus frame an inertial force of magnitude F = 60 kg · 2.0 m/s² = 120 N points west; a real seat-contact force of 120 N east balances it. Try it: same bus acceleration for an 80 kg passenger? (Answer: 160 N west.)

Limits and common mistakes

An outward centrifugal force is not an extra real interaction in an inertial frame. Coriolis and centrifugal terms require care with axes and signs. The simple translational formula assumes a frame without rotation; rotating frames require additional terms.

Related theories

Newton's First Law and Inertial Frames (#20); Newton's Second Law (#23); Dynamics of Circular Motion (#32)

36. Moment of Force and Conditions of Equilibrium

Origin

The law of the lever was studied by Archimedes in antiquity. Newtonian statics developed the combined requirements for translational and rotational equilibrium.

Core idea

A force acting through a pivot has zero moment about that pivot. Another force applied farther away can have greater turning effect even if it is smaller. Clockwise and counterclockwise torques have opposite signs by an agreed convention. A beam supported at two points requires both force and moment balance.

Key terms and formulas

|τ| = rF sin θ = Fd, torque in Н·м, r and perpendicular lever arm d in м, F in Н. Conditions of static equilibrium are ΣFₓ = 0, ΣFᵧ = 0 and Στ = 0 about any axis. The equality of clockwise and counterclockwise moments applies for a planar nonrotating body.

How to apply it

Choose the full body, draw all forces including support reactions and weight. Pick a pivot that eliminates unknown reaction torques. Assign signs to moments, solve Στ = 0, then apply ΣF = 0 to find remaining forces. Verify results using a different pivot.

Worked example

A horizontal lever has a 20 N downward force 0.30 m to the right of the pivot. Its clockwise torque magnitude is τ = 20 N · 0.30 m = 6.0 N·m. A downward force 0.20 m to the left must be 6.0 N·m / 0.20 m = 30 N to balance the torque. Try it: balancing force at 0.60 m on the left? (Answer: 10 N.)

Limits and common mistakes

Zero net torque alone does not guarantee equilibrium if net force is not zero. Do not use the full distance r when the force is oblique: use d = r sin θ. Torque unit N·m is not interchangeable with energy simply because joules also reduce dimensionally to N·m.

Related theories

Force and Net Force (#22); Center of Mass and Types of Equilibrium (#37); Simple Machines and the Golden Rule of Mechanics (#38)

37. Center of Mass and Types of Equilibrium

Origin

Archimedes investigated balance and centres of gravity; Newtonian mechanics later generalized the ideas to systems of particles and moving bodies.

Core idea

The centre of mass acts as the weighted location for translational motion. For a uniform symmetric object it is at its geometric centre, but a nonuniform body can have it elsewhere, even outside its material. Stable equilibrium resists a small disturbance, unstable equilibrium amplifies it, and neutral equilibrium has no restoring or destabilizing tendency.

Key terms and formulas

Along one axis x_cm = (Σmᵢxᵢ)/(Σmᵢ), with masses mᵢ in кг and coordinates xᵢ, x_cm in м. In a uniform gravitational field the weight can be treated as acting at the centre of gravity, coincident with the centre of mass. Equilibrium stability is assessed through the change of gravitational potential energy.

How to apply it

Choose an origin and assign signed coordinates to each piece. Multiply each mass by its coordinate, sum and divide by total mass. For tipping, draw the vertical projection of the centre of mass and compare it with the support polygon. Examine how small shifts change the height of the centre of mass.

Worked example

Two point masses lie on a line: 2.0 kg at x = 0 m and 1.0 kg at x = 3.0 m. Then x_cm = (2.0 kg · 0 m + 1.0 kg · 3.0 m)/(3.0 kg) = 1.0 m. Try it: move the second mass to x = 6.0 m; where is the centre of mass? (Answer: 2.0 m.)

Limits and common mistakes

The geometric centre equals the centre of mass only for suitable uniform symmetry. Tipping also depends on forces and motion, not solely static geometry during a fast manoeuvre. Do not average positions without mass weights or confuse a small contact patch with a body’s centre of mass.

Related theories

Moment of Force and Conditions of Equilibrium (#36); Rotation of a Rigid Body and Moment of Inertia (#39); Potential Energy (#47)

38. Simple Machines and the Golden Rule of Mechanics

Origin

Levers and pulleys were used in ancient construction and studied systematically by Archimedes. Energy conservation later explained the common work trade-off in ideal machines.

Core idea

A lever uses different distances from a pivot; a movable pulley shares a load across rope segments; an inclined plane extends the travel distance needed to gain height. In ideal conditions input work equals useful output work. Real devices convert some input energy into internal energy through friction.

Key terms and formulas

Ideal work relation F_in s_in = F_out s_out, with force in Н and displacement in м, work in Дж. Mechanical advantage MA = F_out/F_in. Efficiency η = A_useful/A_input · 100%, where η ≤ 100%. For a lever in equilibrium F₁d₁ = F₂d₂; an ideal single movable pulley provides MA = 2.

How to apply it

Choose the machine geometry and identify input and output movements. Count supporting rope segments for a pulley or measure lever arms. Apply ideal work balance, then account for efficiency if given. Check that a gain in force corresponds to a greater input distance.

Worked example

An ideal movable pulley raises a 200 N load by 2.0 m. With two supporting rope segments, applied force is F_in = 200 N / 2 = 100 N and pulled rope length is 4.0 m. Input and output work are each 400 J. Try it: at 80% efficiency, what input work is needed for 400 J useful work? (Answer: 500 J.)

Limits and common mistakes

Simple machines cannot create energy. A fixed pulley redirects force but has no ideal force advantage. Do not claim force gain is work gain, count the free rope end incorrectly, or assume a real machine has 100% efficiency.

Related theories

Moment of Force and Conditions of Equilibrium (#36); Mechanical Work (#44); Power and Efficiency (#45)

39. Rotation of a Rigid Body and Moment of Inertia

Origin

Newtonian mechanics described angular motion, while Euler and later physicists developed systematic rigid-body dynamics. Modern formulas are obtained by adding the contributions of mass elements at different distances from the axis.

Core idea

Moment of inertia measures resistance to changing angular speed about a specified axis. Mass farther from the axis contributes more because of the distance squared. Rotational kinetic energy depends on both this distribution and angular speed. The same object has different moments of inertia about different axes.

Key terms and formulas

I = Σmᵢrᵢ², unit кг·м²; point mass I = mr², thin ring MR², solid disk MR²/2, thin rod about centre ML²/12. Net torque τ = Iα for a rigid body rotating about a fixed axis; α in рад/с². Rotational energy E_rot = Iω²/2, in Дж, with ω in рад/с.

How to apply it

Identify the axis before selecting an inertia formula. Convert lengths to metres, calculate I and determine torque signs. Solve τ = Iα if inertia is fixed. For energy, use the angular speed in radians per second. Check that spreading mass outward increases I.

Worked example

A uniform solid disk has M = 2.0 kg and R = 0.50 m. Its inertia is I = MR²/2 = 2.0 kg · (0.50 m)² / 2 = 0.25 kg·m². Applied net torque τ = 1.0 N·m gives α = 1.0 N·m / 0.25 kg·m² = 4.0 rad/s². Try it: E_rot at ω = 4.0 rad/s? (Answer: 2.0 J.)

Limits and common mistakes

The formulas assume rigid bodies with specified axes; deformable structures or changing axes require broader methods. Do not confuse inertia I with mass, use diameter in place of radius, or forget that moment of inertia depends on axis location.

Related theories

Uniform Circular Motion: Period, Frequency and Angular Velocity (#18); Moment of Force and Conditions of Equilibrium (#36); Angular Momentum and Its Conservation (#40)

40. Angular Momentum and Its Conservation

Origin

Euler and later classical mechanicians developed angular momentum as part of rotational dynamics. Its conservation follows from rotational symmetry in modern theoretical physics and explains features of planetary motion and spinning bodies.

Core idea

Angular momentum is a vector about a chosen origin or axis. A net external torque changes its value or direction. If torque is zero, angular momentum stays constant, even while the shape of a system changes. A rotating top may maintain a stable orientation for a time, but external torque can cause precession.

Key terms and formulas

For a particle L = r × p (vector), where p = mv; for a rigid body about a fixed principal axis L = Iω. Units кг·м²/с. Net external torque τ_ext = dL/dt, and if τ_ext = 0, L_initial = L_final. Thus I₁ω₁ = I₂ω₂ for a rotating system about the same axis under zero external torque.

How to apply it

Choose the rotation axis and list external torques about it. If their sum is negligible, equate initial and final angular momentum. Determine initial and final inertia from the mass distribution, then solve for angular speed. Verify that pulling mass inward increases angular speed and rotational kinetic energy can rise due to internal work.

Worked example

A skater has initial I₁ = 4.0 kg·m² and ω₁ = 2.0 rad/s. With arms drawn inward, I₂ = 2.0 kg·m². Conservation gives ω₂ = I₁ω₁/I₂ = (4.0 kg·m² · 2.0 rad/s)/(2.0 kg·m²) = 4.0 rad/s. Try it: if I₂ = 1.0 kg·m² instead, what is ω₂? (Answer: 8.0 rad/s.)

Limits and common mistakes

Angular momentum conservation requires negligible external torque about the selected origin. Rotational kinetic energy need not be constant when the skater does work to move their arms. Do not confuse angular momentum with torque or assume L = Iω works as a simple scalar for arbitrary three-dimensional rotation.

Related theories

Rotation of a Rigid Body and Moment of Inertia (#39); Conservation of Momentum (#42); Conservation of Mechanical Energy (#48)

41. Momentum and Impulse

Origin

Newton developed the mathematical description of momentum in the seventeenth century. His second law relates force to the rate of change of momentum, which is especially useful for impacts that happen over short times.

Core idea

Momentum is a vector in the direction of velocity. A push changes it; doubling mass at fixed speed doubles momentum. Impulse measures the accumulated action of force during an interval. For a fixed momentum change, a longer collision distributes the change over more time and lowers the average force. During a rebound the change can exceed the initial momentum magnitude.

Key terms and formulas

p = mv, where p is momentum in kg·m/s, m mass in kg and v velocity in m/s. J = F_avgΔt = Δp for a constant average net force F_avg in N over time Δt in s. More generally J is the area under a force–time graph; its unit N·s equals kg·m/s. Use signed projections along an axis.

How to apply it

Choose a positive direction. Convert masses to kilograms and speeds to metres per second. Calculate initial and final signed momenta, subtract to obtain Δp, then divide by collision time for average net force. Include a negative sign if the force opposes your axis.

Worked example

A 0.20 kg ball travelling right at 10 m/s stops in 0.050 s. Initial momentum p₁ = 0.20 kg · 10 m/s = 2.0 kg·m/s; final p₂ = 0. Therefore Δp = −2.0 kg·m/s and F_avg = −2.0 kg·m/s / 0.050 s = −40 N. Try it: stopping in 0.10 s instead gives what force? (−20 N).

Limits and common mistakes

For a changing force use its time average or integrate its force–time graph. Momentum is not kinetic energy: the first depends linearly on velocity and has direction. Do not confuse impulse with force, omit units, or ignore the sign when a ball reverses direction.

Related theories

Conservation of Momentum (#42); Elastic and Inelastic Collisions (#49); Newton's Second Law (#23)

42. Conservation of Momentum

Origin

Momentum conservation grew from the work of Descartes, Huygens and Newton during the seventeenth century. Newton’s action–reaction law explains why internal forces do not change a system’s total momentum.

Core idea

Treat two interacting bodies as one system. Internal forces exchange momentum between components, but their impulses cancel in the total. When external forces have no net impulse during the interval, total momentum before equals total momentum after. The individual bodies can speed up, slow down or reverse direction while the total remains unchanged.

Key terms and formulas

Σp_before = Σp_after in kg·m/s if external impulse J_ext = 0. In one dimension m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ = m₁u₁ + m₂u₂, with initial velocities v and final velocities u in m/s. In general Δp_total = J_ext, a vector equation.

How to apply it

Specify exactly which objects belong to the system. Draw external forces and decide whether their impulse is negligible along the chosen axis. Assign positive and negative directions, write the equation before substituting, and check the combined momentum afterward.

Worked example

Two stationary skaters push apart. A 60 kg skater moves right at 2.0 m/s. Initially p_total = 0 kg·m/s; finally 60 kg · 2.0 m/s + 40 kg · v = 0. Thus v = −3.0 m/s. Try it: if the second skater has mass 30 kg, what speed is needed? (4.0 m/s left).

Limits and common mistakes

Momentum is conserved only for a closed system or in a direction with zero net external impulse. Energy need not stay in kinetic form during a collision. Never set individual momenta equal in direction: the vector sum, including negative projections, is conserved.

Related theories

Momentum and Impulse (#41); Elastic and Inelastic Collisions (#49); Newton's Third Law (#24)

43. Jet Propulsion

Origin

Konstantin Tsiolkovsky published foundational mathematical work on rocket motion in 1903. Ukrainian-born spaceflight pioneer Yuriy Kondratyuk studied efficient space trajectories, and Serhiy Korolyov later led major Soviet rocket projects.

Core idea

A rocket and its exhaust form a system whose total momentum is conserved when external impulses can be neglected. Mass exits the engine backward; the rocket gains forward momentum. This is continuous recoil. A squid uses the same physical principle by ejecting water. A rocket carries both fuel and oxidizer, so no atmosphere is needed.

Key terms and formulas

For ideal steady exhaust, thrust F ≈ ṁu, where ṁ is expelled mass per second in kg/s and u is exhaust speed relative to the rocket in m/s; F is in N. For an ideal rocket without external forces, Δv = u ln(m₀ / m_f), where m₀ and m_f are initial and final masses in kg and ln is the natural logarithm. This ideal relation assumes constant exhaust speed and omits atmospheric drag and gravity losses.

How to apply it

First identify whether gas speed is relative to the rocket. For thrust multiply mass flow by exhaust speed; if there is a pressure-thrust contribution, simple F ≈ ṁu is incomplete. For speed change use a mass ratio greater than one and a consistent exhaust speed.

Worked example

An engine ejects 2.0 kg/s of gas backward at 500 m/s relative to the engine. F ≈ 2.0 kg/s · 500 m/s = 1000 N forward. Try it: what thrust results from 3.0 kg/s at the same speed? (1500 N).

Limits and common mistakes

Actual engines can have additional pressure thrust, changing exhaust speed and losses to gravity and air resistance. Do not assume the rocket needs air for support. Do not insert ground-relative exhaust speed into the ideal thrust formula without accounting for the reference frame.

Related theories

Conservation of Momentum (#42); Momentum and Impulse (#41); Newton's Second Law (#23)

44. Mechanical Work

Origin

The quantitative connection between force, displacement and energy developed in eighteenth- and nineteenth-century mechanics, including work by Coriolis and Poncelet. It enabled engineers to compare the useful effects of different machines.

Core idea

Work is positive when a force has a component along the displacement, negative when opposite, and zero when perpendicular. A force may act yet do no mechanical work if its point of application does not move. The work done by gravity depends on the vertical height difference, not on the path.

Key terms and formulas

W = Fs cos α for constant F in N and displacement s in m, with W in joules (J = N·m). For one-dimensional variable force W equals the signed area under F(x). Near Earth, work done by gravity during a downward height change h is W_g = mgh, with g ≈ 9.8 m/s².

How to apply it

Draw the displacement and the force, then identify the angle between them. Convert the distance to metres and find the component along motion. Multiply F by s and cos α; confirm whether work should be positive, negative or zero.

Worked example

A student pulls a suitcase horizontally with a constant 30 N force through 4.0 m. α = 0°, so W = 30 N · 4.0 m · cos 0° = 120 J. Try it: if the same force acts at 60° to the path, what work does it do? (60 J).

Limits and common mistakes

W = Fs cos α is valid for a constant force and a well-defined displacement of its application point. For varying forces use the integral or area under the graph. Do not confuse work with effort or count a perpendicular force as doing work.

Related theories

Power and Efficiency (#45); Kinetic Energy and the Work–Energy Theorem (#46); Potential Energy (#47)

45. Power and Efficiency

Origin

Power became central to engineering during the development of steam engines. James Watt popularised comparing machine output with horsepower; the SI unit of power is named after him.

Core idea

Two machines may do the same amount of work but differ in time. The faster machine has greater power. Real machines lose some supplied energy through heat, sound or friction, so efficiency is less than 100%. Mechanical power at an instant equals the component of force along velocity multiplied by speed.

Key terms and formulas

Average P = W / t, with W in J, t in s and P in watts (W). Instantaneous P = Fv cos α for force F in N and speed v in m/s. η = W_useful / W_input = P_useful / P_input under comparable intervals; percentage efficiency is 100η%. One metric horsepower is about 735.5 W.

How to apply it

Calculate useful work first, often mgh for lifting. Divide by duration to obtain useful power. To account for losses, divide useful power by efficiency expressed as a decimal, not a percentage number.

Worked example

Lift 50 kg by 2.0 m in 5.0 s, using g = 9.8 m/s². W = 50 kg · 9.8 m/s² · 2.0 m = 980 J. P_useful = 980 J / 5.0 s = 196 W. At η = 0.80, P_input = 196 W / 0.80 = 245 W. Try it: at 100% efficiency, what input power is needed? (196 W).

Limits and common mistakes

Efficiency is a ratio, not an energy or power. A machine cannot have energy efficiency above 100%. Do not confuse 80% with 80, or confuse watts with joules. P = Fv needs the component of force parallel to velocity.

Related theories

Mechanical Work (#44); Kinetic Energy and the Work–Energy Theorem (#46); Simple Machines and the Golden Rule of Mechanics (#38)

46. Kinetic Energy and the Work–Energy Theorem

Origin

The modern work–energy relation emerged through nineteenth-century studies of motion and energy. The name kinetic energy and its mathematical role became established as mechanics developed.

Core idea

Mass matters linearly and speed matters quadratically. A moving object can transfer kinetic energy to another system through work. Positive net work speeds a body up; negative net work slows it down. The theorem connects start and end speeds without first solving the full motion over time.

Key terms and formulas

Eₖ = mv² / 2, with m in kg, speed v in m/s and energy in J. W_net = Eₖ,f − Eₖ,i = m(v_f² − v_i²)/2. For a constant net braking force of magnitude F and stopping distance s, Fs = mv₀²/2.

How to apply it

Identify the body and all forces that do work. Determine initial and final speeds; calculate ΔEₖ. Set total work, including its sign, equal to ΔEₖ. For braking, use negative work and solve for the positive stopping distance.

Worked example

A 1000 kg car travels at 20 m/s. Eₖ = 1000 kg · (20 m/s)² / 2 = 200,000 J. If the constant braking force is 5000 N, s = 200,000 J / 5000 N = 40 m. Try it: at 10 m/s with the same braking force, what is the stopping distance? (10 m).

Limits and common mistakes

This is a classical, nonrelativistic formula. The calculated distance excludes reaction distance and assumes constant braking force and level travel. Do not calculate energy with signed velocity or forget to square speed.

Related theories

Mechanical Work (#44); Potential Energy (#47); Conservation of Mechanical Energy (#48)

47. Potential Energy

Origin

Gravitational potential energy developed from early mechanics, while spring energy followed studies of elasticity including Hooke’s seventeenth-century work. The modern conservation framework joined these ideas.

Core idea

A raised object can gain kinetic energy as it falls because its gravitational potential energy decreases. A spring can release stored energy as its stretch reduces. Potential energy depends on the relative arrangement of interacting bodies, and its zero level may be chosen conveniently. Only energy differences determine work done by conservative forces.

Key terms and formulas

Near a nearly uniform gravitational field U_g = mgh, where m is in kg, g ≈ 9.8 m/s², h in m and U in J. Elastic U_s = kx²/2, where k is spring stiffness in N/m and x deformation in m. For a conservative force, W = −ΔU.

How to apply it

Choose the zero level for gravitational energy, often the ground or lowest point. Determine height differences rather than absolute heights. For a spring measure displacement from its undeformed equilibrium length. Check whether the spring remains in the elastic regime.

Worked example

A 2.0 kg book is placed 1.5 m above the floor. U = 2.0 kg · 9.8 m/s² · 1.5 m = 29.4 J relative to the floor. Try it: what is its energy relative to a shelf 0.50 m high? (19.6 J).

Limits and common mistakes

The expression mgh assumes a nearly constant gravitational field; over great altitude changes use gravitational potential −GMm/r. Hooke’s spring formula fails beyond the linear elastic range. Do not report potential energy without explaining the reference level.

Related theories

Kinetic Energy and the Work–Energy Theorem (#46); Conservation of Mechanical Energy (#48); Elastic Force and Hooke's Law (#28)

48. Conservation of Mechanical Energy

Origin

Conservation of energy was established in the nineteenth century through the work of scientists including Mayer, Joule and Helmholtz. Its mechanical form combines the work–energy theorem with conservative forces.

Core idea

Gravity converts potential energy into kinetic energy during descent and reverses this conversion during ascent. The same transformation occurs in a pendulum or roller coaster. If friction acts, mechanical energy decreases while heat and other energy forms increase, so total energy is still conserved.

Key terms and formulas

E_mech = Eₖ + U. If nonconservative forces do no work, Eₖ₁ + U₁ = Eₖ₂ + U₂, with all terms in J. Including friction or an external drive, W_noncons = ΔE_mech. Near Earth use Eₖ = mv²/2 and U = mgh.

How to apply it

Mark two positions and choose a zero for potential energy. List the forces and decide whether nonconservative work can be neglected. Write total mechanical energy at both points before inserting values. Cancel mass only if it occurs throughout.

Worked example

A ball is dropped from rest at 5.0 m without air resistance. At release mgh = mg · 5.0 m. Just above the ground mv²/2 = mg · 5.0 m, so v = √(2 · 9.8 m/s² · 5.0 m) = 9.90 m/s. Try it: what is the speed after falling 1.25 m? (4.95 m/s).

Limits and common mistakes

Do not claim mechanical energy is constant when significant friction, air resistance or propulsion acts. Distinguish mechanical-energy conservation from total-energy conservation. The initial speed must be included when the object is not released from rest.

Related theories

Kinetic Energy and the Work–Energy Theorem (#46); Potential Energy (#47); Mechanical Work (#44)

49. Elastic and Inelastic Collisions

Origin

Christiaan Huygens studied elastic collisions in the seventeenth century. Later energy and momentum principles gave a unified description of elastic and inelastic impacts.

Core idea

During a brief impact, large internal contact forces change individual momenta. Their impulses balance inside the system. If the collision is elastic, the total kinetic energy is unchanged. If it is perfectly inelastic, the objects move together afterward and kinetic energy is reduced, though total energy remains conserved.

Key terms and formulas

In one dimension m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ = m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ when external impulse is negligible. Elastic impact also satisfies m₁v₁²/2 + m₂v₂²/2 = m₁u₁²/2 + m₂u₂²/2. For sticking bodies u = (m₁v₁ + m₂v₂)/(m₁ + m₂). Masses are in kg, speeds in m/s.

How to apply it

Choose a horizontal axis and assign signed velocities. Classify the collision before selecting equations. For sticking objects use a common final velocity; for elastic impact use both momentum and kinetic energy. Calculate energy loss only after finding final speeds.

Worked example

A 2.0 kg cart moving right at 3.0 m/s sticks to a stationary 1.0 kg cart. Total initial momentum is 2.0 kg · 3.0 m/s = 6.0 kg·m/s. Shared final speed u = 6.0 kg·m/s / 3.0 kg = 2.0 m/s. Initial kinetic energy is 9.0 J and final is 6.0 J, so 3.0 J becomes other forms. Try it: what is the shared speed if the second cart has mass 2.0 kg? (1.5 m/s).

Limits and common mistakes

Assume the impact is short enough for external impulse to be negligible. Momentum conservation alone does not imply conservation of kinetic energy. Inelastic does not necessarily mean the bodies stick; sticking defines the perfectly inelastic case.

Related theories

Conservation of Momentum (#42); Kinetic Energy and the Work–Energy Theorem (#46); Conservation of Mechanical Energy (#48)

50. Pressure

Origin

Blaise Pascal investigated fluids and pressure in the seventeenth century. The SI unit of pressure, the pascal, honours his contributions.

Core idea

For a surface under normal force, pressure equals that force divided by contact area. A smaller area concentrates a given force. In a resting fluid, pressure acts in all directions and molecular collisions help explain gas pressure. Pressure itself has no direction even though the force on a particular surface does.

Key terms and formulas

p = F_perp / A, where p is pressure in pascals (Pa = N/m²), F_perp perpendicular force in N and A surface area in m². Rearranged, F_perp = pA and A = F_perp / p. For fluids, distinguish absolute pressure from gauge pressure relative to the atmosphere.

How to apply it

Identify the contact surface and the force component normal to it. Convert cm² to m² by multiplying by 10⁻⁴. Divide force by area, then check whether your result is sensible for a pointed or broad contact.

Worked example

A 200 N load presses uniformly on a surface of 100 cm². A = 100 · 10⁻⁴ m² = 0.010 m². p = 200 N / 0.010 m² = 20,000 Pa = 20 kPa. Try it: what if the area doubles? (10 kPa).

Limits and common mistakes

The elementary formula gives average pressure over an area and assumes the relevant perpendicular force is known. Actual contact pressure can vary spatially. Do not divide by a length or use area in cm² without converting. Pressure is not the same quantity as force.

Related theories

Hydrostatic Pressure and Communicating Vessels (#51); Pascal's Law and the Hydraulic Press (#52); Atmospheric Pressure (#53)

51. Hydrostatic Pressure and Communicating Vessels

Origin

Simon Stevin established the dependence of hydrostatic pressure on depth in the late sixteenth century. Later fluid statics explained communicating vessels and hydraulic systems.

Core idea

Each deeper layer must support the weight of liquid above it. Hydrostatic pressure therefore depends on depth, liquid density and gravity, not on vessel shape. In connected containers, fluid flows until the pressures at matching depths balance. With two immiscible liquids, the columns can have different heights.

Key terms and formulas

Gauge pressure p_g = ρgh, where ρ density in kg/m³, g ≈ 9.8 m/s², h depth in m and p_g in Pa. Absolute pressure p_abs = p_surface + ρgh. In communicating columns at the same level, ρ₁gh₁ = ρ₂gh₂ when the top pressures are equal.

How to apply it

Draw a horizontal comparison level in the liquid. Mark depths from the appropriate free surfaces. Use density in kg/m³ and metres for height. For connected liquids equate pressures at the same depth and solve for unknown heights.

Worked example

Calculate gauge pressure at depth 2.0 m in water of density 1000 kg/m³. p_g = 1000 kg/m³ · 9.8 m/s² · 2.0 m = 19,600 Pa = 19.6 kPa. Try it: pressure at 3.0 m depth? (29.4 kPa gauge).

Limits and common mistakes

This relation assumes a fluid at rest with nearly constant density. Flowing fluids need additional terms. Do not confuse gauge with absolute pressure or claim that a wide vessel creates higher pressure at the same depth.

Related theories

Pressure (#50); Pascal's Law and the Hydraulic Press (#52); Archimedes' Principle and Flotation (#54)

52. Pascal's Law and the Hydraulic Press

Origin

Blaise Pascal investigated pressure transmission in fluids during the seventeenth century. Hydraulic machinery later used this effect in presses, lifts and brakes.

Core idea

An enclosed, nearly incompressible fluid passes an applied pressure increase to other parts of the system. Because force equals pressure multiplied by area, the same pressure on a larger piston creates greater force. Conservation of energy requires a trade-off: the large piston moves less distance than the small one.

Key terms and formulas

For ideal pistons at equal elevation, F₁/A₁ = F₂/A₂, where forces are in N and areas in m². Displaced volumes obey A₁s₁ = A₂s₂, with distances s in m, so F₁s₁ = F₂s₂ without losses. At different heights include hydrostatic pressure differences.

How to apply it

Label the small and large piston areas. Convert areas to m² and use their ratio to find output force. Then use volume conservation to calculate travel distance and check that output work does not exceed input work.

Worked example

A press has A₁ = 0.0020 m², A₂ = 0.020 m² and F₁ = 100 N. F₂ = 100 N · 0.020 m² / 0.0020 m² = 1000 N. If the small piston moves 0.10 m, the large moves 0.010 m. Try it: with input 50 N, find output force. (500 N).

Limits and common mistakes

The ideal model ignores friction, fluid compression and piston-height differences. High-pressure fluid can injure people; do not open or service a pressurised hydraulic system. Never treat force multiplication as a gain in mechanical work.

Related theories

Pressure (#50); Hydrostatic Pressure and Communicating Vessels (#51); Simple Machines and the Golden Rule of Mechanics (#38)

53. Atmospheric Pressure

Origin

Evangelista Torricelli demonstrated the mercury barometer in 1643. Pascal later investigated how atmospheric pressure varies with altitude. Otto von Guericke demonstrated atmospheric forces with the Magdeburg hemispheres.

Core idea

The atmosphere presses on all exposed surfaces. A barometer balances atmospheric pressure against the weight of a liquid column. A sealed or evacuated chamber can therefore experience a large net force from external air. Atmospheric pressure generally falls with altitude, but density and temperature also vary.

Key terms and formulas

Near sea level standard atmospheric pressure p₀ = 101,325 Pa ≈ 101 kPa ≈ 760 mmHg. For a barometer, p_atm = ρgh, with ρ liquid density in kg/m³ and h column height in m; g ≈ 9.8 m/s². These numbers are reference values, not every location’s actual pressure.

How to apply it

Check whether the pressure given is absolute or gauge. For a liquid column use the density of that liquid, not water unless stated. Convert mmHg to pascals using approximately 133.322 Pa per mmHg, and compare to the standard value.

Worked example

A mercury barometer reads 760 mmHg. With 1 mmHg ≈ 133.322 Pa, p ≈ 760 · 133.322 Pa = 101,324.7 Pa ≈ 101 kPa. Try it: what pressure corresponds to 750 mmHg? (about 100 kPa).

Limits and common mistakes

Air density changes with altitude, so p = ρgh cannot describe a tall atmosphere using one constant density. Mercury is toxic; never open or construct mercury barometers. Do not assume standard pressure is the exact pressure at every place or time.

Related theories

Pressure (#50); Hydrostatic Pressure and Communicating Vessels (#51); Archimedes' Principle and Flotation (#54)

54. Archimedes' Principle and Flotation

Origin

Archimedes of Syracuse formulated the buoyancy principle in antiquity, traditionally associated with investigations of floating bodies. The principle now applies to liquids and gases.

Core idea

Fluid pressure increases with depth, so the force on the lower part of a submerged body is greater than that on the upper part. The resulting upward force equals the weight of fluid displaced. Floating equilibrium occurs when upward buoyancy equals downward weight. A hollow steel ship can float because the ship’s overall average density is below that of water.

Key terms and formulas

F_b = ρ_f g V_sub, with density ρ_f in kg/m³, g ≈ 9.8 m/s², submerged volume V_sub in m³ and force in N. For a floating object, F_b = mg. Fully submerged bodies sink if average ρ_body > ρ_f, rise if lower, and are neutrally buoyant if equal, ignoring other forces.

How to apply it

Identify the surrounding fluid and the displaced volume, not automatically the entire body volume. Calculate buoyancy and compare it with weight. For floating objects equate the two forces and solve for submerged fraction or displaced volume.

Worked example

A block displaces 0.0030 m³ of freshwater (ρ = 1000 kg/m³). F_b = 1000 kg/m³ · 9.8 m/s² · 0.0030 m³ = 29.4 N. Try it: what buoyant force acts if the displaced volume is 0.0050 m³? (49 N).

Limits and common mistakes

For strongly accelerating flows, buoyancy alone may not describe all fluid forces. Density must refer to the fluid, not the object. Do not use total volume when a body is only partly submerged. A balloon is buoyed up by air, not by a mysterious lifting force.

Related theories

Hydrostatic Pressure and Communicating Vessels (#51); Pressure (#50); Fluid Flow and the Continuity Equation (#55)

55. Fluid Flow and the Continuity Equation

Origin

The continuity relation follows from conservation of mass and was developed within classical fluid mechanics. It underpins modern water systems, aerodynamics and blood-flow models.

Core idea

Liquid cannot accumulate inside a steady, unbranched pipe. Therefore a narrow section must carry the same volume each second with a higher speed than a wide section. Laminar flow moves in smooth layers; turbulent flow contains fluctuating eddies. In gases, density changes may need to be included.

Key terms and formulas

For incompressible flow Q = Av, where Q is volume flow rate in m³/s, A cross-sectional area in m² and v mean flow speed in m/s. Along an unbranched steady pipe A₁v₁ = A₂v₂. For compressible steady flow, ρ₁A₁v₁ = ρ₂A₂v₂ expresses mass conservation.

How to apply it

Identify two cross-sections and ensure they belong to the same unbranched flow. Convert cm² to m², or use an area ratio in matching units. Calculate the missing average speed and check that narrower sections give larger speeds for incompressible flow.

Worked example

Water enters a hose with A₁ = 4.0 cm² and v₁ = 0.50 m/s. The nozzle area is A₂ = 1.0 cm². v₂ = (A₁/A₂)v₁ = (4.0/1.0) · 0.50 m/s = 2.0 m/s. Try it: if nozzle area becomes 2.0 cm², what is v₂? (1.0 m/s).

Limits and common mistakes

The simple formula requires steady incompressible flow and accounts for no branches or leaks. Real pipe losses affect pressures and speeds but not mass conservation. Do not confuse area with diameter: halving diameter reduces area by a factor of four.

Related theories

Pressure (#50); Bernoulli's Equation and Its Applications (#56); Hydrostatic Pressure and Communicating Vessels (#51)

56. Bernoulli's Equation and Its Applications

Origin

Daniel Bernoulli described the energy-based relation in his book Hydrodynamica (1738). Later developments connected it to broader energy conservation in flowing fluids.

Core idea

Pressure energy, kinetic energy per unit volume and gravitational potential energy per unit volume can exchange as fluid flows. At equal height, a rise in speed requires a drop in static pressure if no energy is lost. An atomizer uses a moving flow to establish a pressure difference. Aircraft wing lift requires the full flow field and deflection of air, not the incorrect equal-transit-time story.

Key terms and formulas

p + ρv²/2 + ρgh = constant along a streamline for steady, incompressible, nonviscous flow, where p is static pressure in Pa, ρ density in kg/m³, v speed in m/s, g ≈ 9.8 m/s² and h elevation in m. At equal heights, p₁ − p₂ = ρ(v₂² − v₁²)/2.

How to apply it

Locate two points on the same streamline. Mark heights, static pressures and mean speeds. For a level pipe cancel height terms; otherwise retain them. Solve for unknown pressure or speed, and check signs and units. Consider energy losses in real pipes.

Worked example

Water of density 1000 kg/m³ speeds up along a level streamline from 2.0 m/s to 4.0 m/s. p₁ − p₂ = (1000 kg/m³ / 2) · ((4.0 m/s)² − (2.0 m/s)²) = 6000 Pa = 6.0 kPa. Try it: from 1.0 m/s to 3.0 m/s, what pressure decrease occurs? (4.0 kPa).

Limits and common mistakes

The basic equation does not directly handle strong viscosity, pumps, shocks or compressible high-speed flows. Faster flow does not mean lower pressure everywhere irrespective of geometry. For wing lift, avoid the myth that upper and lower air parcels must meet at the trailing edge.

Related theories

Fluid Flow and the Continuity Equation (#55); Hydrostatic Pressure and Communicating Vessels (#51); Conservation of Mechanical Energy (#48)

57. Main Ideas of Molecular-Kinetic Theory

Origin

The particle view developed through the work of Democritus, Dalton, Clausius, Maxwell and Boltzmann. Nineteenth-century kinetic theory became quantitative, while twentieth-century experiments provided strong evidence for atomic structure.

Core idea

Particles are separated by distances and are in constant motion. Their collisions and interactions shape gas pressure and diffusion, while competing attraction and repulsion explain many properties of liquids and solids. The microscopic picture gives testable predictions rather than relying only on how matter appears to the eye.

Key terms and formulas

Three core statements: matter contains discrete particles; particles move in perpetual thermal motion; particles interact via attractive and repulsive forces depending on separation. For a rough particle count N = νN_A, where ν is amount in mol and N_A ≈ 6.022 · 10²³ mol⁻¹. The speed distribution and quantitative gas relations are studied separately.

How to apply it

Identify a macroscopic observation such as smell spreading, gas compression or a crystal resisting deformation. Explain it in terms of particles, their motion and interactions. Distinguish random molecular motion from bulk motion of the whole substance.

Worked example

A perfume molecule travels unpredictably as it collides with surrounding air particles, so its concentration gradually spreads through a room. A sample containing 0.010 mol of molecules has N = 0.010 mol · 6.022 · 10²³ mol⁻¹ ≈ 6.02 · 10²¹ molecules. Try it: how many molecules in 0.020 mol? (1.20 · 10²²).

Limits and common mistakes

The classical particle model is incomplete for quantum-scale behaviour and some strongly interacting systems. Molecules do not all move at one speed. Do not explain diffusion as air currents alone or confuse thermal molecular motion with the motion of an entire object.

Related theories

Amount of Substance and the Avogadro Constant (#58); Brownian Motion and Diffusion (#59); Ideal Gas and the Basic Equation of Molecular-Kinetic Theory (#60)

58. Amount of Substance and the Avogadro Constant

Origin

The mole concept arose from nineteenth-century chemistry and gas studies linked to Avogadro. Since the 2019 SI revision, the Avogadro constant is fixed exactly, making the mole defined by a precise number of entities.

Core idea

Mass alone does not tell you how many particles a sample contains, because different particles have different masses. Molar mass converts mass to moles; the Avogadro constant converts moles to particle count. Relative molecular mass is the sum of atomic relative masses in the chemical formula.

Key terms and formulas

ν = m/M = N/N_A. Here ν is amount in mol, m mass in kg or g, M molar mass in matching kg/mol or g/mol, N particle count and N_A = 6.02214076 · 10²³ mol⁻¹ exactly. The mass of a single particle is m₀ = M/N_A when M is in kg/mol. For H₂O, M ≈ 18 g/mol.

How to apply it

Write the chemical formula and calculate molar mass from atomic masses. Convert the sample mass to units matching M. Divide to find moles, then multiply by N_A for the particle number. State explicitly whether you count molecules or atoms.

Worked example

A sample of water has mass 36 g and M ≈ 18 g/mol. ν = 36 g / (18 g/mol) = 2.0 mol. N ≈ 2.0 mol · 6.022 · 10²³ mol⁻¹ = 1.20 · 10²⁴ water molecules. Try it: how many moles are in 9 g of water? (0.50 mol).

Limits and common mistakes

The approximation 18 g/mol is suitable for school calculations, but precise molar mass depends on isotopic composition. A mole is not a mass unit. Do not mix grams with kg/mol, and do not confuse number of molecules with number of atoms in polyatomic molecules.

Related theories

Main Ideas of Molecular-Kinetic Theory (#57); Ideal Gas and the Basic Equation of Molecular-Kinetic Theory (#60); Temperature and Thermal Equilibrium (#61)

59. Brownian Motion and Diffusion

Origin

Robert Brown described the irregular movement of pollen-related particles in water in 1827. Einstein gave a quantitative explanation in 1905, and Jean Perrin’s experiments strengthened evidence for atoms and molecules.

Core idea

Surrounding molecules strike a microscopic suspended particle unevenly, producing a random zigzag path: Brownian motion. Diffusion instead describes the statistical redistribution of many particles. Both arise from thermal motion. Higher temperature generally increases molecular motion, while diffusion through solids is much slower than through liquids or gases.

Key terms and formulas

For simple one-dimensional diffusion, the mean-square displacement is ⟨x²⟩ = 2Dt, where D is diffusion coefficient in m²/s, t time in s and ⟨x²⟩ mean-square displacement in m². A characteristic spread is x_rms = √(2Dt). This relation describes an ideal diffusive regime, not one molecule following a predictable straight path.

How to apply it

Distinguish an individual suspended particle’s jitter from the collective concentration change. For a diffusion estimate identify D and duration, then find the root-mean-square spread. Interpret the result statistically, not as a guaranteed position.

Worked example

A tracer diffuses in one dimension with D = 2.0 · 10⁻⁹ m²/s for t = 10 s. x_rms = √(2 · 2.0 · 10⁻⁹ m²/s · 10 s) = √(4.0 · 10⁻⁸ m²) = 2.0 · 10⁻⁴ m = 0.20 mm. Try it: after 40 s, what is x_rms? (0.40 mm).

Limits and common mistakes

The simple model assumes ordinary diffusion in a uniform medium; bulk flow, boundaries and variable diffusion coefficients alter spreading. Do not confuse Brownian particles with individual molecules or suppose diffusion always proceeds in only one direction.

Related theories

Main Ideas of Molecular-Kinetic Theory (#57); Amount of Substance and the Avogadro Constant (#58); Ideal Gas and the Basic Equation of Molecular-Kinetic Theory (#60)

60. Ideal Gas and the Basic Equation of Molecular-Kinetic Theory

Origin

Daniel Bernoulli proposed a kinetic picture of gases in the eighteenth century. Clausius, Maxwell and Boltzmann made molecular-kinetic theory quantitative during the nineteenth century.

Core idea

Gas molecules move in all directions. Their collisions with walls produce pressure; greater average molecular kinetic energy means larger momentum transfer. The ideal-gas model ignores molecular size compared with the container and forces between molecules except during collisions. Random motion is characterised by a distribution of speeds, not one common speed.

Key terms and formulas

p = (1/3) n m₀ v_rms² = (2/3)n Ēₖ, where p is pressure in Pa, n = N/V is particle concentration in m⁻³, m₀ mass of one molecule in kg, v_rms root-mean-square speed in m/s and Ēₖ mean translational kinetic energy per molecule in J. Here v_rms² = ⟨v²⟩ and Ēₖ = m₀⟨v²⟩/2.

How to apply it

Check that the problem concerns a dilute gas well described as ideal. Calculate concentration N/V, not amount in moles per volume unless converted with N_A. Choose the mean-square speed, not ordinary arithmetic mean speed. Substitute quantities in SI units.

Worked example

A gas has n = 2.0 · 10²⁵ m⁻³, molecular mass m₀ = 4.0 · 10⁻²⁶ kg, and v_rms = 500 m/s. p = (1/3) · 2.0 · 10²⁵ m⁻³ · 4.0 · 10⁻²⁶ kg · (500 m/s)² ≈ 6.67 · 10⁴ Pa = 66.7 kPa. Try it: if v_rms doubles at fixed n, how does pressure change? (It quadruples).

Limits and common mistakes

The ideal model deviates for dense gases, low temperatures and near condensation, where molecular interactions matter. Never use the average of speeds as v_rms without conversion. The kinetic equation concerns translational motion; molecular rotations and vibrations require separate treatment.

Related theories

Main Ideas of Molecular-Kinetic Theory (#57); Amount of Substance and the Avogadro Constant (#58); Temperature and the Kinetic Energy of Molecules (#62)

61. Temperature and Thermal Equilibrium

Origin

Early thermometers were developed during the seventeenth century. Fahrenheit and Celsius established practical scales in the eighteenth century; Kelvin introduced an absolute temperature scale in the nineteenth century. The zeroth law of thermodynamics later formalised the transitive property of thermal equilibrium.

Core idea

Two bodies in thermal contact exchange energy until neither has a net tendency to heat the other. A thermometer measures its own temperature after equilibrating with an object, so its reading represents the object's temperature only if contact and calibration are suitable. The Kelvin scale starts at absolute zero, the limiting temperature of lowest thermal energy, rather than at the freezing point of water.

Key terms and formulas

T = t + 273.15, where T is thermodynamic temperature in kelvin (K) and t is Celsius temperature in degrees Celsius (°C). Temperature differences have equal numerical values in K and °C: ΔT = Δt. Fahrenheit conversion is t_F = 1.8t + 32, with t_F in °F. Absolute zero is 0 K = −273.15 °C; ordinary laboratory temperatures are about 290–300 K.

How to apply it

Identify whether the problem asks for a temperature or a temperature difference. Convert Celsius to kelvin before using a gas-law or molecular-energy formula. For temperature differences, retain the same numerical interval in degrees Celsius or kelvin. If a thermometer is moved between objects, allow it to settle before recording a result.

Worked example

A classroom thermometer reads 22 °C. Convert using T = 22 °C + 273.15 K = 295.15 K, usually 295 K. At the same temperature Fahrenheit reads t_F = 1.8 · 22 + 32 = 71.6 °F. Try it: convert −3 °C to kelvin. (270.15 K).

Limits and common mistakes

Do not treat temperature as an amount of heat or confuse a hot spark with a large store of thermal energy. The relation T = t + 273 is a rounded classroom approximation; use 273.15 for precise work. Real thermometers need calibration, and a rapidly changing object may not be in equilibrium with the sensor.

Related theories

Temperature and the Kinetic Energy of Molecules (#62); Ideal Gas Equation of State (#63); Quantity of Heat and Specific Heat Capacity (#74)

62. Temperature and the Kinetic Energy of Molecules

Origin

In the nineteenth century Clausius, Maxwell and Boltzmann developed kinetic theory connecting molecular motion with measurable pressure and temperature. The modern kelvin is defined using a fixed Boltzmann constant.

Core idea

Gas pressure results from molecular collisions with a container. Increasing temperature raises typical particle energies and, at fixed number density, raises pressure. For translational motion an ideal gas molecule has three independent degrees of freedom, each contributing kT / 2 to its mean kinetic energy. Equal temperature means equal mean translational energies, not identical speeds.

Key terms and formulas

Ēₖ = 3kT / 2 in J; k = 1.380649 · 10⁻²³ J/K; T in K. p = nkT, where p is pressure in Pa and n = N/V is number concentration in m⁻³. v_rms = √(3kT/m₀) = √(3RT/M), where m₀ is mass per molecule in kg, M molar mass in kg/mol, R ≈ 8.314 J/(mol·K), and v_rms is in m/s.

How to apply it

Convert temperatures to kelvin and molar mass to kg/mol. Select the formula for molecular energy, pressure or root-mean-square speed. Use number concentration N/V rather than amount of substance per litre. Check that energy grows linearly with temperature and molecular speed grows as its square root.

Worked example

At T = 300 K, Ēₖ = 1.5 · 1.380649 · 10⁻²³ J/K · 300 K = 6.21 · 10⁻²¹ J. Doubling T to 600 K doubles Ēₖ to 1.24 · 10⁻²⁰ J. Try it: what is Ēₖ at 200 K? (4.14 · 10⁻²¹ J).

Limits and common mistakes

These expressions concern ideal-gas translational motion. Molecules in real gases interact, and rotational or vibrational energy also contributes to total internal energy. Do not use Celsius in kT, treat rms speed as the speed of every molecule, or confuse concentration n with amount of substance ν.

Related theories

Ideal Gas and the Basic Equation of Molecular-Kinetic Theory (#60); Temperature and Thermal Equilibrium (#61); Ideal Gas Equation of State (#63)

63. Ideal Gas Equation of State

Origin

The relationship combines experimental gas laws developed by Boyle, Charles and Gay-Lussac. Clapeyron expressed a combined equation in the nineteenth century, and Mendeleev helped establish its molar form.

Core idea

An ideal gas consists of particles with negligible volume that do not exert long-range forces on one another. At a fixed amount of gas, compressing it raises pressure unless temperature falls. Heating at fixed pressure increases volume. The equation is a state relation: it compares equilibrium states, not the details of how a change happens.

Key terms and formulas

pV = νRT = (m/M)RT, with p in Pa, V in m³, ν in mol, m in kg, M in kg/mol, R = 8.314462618… J/(mol·K), and T in K. For a fixed amount p₁V₁/T₁ = p₂V₂/T₂. Density ρ = m/V = pM/(RT), in kg/m³. Standard reference conditions must specify both temperature and pressure.

How to apply it

Make a list of known state variables and convert litres to m³, kilopascals to pascals and Celsius to kelvin. If mass is supplied, calculate ν = m/M before solving. Check whether the mass of gas stays constant; a leaking vessel requires a different approach.

Worked example

At p = 100000 Pa and T = 300 K, one mole has V = νRT/p = 1.00 mol · 8.314 J/(mol·K) · 300 K / 100000 Pa = 0.0249 m³ = 24.9 L. Try it: what volume does 2.00 mol occupy at the same p and T? (49.9 L).

Limits and common mistakes

The ideal gas approximation works best at low density and away from condensation. A real-gas model is needed near phase changes and at high pressure. Do not insert Celsius temperatures, use litres with SI pressure without conversion, or assume a vessel contains the same gas mass after venting.

Related theories

Ideal Gas and the Basic Equation of Molecular-Kinetic Theory (#60); Temperature and Thermal Equilibrium (#61); Isoprocesses in Gases (#64)

64. Isoprocesses in Gases

Origin

Boyle and Mariotte investigated pressure and volume in the seventeenth century. Charles and Gay-Lussac established temperature relationships around the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries. The names of temperature laws differ between some textbook traditions.

Core idea

An isothermal process keeps T unchanged, so p and V vary inversely. An isobaric process holds p fixed, so V/T stays unchanged. An isochoric process holds V fixed, so p/T stays unchanged. Their graphs are respectively a hyperbola on p–V axes, a straight line on V–T axes, and a straight line on p–T axes when temperature is in kelvin.

Key terms and formulas

Isothermal: p₁V₁ = p₂V₂. Isobaric: V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂. Isochoric: p₁/T₁ = p₂/T₂. Here p is in Pa, V in m³ and T in K. All three follow from pV = νRT when the amount of ideal gas ν remains fixed.

How to apply it

Read the task for the quantity explicitly kept constant. Sketch the axes and identify the two end states. Convert temperatures to kelvin, use the correct two-state ratio, then verify the physical direction: compression at fixed T raises p, and heating at fixed V raises p.

Worked example

A gas initially occupies 3.0 L at 100 kPa. It is compressed isothermally to 1.5 L. p₂ = p₁V₁/V₂ = 100 kPa · 3.0 L / 1.5 L = 200 kPa. Try it: at constant pressure, what volume follows heating 2.0 L from 300 K to 450 K? (3.0 L).

Limits and common mistakes

These equalities presume a fixed amount of ideal gas in equilibrium. Rapid compression is not automatically isothermal, since it may warm the gas. Do not replace Kelvin temperature ratios with Celsius ratios, or confuse a straight graph in T with one in t without checking the intercept.

Related theories

Temperature and Thermal Equilibrium (#61); Ideal Gas Equation of State (#63); Work Done by a Gas (#78)

65. Evaporation, Boiling and Saturated Vapor

Origin

The molecular understanding of phase changes developed alongside nineteenth-century thermodynamics. Careful measurements of water's vapour pressure showed why boiling temperature depends on external pressure rather than being a fixed universal number.

Core idea

Fast molecules can escape a liquid's surface, removing energy and cooling the remaining liquid. In a closed vessel evaporation and condensation eventually balance, giving saturated vapour. Its equilibrium pressure depends primarily on temperature as long as some liquid remains. Bubbles can grow in the bulk liquid when saturated vapour pressure matches or exceeds pressure at their location.

Key terms and formulas

Boiling condition: p_sat(T_b) ≈ p_external at the liquid surface, with hydrostatic corrections at depth; both pressures are in Pa. Relative humidity compares actual water-vapour pressure with p_sat at the same temperature. The latent heat for vaporisation is treated separately by Q = Lm, with L in J/kg and m in kg.

How to apply it

First identify whether the question concerns surface evaporation, equilibrium vapour or bulk boiling. Compare surrounding pressure with the saturation pressure at the given temperature. Do not infer boiling solely from visible mist, which often consists of condensed droplets above hot liquid.

Worked example

Near sea level water boils at about 100 °C when external pressure is 101 kPa. In a pressure cooker near 200 kPa absolute pressure, the boiling point is about 120 °C. Thus higher pressure raises the boiling temperature by about 20 °C in this example. Try it: does water boil above or below 100 °C at 80 kPa? (Below 100 °C).

Limits and common mistakes

Real boiling depends on impurities, nucleation sites and pressure within the liquid. Water vapour itself is invisible; a white cloud over a kettle is mainly tiny liquid droplets. Never open a hot pressure vessel or place your face above escaping steam: steam can cause severe burns.

Related theories

Air Humidity (#66); Melting, Vaporization and Latent Heat (#75); Atmospheric Pressure (#53)

66. Air Humidity

Origin

Humidity measurement improved as meteorology and thermodynamics developed during the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. Hygrometers and paired wet-bulb and dry-bulb thermometers became practical instruments for weather observations and indoor climate control.

Core idea

Air contains water molecules mixed with other gases. Relative humidity compares the water vapour's partial pressure with the equilibrium saturation pressure. If air cools to the dew point, the existing water-vapour pressure reaches saturation and condensation may begin. Absolute humidity instead reports the mass of water vapour per volume of air.

Key terms and formulas

φ = p_v / p_sat(T) · 100%, where φ is relative humidity in percent, p_v actual water-vapour partial pressure in Pa and p_sat(T) saturation pressure in Pa. Absolute humidity ρ_v = m_v / V is in kg/m³ or g/m³. The dew point is the temperature at which p_sat equals the existing p_v, assuming no change in vapour amount.

How to apply it

Read the saturation pressure from a table at the air temperature. Divide actual vapour partial pressure by that value and multiply by 100%. For dew-point questions keep actual vapour pressure fixed while identifying the temperature at which saturation occurs. State whether the data use pressure or density rather than mixing their ratios without justification.

Worked example

Air at 20 °C has actual water-vapour pressure p_v = 1.17 kPa; saturation pressure is 2.34 kPa. φ = 1.17 kPa / 2.34 kPa · 100% = 50%. Try it: if p_v = 1.87 kPa at the same temperature, what is φ? (About 80%).

Limits and common mistakes

Relative humidity is not the percentage of air made of water. A value of 100% means saturation, not that air is liquid water. Condensation, ventilation and temperature changes can alter readings; practical comfort often lies around 30–60% relative humidity, depending on conditions.

Related theories

Temperature and Thermal Equilibrium (#61); Evaporation, Boiling and Saturated Vapor (#65); Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation (#73)

67. Surface Tension

Origin

The study of capillarity and liquid interfaces advanced through the work of Young and Laplace in the early nineteenth century. Molecular explanations later connected surface tension to differences in cohesive forces at a liquid surface.

Core idea

Molecules deep inside a liquid attract neighbours from all directions. Surface molecules have a different molecular environment, making surface area energetically costly. A liquid therefore tends to reduce exposed area when other forces allow it. A soap film has two surfaces and therefore pulls a movable wire from both sides.

Key terms and formulas

σ = F/l in N/m for one interface exerting tangential force F (N) along contact length l (m). For a film with two surfaces, F = 2σl. Surface energy increase is ΔE = σΔS, where ΔS is the increase of area of one interface in m² and energy is in J. The value of σ depends on liquid, temperature and contaminants.

How to apply it

Count the number of interfaces acting on the moving edge before choosing F = σl or F = 2σl. Measure the contact length in metres. Use the given surface tension rather than assuming every liquid has water's value. Check whether gravity or adhesion matters in the described situation.

Worked example

A soap film pulls a 0.050 m wire with σ = 0.030 N/m on each of its two surfaces. F = 2σl = 2 · 0.030 N/m · 0.050 m = 0.0030 N. Try it: what force acts on a 0.10 m wire in the same film? (0.0060 N).

Limits and common mistakes

Macroscopic effects also depend on gravity, contact angle and wetting, so surface tension alone may not determine a drop's shape. Do not forget the factor of two for soap films or treat surface tension as pressure. Avoid detergent contact with eyes during demonstrations.

Related theories

Wetting and Capillary Action (#68); Evaporation, Boiling and Saturated Vapor (#65); Mechanical Properties of Solids (#70)

68. Wetting and Capillary Action

Origin

Thomas Young introduced a quantitative description of contact angles in the early nineteenth century. Capillary-rise relationships emerged from the work of Jurin and Laplace, helping explain liquid movement through narrow spaces.

Core idea

Adhesion between liquid and solid competes with cohesion within the liquid. A small contact angle means good wetting; a contact angle above 90° means poor wetting. Surface tension along the tilted meniscus provides an upward or downward vertical component, balanced by the weight of the displaced liquid column. Narrower tubes produce larger height changes.

Key terms and formulas

For a cylindrical tube, h = 2σ cosθ/(ρgr), where h is signed rise in m, σ surface tension in N/m, θ contact angle, ρ liquid density in kg/m³, g ≈ 9.8 m/s² and r tube radius in m. For complete wetting cosθ ≈ 1, giving h ≈ 2σ/(ρgr). A negative cosine indicates capillary depression.

How to apply it

Check whether the surface is wetting and whether a contact angle is given. Convert tube radius from millimetres to metres. Insert surface tension, density and g in SI units. Predict that the rise increases as the radius decreases; inspect the sign of cosθ.

Worked example

For clean water in a glass capillary of radius r = 0.50 mm, take σ = 0.072 N/m, ρ = 1000 kg/m³, cosθ ≈ 1. Then h = 2 · 0.072 N/m / (1000 kg/m³ · 9.8 m/s² · 0.00050 m) = 0.0294 m = 2.94 cm. Try it: halve the radius. (Rise ≈ 5.88 cm).

Limits and common mistakes

The formula assumes a thin cylindrical tube, static equilibrium and a well-defined contact angle. Flowing sap in tall trees is not explained by simple capillary rise alone; transpiration and cohesion matter. Do not use tube diameter where radius is required.

Related theories

Surface Tension (#67); Hydrostatic Pressure and Communicating Vessels (#51); Archimedes' Principle and Flotation (#54)

69. Crystalline and Amorphous Solids

Origin

Crystallography developed through mineral studies and became quantitative with X-ray diffraction in the early twentieth century. Laue and the Braggs showed how diffraction reveals orderly atomic positions in crystals.

Core idea

The repeating arrangement of particles in a crystal is called a lattice. A single crystal may have different physical properties along different directions, known as anisotropy. A polycrystal consists of many small crystalline grains whose different orientations may average out these effects. Amorphous solids have local organisation but no repeating three-dimensional pattern over large distances.

Key terms and formulas

No single school-level equation distinguishes every solid, but density ρ = m/V in kg/m³ and melting temperature in K or °C help compare samples. Anisotropy may make thermal conductivity, optical response and mechanical strength direction-dependent. A sharp melting point often suggests crystallinity but is not a universal diagnostic.

How to apply it

Look for observations of crystal faces, diffraction patterns, direction-dependent behaviour and the temperature range of softening or melting. Determine whether the specimen is one grain, many grains or glassy material. State which evidence supports the classification rather than relying only on appearance.

Worked example

A 120 g glass sample occupies 50 cm³. Its density is ρ = 120 g / 50 cm³ = 2.4 g/cm³ = 2400 kg/m³. This measurement does not by itself prove amorphous structure. Try it: find density for 80 g in 40 cm³. (2.0 g/cm³).

Limits and common mistakes

Crystalline materials may contain defects and polycrystalline materials can be approximately isotropic. Some amorphous solids have a glass transition, not a single equilibrium melting temperature. Do not label an object crystalline merely because it is hard or transparent, and do not infer internal ordering from density alone.

Related theories

Mechanical Properties of Solids (#70); Thermal Expansion (#71); Surface Tension (#67)

70. Mechanical Properties of Solids

Origin

Robert Hooke studied elastic deformation in the seventeenth century, while Thomas Young helped quantify the relationship between force and elongation in the nineteenth century. Modern materials science extends these ideas to complex materials and failure modes.

Core idea

Tension, compression, shear, bending and torsion are different kinds of deformation. Normal stress describes force distributed over area; strain measures relative change in length. Young's modulus E indicates how resistant a material is to elastic stretching: a larger E means less strain under the same stress. Strength is the load at which material fails, not the same as stiffness.

Key terms and formulas

Normal stress σ = F/S in Pa, where force F is in N and area S in m². Longitudinal strain ε = Δl/l₀ is dimensionless. For small elastic deformation σ = Eε, where E is Young's modulus in Pa, giving Δl = Fl₀/(ES). These formulas assume uniaxial loading, uniform section and linear elastic behaviour.

How to apply it

Draw the loading direction and the cross-section perpendicular to force. Convert mm² to m²; calculate stress and then strain using E. Multiply by the original length to obtain extension. Check that the resulting strain is small enough for the elastic model.

Worked example

A 2.0 m steel wire has area S = 1.0 mm² = 1.0 · 10⁻⁶ m². A force 100 N acts along it; take E = 2.0 · 10¹¹ Pa. Δl = 100 N · 2.0 m / (2.0 · 10¹¹ Pa · 1.0 · 10⁻⁶ m²) = 0.0010 m = 1.0 mm. Try it: double the force within the elastic range. (2.0 mm).

Limits and common mistakes

Hooke's law fails after the proportional limit and is unsuitable for large deformation, many polymers or materials undergoing creep. Brittle materials may fracture with little plastic deformation; ductile ones deform more. Do not confuse stress in pascals with force in newtons or stiffness with breaking strength.

Related theories

Elastic Force and Hooke's Law (#28); Crystalline and Amorphous Solids (#69); Moment of Force and Conditions of Equilibrium (#36)

71. Thermal Expansion

Origin

The practical effects of thermal expansion were known to craftspeople for centuries. Quantitative measurements developed during the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries and became important for bridges, precision instruments and industrial machinery.

Core idea

Heating normally increases the length and volume of solids and liquids. A material's expansion coefficient describes the fractional change per degree. In an isotropic solid, small changes of volume are about three times the linear fractional change. Water behaves unusually between 0 °C and about 4 °C: heating it toward 4 °C increases its density, while further heating decreases density.

Key terms and formulas

Linear expansion: Δl = αl₀ΔT, with Δl and initial length l₀ in m, α in K⁻¹, and temperature change ΔT in K. Final length l = l₀ + Δl. Volume expansion: ΔV = βV₀ΔT, β ≈ 3α for isotropic solids over small temperature ranges. A change of 1 K equals a change of 1 °C.

How to apply it

Determine the original temperature and the change rather than inserting the final temperature alone. Look up the appropriate expansion coefficient and convert lengths to SI. Multiply α by initial length and temperature change. For cooling the temperature change is negative and the material contracts if α is positive.

Worked example

A steel rail is 10.0 m long; α = 12 · 10⁻⁶ K⁻¹ and ΔT = 40 K. Δl = 12 · 10⁻⁶ K⁻¹ · 10.0 m · 40 K = 0.0048 m = 4.8 mm. Try it: what is the extension if ΔT = 20 K? (2.4 mm).

Limits and common mistakes

Coefficients depend on temperature, and expansion may differ by direction in anisotropic crystals. Constrained expansion creates stress rather than free lengthening. Do not use absolute temperature instead of a difference or assume all liquids behave like water near 4 °C.

Related theories

Temperature and Thermal Equilibrium (#61); Crystalline and Amorphous Solids (#69); Mechanical Properties of Solids (#70)

72. Internal Energy and Ways to Change It

Origin

The concept of internal energy emerged with nineteenth-century studies of heat and work by Joule and other researchers. Thermodynamics established energy conservation without needing to track each microscopic particle separately.

Core idea

A hot object's particles have microscopic kinetic and potential energies. Internal energy excludes the overall kinetic energy of the whole object and its gravitational potential energy as a whole. Heat transfer changes internal energy through a temperature difference; work changes it through macroscopic forces, such as compression. For an ideal monatomic gas the interaction potential energy is neglected.

Key terms and formulas

For an ideal monatomic gas, U = 3νRT/2, where U is in J, ν is amount of substance in mol, R ≈ 8.314 J/(mol·K) and T is in K. Hence ΔU = 3νRΔT/2 for fixed ν. For other substances, U depends on additional properties and molecular degrees of freedom, so the monatomic formula is not universal.

How to apply it

Classify the system and the kind of energy involved. If it is a monatomic ideal gas with known ν and T, use U = 3νRT/2; for a temperature change use ΔU. If the question includes heat and work, track their signs using the first law rather than equating heat directly with internal energy.

Worked example

For ν = 1.00 mol of monatomic ideal gas at T = 300 K, U = 1.5 · 1.00 mol · 8.314 J/(mol·K) · 300 K = 3741 J ≈ 3.74 kJ. Try it: what is U for the same gas at 400 K? (4.99 kJ).

Limits and common mistakes

Not every substance follows the monatomic ideal-gas expression. A moving object may have large mechanical kinetic energy without changing its internal energy. Do not treat heat as something permanently stored in a body or confuse total U with its change ΔU.

Related theories

Temperature and the Kinetic Energy of Molecules (#62); First Law of Thermodynamics (#79); Quantity of Heat and Specific Heat Capacity (#74)

73. Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation

Origin

Fourier formulated quantitative heat conduction in the early nineteenth century. Studies of fluid circulation and electromagnetic radiation later clarified the other two mechanisms. The three processes are often present simultaneously in real buildings and devices.

Core idea

Conduction moves energy through microscopic particle interactions without bulk movement of material. Convection transports energy with moving liquids or gases. Radiation uses electromagnetic waves and can travel through vacuum. Metal feels colder than wood at the same room temperature because it transfers energy away from your hand faster, not because it must have a lower temperature.

Key terms and formulas

For steady one-dimensional conduction through a uniform slab, P = k_th SΔT/d, where heat-transfer power P is in W, conductivity k_th in W/(m·K), cross-section S in m², temperature difference ΔT in K and thickness d in m. This simplified formula presumes fixed surface temperatures and negligible edge effects.

How to apply it

Identify the medium and motion: bulk fluid motion suggests convection, transfer through a still solid suggests conduction, and transfer across vacuum requires radiation. For a slab use thickness perpendicular to heat flow and conductivity of the given material. Examine insulation as a way to increase thermal resistance rather than to destroy energy.

Worked example

A wall has area S = 10 m², thickness d = 0.20 m, conductivity k_th = 0.040 W/(m·K) and ΔT = 20 K. P = 0.040 W/(m·K) · 10 m² · 20 K / 0.20 m = 40 W. Try it: double the thickness. (20 W).

Limits and common mistakes

Convection varies with flow and geometry; radiation depends strongly on surface temperature and emissivity. The slab formula omits these contributions and thermal bridges. Do not claim a vacuum prevents radiation or that shiny foil blocks all heat transfer.

Related theories

Internal Energy and Ways to Change It (#72); Quantity of Heat and Specific Heat Capacity (#74); Thermal Expansion (#71)

74. Quantity of Heat and Specific Heat Capacity

Origin

James Prescott Joule's nineteenth-century experiments established the equivalence of mechanical work and heat. Calorimetry gradually produced tables of specific heat capacities for different materials and temperatures.

Core idea

The amount of heat transferred changes the internal energy of a substance and often its temperature. Specific heat capacity depends on the material and its state; heat capacity of a whole object also depends on its mass. During melting or boiling, heat can change the phase without causing a temperature rise, so a different equation is needed.

Key terms and formulas

Q = cmΔT, where Q is transferred heat in J, c specific heat capacity in J/(kg·K), m mass in kg, and ΔT in K or numerical degrees Celsius. Heat capacity C = cm is in J/K and gives Q = CΔT. For liquid water near room temperature c ≈ 4200 J/(kg·K). A negative Q indicates energy lost under the chosen convention.

How to apply it

Check whether the substance remains in one phase. Convert mass and temperature differences to SI. Find the correct c for the temperature range, then multiply c, mass and temperature change. For several bodies use a heat-balance equation rather than applying one common c.

Worked example

Heat 2.0 kg of water from 20 °C to 30 °C. ΔT = 10 K, so Q = 4200 J/(kg·K) · 2.0 kg · 10 K = 84000 J = 84 kJ. Try it: how much heat warms 0.50 kg of water by 5 °C? (10.5 kJ).

Limits and common mistakes

Specific heat can vary with temperature and phase. The formula excludes latent heat and energy lost to the surroundings; it is an approximation for large temperature ranges. Do not use degrees Celsius as an absolute temperature in gas equations or confuse specific heat c with total heat capacity C.

Related theories

Internal Energy and Ways to Change It (#72); Melting, Vaporization and Latent Heat (#75); Heat Balance Equation (#77)

75. Melting, Vaporization and Latent Heat

Origin

Nineteenth-century calorimetry clarified latent heat and phase changes, building on earlier work by Joseph Black in the eighteenth century. Thermodynamics later explained latent heat through energy and equilibrium between phases.

Core idea

Melting turns solid into liquid, and crystallisation reverses it. Vaporisation turns liquid into vapour, and condensation reverses it. At fixed pressure, a pure material can change phase at almost constant temperature while it absorbs or releases latent heat. A heating curve therefore has sloping segments for temperature rise and flat segments for phase transitions.

Key terms and formulas

Melting or freezing: Q = λm, where λ is specific latent heat of fusion in J/kg and m in kg. Vaporisation or condensation: Q = Lm, also written Q = rm in Ukrainian notation, where L or r is specific latent heat of vaporisation in J/kg. For ice near 0 °C, λ ≈ 3.34 · 10⁵ J/kg; for water boiling near 100 °C, L ≈ 2.26 · 10⁶ J/kg.

How to apply it

Break a multistage process into separate segments: warm solid, melt, warm liquid, vaporise, warm vapour. Apply Q = cmΔT for temperature changes and Q = λm or Lm for phase changes. Add the energy magnitudes, using signs if the process cools or condenses.

Worked example

To melt 0.50 kg of ice already at 0 °C, use λ = 3.34 · 10⁵ J/kg. Q = λm = 3.34 · 10⁵ J/kg · 0.50 kg = 1.67 · 10⁵ J = 167 kJ. Try it: how much heat melts 0.20 kg of ice at 0 °C? (66.8 kJ).

Limits and common mistakes

Latent heats vary with pressure and temperature, and phase changes may occur over ranges in mixtures. Do not apply Q = cmΔT to a constant-temperature melting interval or assume all absorbed energy raises temperature. Beware of hot steam, which releases substantial heat on condensation.

Related theories

Evaporation, Boiling and Saturated Vapor (#65); Quantity of Heat and Specific Heat Capacity (#74); Heat Balance Equation (#77)

76. Fuel Combustion and Heat of Combustion

Origin

The energy content of fuels became measurable through nineteenth-century thermochemistry and calorimetry. Engineering studies of steam engines and later internal-combustion systems motivated practical definitions of fuel heating values and efficiency.

Core idea

Combustion is a chemical reaction with an oxidiser, often oxygen, forming products and releasing energy. Fuels differ greatly in heating value per mass and per volume. Useful heat depends on appliance efficiency and losses to exhaust gases, surrounding air and incomplete combustion. Food also stores chemical energy, but its nutritional energy must be interpreted through metabolism rather than direct burning in a heater.

Key terms and formulas

Q_release = qm, where Q_release is in J, q is specific heat of combustion in J/kg, and m is fuel mass in kg. Useful energy Q_useful = ηqm, where η is dimensionless efficiency between 0 and 1. When a heater warms water, Q_useful ≈ cmΔT if the water remains liquid and other losses are represented by η.

How to apply it

Identify whether the given q is a higher or lower heating value if precision matters. Convert grams to kilograms and MJ/kg to J/kg. Compute released energy before applying efficiency. Check that useful energy cannot exceed released energy for an ordinary heater.

Worked example

A stove burns 0.50 kg of fuel with q = 40 MJ/kg. The energy released is Q = 40 MJ/kg · 0.50 kg = 20 MJ. At η = 0.60, useful heat is 0.60 · 20 MJ = 12 MJ. Try it: with η = 0.75, how much useful heat results? (15 MJ).

Limits and common mistakes

Actual energy release depends on moisture, oxygen availability and the chosen heating-value convention. Combustion produces pollutants and can produce deadly carbon monoxide if ventilation is poor. Never burn fuel indoors without suitable certified ventilation and safety equipment.

Related theories

Power and Efficiency (#45); Quantity of Heat and Specific Heat Capacity (#74); Heat Engines and Their Efficiency (#81)

77. Heat Balance Equation

Origin

Calorimetry developed through experiments on heat exchange and heat capacities from the eighteenth century onward. The energy-conservation principle made it possible to calculate equilibrium temperatures without assuming that heat is a material fluid.

Core idea

Bodies at different temperatures exchange energy until thermal equilibrium. An isolated mixture has a total energy change of zero, so positive energy gains balance negative losses. A calorimeter reduces exchanges with the surroundings, but real calorimeters also absorb some energy. If ice is present, melting may consume energy while temperature stays near 0 °C.

Key terms and formulas

ΣQᵢ = 0 for an isolated set of bodies, with signed heat transfers Qᵢ in J. Without phase changes Qᵢ = mᵢcᵢ(T_f − Tᵢ), where T_f is common final temperature. During ice melting add Q_melt = λm, with λ in J/kg. The calorimeter contribution can be C_cal(T_f − T_cal,initial), with C_cal in J/K.

How to apply it

List all participating bodies, their initial temperatures and whether a phase transition occurs. Write each Q term with its proper sign, include the calorimeter if needed, and solve for one shared equilibrium temperature. Check that the answer lies between the initial temperatures when no phase change or external work intervenes.

Worked example

Mix 0.20 kg of water at 20 °C with 0.30 kg at 60 °C and ignore the vessel. Because c is the same, 0.20 kg · (T_f − 20 °C) + 0.30 kg · (T_f − 60 °C) = 0; T_f = (0.20 · 20 + 0.30 · 60)/0.50 = 44 °C. Try it: equal masses at 20 °C and 60 °C? (40 °C).

Limits and common mistakes

Heat loss, evaporation and the container's heat capacity cause deviations. Ice may only partly melt if there is insufficient incoming energy, so do not assume the entire sample changes phase. Never add heat magnitudes without assigning loss and gain correctly.

Related theories

Quantity of Heat and Specific Heat Capacity (#74); Melting, Vaporization and Latent Heat (#75); First Law of Thermodynamics (#79)

78. Work Done by a Gas

Origin

The nineteenth-century development of steam engines motivated the mathematical description of pressure–volume work. Thermodynamics established work as an energy transfer rather than a form of energy stored inside a substance.

Core idea

Gas particles collide with a moving piston and exert force on it. During expansion the piston moves in the direction of the force, transferring energy from the gas to its surroundings. On a p–V graph, the area under the path gives work for a quasistatic change. Different paths between the same states can produce different amounts of work.

Key terms and formulas

At constant pressure A = pΔV = p(V₂ − V₁), with A in J, p in Pa and V in m³. More generally for quasistatic work A = ∫p dV, the signed area under the p–V path. With work by gas positive, A > 0 for expansion and A < 0 for compression; work done on the gas is −A.

How to apply it

Sketch the p–V graph and identify expansion or compression. Convert kPa to Pa and litres to m³. If pressure is constant, multiply it by the signed volume change. If pressure varies, calculate the area using the graph or a given pressure law rather than blindly applying pΔV with an arbitrary p.

Worked example

A cylinder contains gas at p = 100 kPa = 100000 Pa. The volume rises from 0.020 m³ to 0.030 m³. ΔV = 0.010 m³, so A = 100000 Pa · 0.010 m³ = 1000 J. Try it: what is the work if the same gas is compressed through 0.005 m³ at that pressure? (−500 J).

Limits and common mistakes

The area formula assumes pressure is well defined along the process. In a rapid irreversible expansion the gas pressure may not match external pressure; external-pressure work must then be handled carefully. Do not confuse work by the gas with work on the gas or forget to convert litres.

Related theories

Ideal Gas Equation of State (#63); First Law of Thermodynamics (#79); First Law for Isoprocesses and the Adiabatic Process (#80)

79. First Law of Thermodynamics

Origin

Joule's measurements of the mechanical equivalent of heat and the work of Mayer and Helmholtz in the nineteenth century helped establish the first law. It unified thermal and mechanical energy changes within one conservation principle.

Core idea

Internal energy U is a state function: its change depends on initial and final states. Heat Q and work A are transfers of energy during a process and depend on the path. In the convention used here, heat entering the system is positive and work done by the system is positive, so some incoming energy leaves as work while the remainder changes internal energy.

Key terms and formulas

Q = ΔU + A, equivalently ΔU = Q − A, with all three terms in J. Here Q > 0 means heat absorbed, A > 0 means work done by the system, and ΔU > 0 means internal energy increased. If work is defined as work done on the system instead, the equation takes the form ΔU = Q + W_on.

How to apply it

Draw the system boundary and identify each transfer across it. Write the sign convention before calculations. Assign positive Q for heat supplied and positive A for expansion work done by the gas. Solve algebraically, then state whether internal energy rose or fell. Compare input and output energies as a conservation check.

Worked example

A gas receives Q = +500 J and expands, doing A = +200 J. ΔU = Q − A = 500 J − 200 J = +300 J. The gas stores 300 J more internal energy. Try it: if Q = 100 J and A = 150 J, find ΔU. (−50 J).

Limits and common mistakes

The simple form assumes a closed system with only the stated heat and work transfers; matter flow requires additional terms. A first-kind perpetual-motion machine would violate energy conservation. Do not switch signs midway or treat heat and internal energy as interchangeable quantities.

Related theories

Internal Energy and Ways to Change It (#72); Work Done by a Gas (#78); First Law for Isoprocesses and the Adiabatic Process (#80)

80. First Law for Isoprocesses and the Adiabatic Process

Origin

Nineteenth-century thermodynamics analysed idealised isothermal, isobaric, isochoric and adiabatic changes while steam-engine technology advanced. Poisson's relations describe reversible adiabatic changes of ideal gases under additional assumptions.

Core idea

For an isochoric change ΔV = 0, so supplied heat changes internal energy. For an isothermal ideal-gas change ΔT = 0, so ΔU = 0 and heat absorbed equals work done. Under isobaric heating, the gas both warms and expands. During adiabatic compression Q = 0 and negative work by the gas increases its internal energy; adiabatic expansion cools it.

Key terms and formulas

General law Q = ΔU + A in J. Isochoric: A = 0, Q = ΔU. Isothermal ideal gas: ΔU = 0, Q = A. Isobaric: A = pΔV, Q = ΔU + pΔV. Adiabatic: Q = 0, ΔU = −A. For a reversible adiabatic ideal gas with constant heat capacities, pV^γ = const, where γ = C_p/C_v is dimensionless.

How to apply it

Identify which quantity stays constant or whether heat transfer is negligible. Sketch p–V behaviour and choose the matching first-law simplification. In adiabatic problems assign signs carefully: compression means ΔV < 0 and A < 0. If calculating temperatures, include the gas's heat capacity or amount of substance.

Worked example

An ideal gas undergoes adiabatic compression. The surroundings do 250 J of work on it, so the work by the gas is A = −250 J and Q = 0 J. ΔU = Q − A = 0 J − (−250 J) = +250 J. Try it: during adiabatic expansion the gas does 100 J of work. What is ΔU? (−100 J).

Limits and common mistakes

Adiabatic does not mean temperature is constant. Real processes may exchange heat, and γ is not constant across all temperatures. Isochoric, isothermal and isobaric assumptions must be explicitly justified rather than chosen for convenience. Rapid compression can heat pumps and cylinders, so avoid touching hot surfaces.

Related theories

Isoprocesses in Gases (#64); Work Done by a Gas (#78); First Law of Thermodynamics (#79)

81. Heat Engines and Their Efficiency

Origin

In the nineteenth century, engineers studied steam engines to reduce fuel consumption. Carnot analysed ideal engines in 1824; later thermodynamics explained the role of heat reservoirs and irreversibility.

Core idea

A heater supplies energy Q₁ to a working substance, such as hot gas or steam. The substance expands, drives a piston or turbine, and returns to its initial state after releasing Q₂ to a cooler reservoir. Over a complete cycle its internal energy change is zero, hence net work equals heat input minus rejected heat.

Key terms and formulas

A = Q₁ − Q₂ and η = A/Q₁ = 1 − Q₂/Q₁, where Q₁ and Q₂ are positive heat magnitudes in joules (J), A is net work in J, and η is dimensionless; η · 100% gives percentage efficiency. For a normal heat engine 0 ≤ η < 1. Fuel-to-shaft efficiency includes further losses.

How to apply it

First label the heater, working substance and cooler. Find Q₁ and Q₂ for one full cycle, not for a single expansion stroke. Subtract to find net work, then divide by the input heat. If the answer exceeds 100%, recheck the heat directions. Consider exhaust heat and emissions in practical engines.

Worked example

An engine absorbs Q₁ = 2.0 kJ = 2000 J and rejects Q₂ = 1.2 kJ = 1200 J. A = 2000 J − 1200 J = 800 J; η = 800 J / 2000 J = 0.40 = 40%. Try it: input 500 J, output work 150 J; find η. (30%).

Limits and common mistakes

The formula concerns one complete cycle with consistently defined heat flows. Real engines also lose energy through friction and imperfect combustion. Do not use exhaust temperature as rejected heat, or divide useful work by Q₂.

Related theories

First Law of Thermodynamics (#79); The Carnot Cycle and Maximum Efficiency (#82); Second Law of Thermodynamics and Entropy (#83)

82. The Carnot Cycle and Maximum Efficiency

Origin

Sadi Carnot described an ideal reversible engine in 1824. Clausius and Kelvin later developed the second law that explains why no engine using the same two reservoirs can surpass this limit.

Core idea

An ideal Carnot cycle has two reversible isothermal steps, one in contact with each heat reservoir, and two reversible adiabatic steps that change the working substance's temperature without transferring heat. Reversibility makes the required rejected heat as small as thermodynamics permits for the given temperatures.

Key terms and formulas

η_C = 1 − T_c/T_h = (T_h − T_c)/T_h, with hot-reservoir temperature T_h and cold-reservoir temperature T_c both in kelvin (K), T_h > T_c > 0. The ratio is dimensionless. No real cyclic engine between only these reservoirs has η > η_C.

How to apply it

Convert both source temperatures to kelvin, identify the hotter and colder reservoirs and substitute in the Carnot expression. Compare any stated real efficiency with the bound. Increasing T_h or reducing T_c can raise the bound, but material limits and environmental costs matter.

Worked example

For T_h = 500 K and T_c = 300 K, η_C = 1 − 300 K/500 K = 0.40 = 40%. An engine claiming 55% between those reservoirs is impossible. Try it: calculate the limit for 800 K and 400 K. (50%).

Limits and common mistakes

Carnot efficiency applies to cyclic engines operating only between two fixed-temperature reservoirs and requires absolute temperatures. Reversible operation is a theoretical ideal. Never substitute Celsius values or claim that 100% follows from perfect engineering.

Related theories

Heat Engines and Their Efficiency (#81); Second Law of Thermodynamics and Entropy (#83); Refrigerators and Heat Pumps (#84)

83. Second Law of Thermodynamics and Entropy

Origin

Clausius and Kelvin formulated complementary statements of the second law in the nineteenth century, drawing on Carnot's engine analysis. Boltzmann later connected entropy with microscopic configurations.

Core idea

A cyclic machine cannot merely take heat from one reservoir and convert all of it to work. Nor can heat move by itself from a colder body to a hotter body without a compensating effect. Entropy is not simply everyday disorder: it is a thermodynamic state quantity connected to how energy is distributed among accessible microscopic states.

Key terms and formulas

For a reversible transfer at absolute temperature T, dS = δQ_rev/T; for constant T, ΔS = Q_rev/T. Entropy S has SI unit J/K, heat Q_rev is in J, and T in K. For any isolated system ΔS_total ≥ 0; equality characterises reversible processes, while strict increase characterises irreversible ones.

How to apply it

Identify the system and its surroundings. When finding an entropy change, choose a reversible calculation path even if the real process is irreversible, since entropy depends on state. Sum entropy changes of all parts of an isolated composite system. Never use the temperature in Celsius in Q/T.

Worked example

A hot reservoir at 400 K gives 200 J directly to one at 300 K. ΔS_hot = −200 J/400 K = −0.500 J/K; ΔS_cold = +200 J/300 K = +0.667 J/K. Total ΔS ≈ +0.167 J/K. Try it: 100 J passes from 500 K to 250 K; total ΔS? (+0.200 J/K).

Limits and common mistakes

ΔS = Q/T in this simple form requires a reversible isothermal heat transfer. Entropy of a subsystem can decrease if its surroundings increase more. Do not say entropy always increases everywhere or treat it solely as visual messiness.

Related theories

First Law of Thermodynamics (#79); Heat Engines and Their Efficiency (#81); The Carnot Cycle and Maximum Efficiency (#82)

84. Refrigerators and Heat Pumps

Origin

Mechanical refrigeration developed in the nineteenth century, following advances in the study of vapour compression and thermodynamics. Modern electric compressors circulate refrigerant through evaporation, compression, condensation and expansion.

Core idea

The refrigerant evaporates at low pressure while absorbing heat Q_c from a cold region. A compressor supplies work W; at higher pressure the refrigerant releases Q_h to a warm region. Energy balance requires Q_h = Q_c + W. Refrigerators and heat pumps differ mainly in which heat flow is useful.

Key terms and formulas

COP_cooling = Q_c/W and COP_heating = Q_h/W = 1 + COP_cooling; all energy terms are in J or the same energy unit, COP is dimensionless. For an ideal reversible device, COP_heating,Carnot = T_h/(T_h − T_c), temperatures in K.

How to apply it

Determine whether the desired result is cooling removed or heating delivered. Draw the heat and work arrows, apply energy conservation, then compute the appropriate COP. For the Carnot formula convert temperatures to kelvin and remember that real values are lower.

Worked example

A heat pump takes Q_c = 3.0 kWh from outdoor air and uses W = 1.0 kWh of electricity. Q_h = 3.0 + 1.0 = 4.0 kWh; COP_heating = 4.0/1.0 = 4.0. Try it: a refrigerator removes 600 J with 200 J of work. What is COP_cooling? (3.0).

Limits and common mistakes

COP is not the same as heat-engine efficiency and may exceed one. It depends on the indoor–outdoor temperature gap and equipment conditions. Do not identify delivered heat with electrical input alone; refrigerants and pressurised parts require qualified servicing.

Related theories

Heat Engines and Their Efficiency (#81); The Carnot Cycle and Maximum Efficiency (#82); Second Law of Thermodynamics and Entropy (#83)

85. Electric Charge and Its Conservation

Origin

Observations of electrical attraction go back to antiquity. Franklin distinguished two signs of charge; nineteenth-century experiments established their conservation, and Millikan measured the elementary charge in the early twentieth century.

Core idea

Protons carry positive charge, electrons negative charge and neutrons no net charge. In ordinary charging, electrons transfer between objects or redistribute inside them. Friction, contact and electrostatic induction can produce charge separation. An electroscope shows that charge is present, but its deflection alone does not identify the sign.

Key terms and formulas

q = ne, where q is charge in coulombs (C), n is a signed integer for isolated charged objects with quantised net charge, and e = 1.602176634 · 10⁻¹⁹ C. In a closed system Σq_before = Σq_after. Charge is a scalar with a positive or negative sign.

How to apply it

List all bodies and give charge signs before and after contact. For electron transfer, each added electron decreases charge by e. Apply conservation to the entire isolated set rather than one object. Keep nano- and microcoulomb conversion factors straight.

Worked example

A neutral metal sphere receives 5.0 · 10¹² electrons. Its charge becomes q = −Ne = −5.0 · 10¹² · 1.602 · 10⁻¹⁹ C = −8.01 · 10⁻⁷ C = −0.801 μC. Try it: removing 2 electrons leaves what charge? (+3.20 · 10⁻¹⁹ C).

Limits and common mistakes

Charge conservation applies to the complete isolated system, not to an individual body exchanging electrons. Charge quantisation is most obvious at particle scale. Do not confuse charge with current, or assume induction creates charge from nothing.

Related theories

Coulomb's Law (#86); Electric Field and Field Strength (#87); Electric Current and Current Strength (#94)

86. Coulomb's Law

Origin

Charles-Augustin de Coulomb measured electrostatic forces with a torsion balance in the 1780s, establishing their inverse-square dependence in the point-charge approximation.

Core idea

Charges of the same sign repel along the joining line; opposite signs attract. Doubling the separation reduces the force to one quarter. A uniform insulating medium changes the force through its relative permittivity. Unlike gravity, electric forces can repel as well as attract.

Key terms and formulas

F = (1/(4πε₀ε_r)) |q₁q₂|/r² = k|q₁q₂|/(ε_r r²), with F in N, charges q in C, separation r in m, ε_r dimensionless, ε₀ ≈ 8.854 · 10⁻¹² F/m and k ≈ 8.99 · 10⁹ N·m²/C² in vacuum.

How to apply it

Convert charges to coulombs and distance to metres. Identify the sign of each charge to select attraction or repulsion, then calculate the positive force magnitude. Check that multiplying distance by two divides the force by four. Use vector addition if more than two charges are present.

Worked example

For q₁ = +2.0 μC, q₂ = −3.0 μC, r = 0.30 m in vacuum: F = 8.99 · 10⁹ · (2.0 · 10⁻⁶)(3.0 · 10⁻⁶)/(0.30)² N ≈ 0.60 N, attractive. Try it: double r. What is F? (0.15 N, attractive).

Limits and common mistakes

The point-charge approximation fails when dimensions are comparable with separation; nonuniform media need more advanced methods. Never omit the square on r or use microcoulombs directly as coulombs. Direction follows charge signs, not the absolute-value formula.

Related theories

Electric Charge and Its Conservation (#85); Electric Field and Field Strength (#87); Field Lines and the Superposition Principle (#88)

87. Electric Field and Field Strength

Origin

Faraday developed the physical picture of fields and lines of force in the nineteenth century. Maxwell later expressed electric and magnetic fields mathematically.

Core idea

The field exists whether or not the test charge is inserted. At each point, its direction is the direction of force on a small positive test charge. Negative charges experience force opposite the field. An isolated positive point charge creates an outward radial field; a negative point charge creates an inward one.

Key terms and formulas

E = F/q for a sufficiently small positive test charge; E in N/C, F in N, q in C. For a stationary point charge Q in vacuum, the field magnitude is E = k|Q|/r², with k ≈ 8.99 · 10⁹ N·m²/C² and r in m. For any charge F = qE in vector form.

How to apply it

Draw the source charges and field direction before calculating. Convert μC and distances to SI. First compute E from the sources, then multiply by the test charge with its sign accounted for. For multiple charges add field vectors, not magnitudes unless they are collinear.

Worked example

A +3.0 μC charge placed in a field E = 200 N/C feels F = qE = 3.0 · 10⁻⁶ C · 200 N/C = 6.0 · 10⁻⁴ N along the field. Try it: a −2.0 μC charge in this field feels what force magnitude? (4.0 · 10⁻⁴ N, opposite the field).

Limits and common mistakes

The formula for point-charge field requires distance outside the finite charge distribution and electrostatics. E is independent of the chosen small test charge. Do not reverse the field for negative source charges or confuse E with force F.

Related theories

Electric Charge and Its Conservation (#85); Coulomb's Law (#86); Field Lines and the Superposition Principle (#88)

88. Field Lines and the Superposition Principle

Origin

Faraday popularised field-line diagrams in the nineteenth century. The superposition principle follows from the linearity of electrostatics in vacuum and many ordinary materials.

Core idea

Lines originate on positive charge and terminate on negative charge or extend to infinity; they never cross because the field cannot point in two directions at one location. A dipole, formed by nearby equal opposite charges, produces curved lines connecting its charges. Between ideal, large, oppositely charged parallel plates, the field is approximately uniform away from edges.

Key terms and formulas

E_total = E₁ + E₂ + … as a vector sum, with each E in N/C or V/m. Along perpendicular axes, E_magnitude = √(Eₓ² + Eᵧ²). For a point charge E_i = k|q_i|/r_i², with q in C and r in m in vacuum.

How to apply it

Draw the charge signs. At the chosen point sketch an E vector contributed by each source, following outward or inward direction. Resolve vectors into components, add signed components, then calculate magnitude and direction. Avoid interpreting the drawn line count as a literal number of physical strings.

Worked example

At a point field E₁ = 300 N/C right and E₂ = 400 N/C upward. They are perpendicular, so E = √(300² + 400²) N/C = 500 N/C, directed about 53.1° above right. Try it: E₂ points left at 100 N/C while E₁ points right at 300 N/C. What is the resultant? (200 N/C right).

Limits and common mistakes

Field lines are not trajectories of charged particles, and line density is a drawing convention. At a point of zero field direction is undefined. Do not add magnitudes of angled vectors or draw intersecting field lines.

Related theories

Coulomb's Law (#86); Electric Field and Field Strength (#87); Field Strength and Voltage in a Uniform Field (#91)

89. Conductors and Dielectrics in an Electric Field

Origin

Faraday demonstrated electrostatic shielding in the nineteenth century. Later studies of matter clarified how mobile carriers in metals and bound charges in insulators respond to electric fields.

Core idea

If a static field remained inside an ideal conductor, its mobile charges would continue moving, contradicting equilibrium. Their redistribution creates an opposing field. A closed conducting shell shields its interior from external static fields under ideal conditions. In an insulator, molecules develop or align electric dipoles; the field is generally reduced but not necessarily cancelled.

Key terms and formulas

In electrostatic equilibrium E_inside conductor = 0, where E is measured in N/C. For a linear uniform dielectric between ideal parallel plates, C = ε_r ε₀S/d, where ε_r is relative permittivity, ε₀ ≈ 8.854 · 10⁻¹² F/m, S is plate area in m², d separation in m, C capacitance in F.

How to apply it

Decide whether the material is a conductor or dielectric and whether the situation is static. For a metal in equilibrium, set the internal field to zero. For a capacitor with dielectric, identify the dielectric constant and whether voltage or free charge is fixed. Do not assume every dielectric perfectly blocks electric fields.

Worked example

A parallel-plate capacitor with air has C₀ = 20 pF. Filling its gap completely with an ideal dielectric of ε_r = 4 gives C = ε_r C₀ = 4 · 20 pF = 80 pF. Try it: ε_r = 3 and C₀ = 15 pF; find C. (45 pF).

Limits and common mistakes

Zero field inside a conductor assumes electrostatic equilibrium, not a conducting wire with current. Shielding is incomplete for some time-varying fields and openings. A dielectric may break down at high voltage; do not test shielding on live mains.

Related theories

Electric Field and Field Strength (#87); Capacitance and Capacitors (#92); Capacitor Combinations and Electric Field Energy (#93)

90. Work of the Electric Field, Potential and Voltage

Origin

Potential energy in electrostatics developed alongside Coulomb's law and nineteenth-century field theory. The electronvolt became a convenient energy unit in atomic physics.

Core idea

For a positive charge, electric force tends to move it from higher to lower potential, reducing its potential energy. A negative charge responds oppositely. In an electrostatic field the work depends only on the endpoints, not the path. Potential itself depends on a chosen zero; voltage differences have direct physical meaning.

Key terms and formulas

φ = U_p/q (potential, V), U_AB = φ_A − φ_B (voltage, V), A_AB = qU_AB (work, J), and ΔU_p = −A_AB. For a point charge Q in vacuum with zero at infinity, φ = kQ/r, where r is in m. 1 eV = 1.602176634 · 10⁻¹⁹ J.

How to apply it

Select initial A and final B and write U_AB = φ_A − φ_B before inserting signs. Multiply by charge q in C to find work in J. Distinguish electric work A from an external agent's work and never confuse the potential at one location with voltage across two points.

Worked example

A charge q = +2.0 μC moves from φ_A = 12 V to φ_B = 5 V. U_AB = 12 − 5 = 7 V; A_AB = 2.0 · 10⁻⁶ C · 7 V = 1.4 · 10⁻⁵ J. Try it: a +3 C charge drops through 2 V; what work? (+6 J).

Limits and common mistakes

Electrostatic work is path-independent only in electrostatics; changing magnetic fields can induce nonconservative electric fields. Signs matter: work done by the field is the negative change of potential energy. Potential and voltage are not the same.

Related theories

Electric Field and Field Strength (#87); Field Strength and Voltage in a Uniform Field (#91); Capacitance and Capacitors (#92)

91. Field Strength and Voltage in a Uniform Field

Origin

Field lines and equipotential surfaces were central to nineteenth-century electrostatics, especially Faraday's field picture and Maxwell's mathematical development.

Core idea

Field strength describes force per charge, whereas voltage describes energy change per charge between locations. An equipotential surface has the same potential everywhere; moving a charge along it requires no electrostatic work. In electrostatics field lines meet equipotential surfaces at right angles. For an ideal plate region the potential changes linearly across the gap.

Key terms and formulas

For uniform field parallel to displacement, |U| = Ed and E = |U|/d, where E is in V/m = N/C, U in V, and d in m. In general the potential change is Δφ = −∫E·dl. A charge q accelerated through a voltage magnitude U changes kinetic energy by |q|U if other energy changes are negligible.

How to apply it

Sketch plate polarity, field arrows from positive to negative, and equipotential planes perpendicular to them. Convert gap to metres. Compute E = U/d, then check force direction for a positive or negative particle. This simple formula does not apply directly to strongly nonuniform fields.

Worked example

Two ideal plates differ by 150 V and are 5.0 mm = 0.0050 m apart. E = 150 V/0.0050 m = 3.0 · 10⁴ V/m. Try it: 60 V across a 2.0 mm gap gives what E? (3.0 · 10⁴ V/m).

Limits and common mistakes

E = U/d requires a uniform field and a distance measured along its direction. Near plate edges the field bends. Do not replace d by an arbitrary diagonal distance or use centimetres as metres.

Related theories

Electric Field and Field Strength (#87); Work of the Electric Field, Potential and Voltage (#90); Capacitance and Capacitors (#92)

92. Capacitance and Capacitors

Origin

The Leyden jar, developed in the eighteenth century, was an early device for storing separated electric charge. Modern capacitors use metal foils, ceramic or film dielectrics, or other electrode structures.

Core idea

A capacitor has two conductors separated by an insulator. A source moves charges so the plates acquire equal and opposite charges. The electric field between them stores energy. Increasing plate area or dielectric permittivity raises the capacitance, while increasing plate separation reduces it in an ideal parallel-plate model.

Key terms and formulas

C = |q|/|U|, where C is in farads (F), q is the magnitude of charge on one plate in C and U in V. For large ideal parallel plates C = ε_r ε₀S/d, with ε₀ ≈ 8.854 · 10⁻¹² F/m, area S in m² and separation d in m. 1 μF = 10⁻⁶ F; 1 nF = 10⁻⁹ F.

How to apply it

Convert capacitance and voltage to SI units. Use q = CU to find the magnitude of stored plate charge, not the sum of absolute charges on both plates. In a geometry problem check dielectric coverage and fringe-field assumptions. Disconnect power and discharge safely before touching capacitors.

Worked example

A capacitor has C = 22 μF and U = 9.0 V. |q| = CU = 22 · 10⁻⁶ F · 9.0 V = 1.98 · 10⁻⁴ C = 198 μC. Try it: C = 4.0 μF, U = 12 V; find |q|. (48 μC).

Limits and common mistakes

The parallel-plate formula neglects fringing and assumes a homogeneous linear dielectric. Real capacitors have voltage ratings and leakage. Do not add plate-charge magnitudes to obtain q; charge is specified per plate.

93. Capacitor Combinations and Electric Field Energy

Origin

Rules for capacitor networks developed from electrostatics and conservation of charge during the nineteenth century. Stored electric energy is central to pulse circuits, camera flashes and medical equipment.

Core idea

In parallel, each branch connects across the same two nodes, so charges add. In series, intermediate isolated conductor nodes cannot accumulate a net externally supplied charge, so equal-magnitude plate charges occur in each component. The source does work separating charge, leaving energy in the field. A charged capacitor may remain hazardous after disconnecting supply.

Key terms and formulas

Parallel: C_eq = ΣC_i. Series: 1/C_eq = Σ(1/C_i). Stored energy W = CU²/2 = qU/2 = q²/(2C), where C in F, U in V, q in C and W in J. These assume ideal linear capacitors and steady electrostatic conditions.

How to apply it

Mark shared nodes to distinguish parallel from series sections. Reduce the network step by step, then recover branch voltage or charge as needed. Use a single consistent energy formula and units. Never handle high-voltage capacitors or defibrillator circuits without qualified supervision.

Worked example

Two capacitors 6 μF and 3 μF are in series across 9 V. C_eq = (6 · 3)/(6 + 3) μF = 2 μF. q = C_eq U = 18 μC; W = C_eq U²/2 = 81 μJ. Try it: the same capacitors in parallel give what C_eq? (9 μF).

Limits and common mistakes

Network formulas assume initially uncharged ideal intermediate nodes and linear elements. Unequal series capacitors may see unequal voltages; exceeding rated voltage can be dangerous. Stored energy remains after switching off the source.

94. Electric Current and Current Strength

Origin

Volta's battery around 1800 enabled sustained currents. Ampère and other nineteenth-century scientists studied their magnetic and electrical effects.

Core idea

A sustained conduction current needs mobile charge carriers and a driving electric field maintained by a source. Metals conduct mainly via electrons, electrolytes via ions and semiconductors via electrons and holes. Conventional current is defined in the direction of positive charge flow. Current is not consumed by a resistor; energy is transferred while charge continues through the circuit.

Key terms and formulas

I = Δq/Δt for average current, and I = dq/dt instantaneously, where I is current strength in amperes (A), q in coulombs (C), and t in seconds (s). For steady current, q = It; 1 A = 1 C/s. Current is a scalar signed by a chosen reference direction.

How to apply it

Choose a cross-section and current direction, then divide transported charge by elapsed time. Distinguish the movement of positive carriers from electrons. For alternating or varying currents use a time interval or the instantaneous derivative as appropriate. Avoid touching mains wiring.

Worked example

A steady current I = 0.50 A flows for t = 3.0 min = 180 s. Charge transferred is q = It = 0.50 C/s · 180 s = 90 C. Try it: 24 C passes in 6 s; what current? (4 A).

Limits and common mistakes

I = q/t with a single q/t quotient applies to average or constant current, not all instantaneous variations. Electrons in metal move opposite conventional current. Do not imagine a resistor consumes charges or that electron drift speed is signal speed.

Related theories

Electric Charge and Its Conservation (#85); Electrical Resistance and Resistivity (#95); Ohm's Law for a Circuit Section (#96)

95. Electrical Resistance and Resistivity

Origin

Georg Ohm systematically studied the relation between voltage, current and conductor properties in the 1820s. Resistivity became an important material property as electrical engineering developed.

Core idea

Moving carriers scatter from vibrating atoms, defects and other carriers, creating resistance in ordinary metals. Resistivity ρ is a material parameter that varies with temperature. A rheostat changes useful resistance by varying the effective length of resistive wire or the circuit connection.

Key terms and formulas

R = ρl/S, where R is resistance in ohms (Ω), ρ resistivity in Ω·m, l wire length in m, and S cross-sectional area in m². For a round wire S = πd²/4, diameter d in m. For a limited temperature range in metals, R ≈ R₀[1 + α(T − T₀)], with α in K⁻¹.

How to apply it

Identify the conductor geometry, select resistivity at the relevant temperature, and convert mm² to m² using 1 mm² = 10⁻⁶ m². Calculate area before dividing. Check scaling with length and area. Avoid assuming resistance is temperature independent in hot filaments.

Worked example

Copper-like wire with ρ = 1.7 · 10⁻⁸ Ω·m, length l = 10 m and area S = 1.0 mm² = 1.0 · 10⁻⁶ m² has R = ρl/S = 0.17 Ω. Try it: double the length at equal area and temperature. What is R? (0.34 Ω).

Limits and common mistakes

R = ρl/S assumes uniform composition, cross-section and temperature. Resistivity changes with temperature; electrical contacts can add resistance. Do not confuse ρ in Ω·m with density in kg/m³ or use mm² without conversion.

Related theories

Electric Current and Current Strength (#94); Ohm's Law for a Circuit Section (#96); Current in Metals and Superconductivity (#103)

96. Ohm's Law for a Circuit Section

Origin

Georg Ohm reported his quantitative investigations in 1827. His measurements showed how current, voltage and resistance are related for appropriate conductors.

Core idea

Voltage drives mobile charge through a conducting section; resistance relates the resulting steady current to that voltage. An ohmic resistor has a straight-line I-versus-U graph through the origin at fixed temperature. A filament lamp warms and changes resistance; a semiconductor diode typically has a strongly nonlinear characteristic.

Key terms and formulas

I = U/R, U = IR, R = U/I, with I in amperes (A), U in volts (V), and R in ohms (Ω). In an I–U plot, slope ΔI/ΔU = 1/R for a constant ohmic resistance. In a U–I plot, slope is R.

How to apply it

Draw the circuit section and mark the potential drop. Convert values into V, A and Ω. Choose the desired form of Ohm's law and check whether the element is ohmic. Verify that increasing voltage increases current for a fixed positive resistor. Work only with safe low-voltage practice circuits.

Worked example

A resistor R = 8.0 Ω is connected across U = 12 V. I = U/R = 12 V/8.0 Ω = 1.5 A. Try it: I = 0.25 A through R = 40 Ω; find U. (10 V).

Limits and common mistakes

Ohm's law I = U/R with fixed R describes ohmic elements under appropriate conditions. It does not establish a constant resistance for a diode or incandescent filament. Be careful which axis the graph uses when finding slope.

Related theories

Electric Current and Current Strength (#94); Electrical Resistance and Resistivity (#95); Series and Parallel Connections (#97)

97. Series and Parallel Connections

Origin

Series and parallel rules follow from Ohm's law and conservation of charge and energy, developed with nineteenth-century circuit theory.

Core idea

Series components share a single unbranched current path; more resistance means less current from a fixed-voltage source. Parallel components share both connection nodes; adding a branch increases overall conductance. In mixed circuits, sections can be reduced to equivalents without changing the external current-voltage relationship.

Key terms and formulas

Series: R_eq = ΣR_i, I_i = I and U_total = ΣU_i. Parallel: 1/R_eq = Σ(1/R_i), U_i = U and I_total = ΣI_i. For two parallel resistors R_eq = R₁R₂/(R₁ + R₂). A series voltage divider gives U₂ = U_total R₂/(R₁ + R₂), assuming no load and ideal resistors.

How to apply it

Identify junctions and mark shared nodes. Reduce obvious series and parallel groups one at a time. Compute total current from the total resistance, then recover branch currents or voltages. Check that currents entering a junction equal currents leaving and that series voltages add to the source voltage.

Worked example

A 4 Ω and an 8 Ω resistor are in series across 12 V. R_eq = 12 Ω; I = 12 V/12 Ω = 1.0 A; drops are 4 V and 8 V. Try it: connect 4 Ω and 8 Ω in parallel. What is R_eq? (8/3 Ω ≈ 2.67 Ω).

Limits and common mistakes

Two resistors sharing just one node are not automatically in series if that node has another branch. A voltage divider changes when loaded. Do not treat household outlets as series-connected; mains voltage is hazardous.

98. Electromotive Force and Ohm's Law for a Complete Circuit

Origin

Volta's electrochemical cell provided an early steady source of electrical energy. Ohm's circuit law and later research distinguished source EMF from the measured terminal potential difference under load.

Core idea

A source converts chemical or another form of energy into electrical energy. In a simple closed circuit its EMF drives current through the external load R and internal resistance r. Some energy heats the source, so the voltage across its terminals during discharge is less than its EMF. Under a short circuit, current can become dangerously high.

Key terms and formulas

ℰ = A_non-electric/q, in volts (V), with work A in J and charge q in C. For one source and resistive load I = ℰ/(R + r), terminal U = ℰ − Ir = IR. Short-circuit idealisation I_short = ℰ/r if r > 0. For identical cells in series EMFs and internal resistances add; identical parallel cells need careful current sharing.

How to apply it

Draw the external resistance and source internal resistance separately. Add resistances, calculate current, then calculate terminal voltage using either IR or ℰ − Ir. Check U ≤ ℰ during discharge. Never deliberately short-circuit a battery.

Worked example

Given ℰ = 9.0 V, R = 4.0 Ω and r = 0.50 Ω, I = 9.0 V/(4.50 Ω) = 2.0 A. Terminal U = IR = 2.0 A · 4.0 Ω = 8.0 V. Try it: ℰ = 6 V, R = 2 Ω, r = 1 Ω; find I. (2 A).

Limits and common mistakes

The simple equation assumes a source with approximately constant EMF and internal resistance. Both may depend on temperature and state of charge. The theoretical short-circuit current is not a safe experiment. Do not confuse EMF with terminal voltage under load.

Related theories

Electric Current and Current Strength (#94); Ohm's Law for a Circuit Section (#96); Kirchhoff's Rules (#99)

99. Kirchhoff's Rules

Origin

Gustav Kirchhoff formulated circuit rules in 1845 while studying electric networks. The laws are foundational for modern circuit analysis.

Core idea

At a junction, charge does not accumulate in the steady-state model, so currents balance. Around a closed loop each rise in potential across a source must be balanced by drops over resistors and other elements. Assigning a trial current direction is allowed; a negative solved current means the actual flow is opposite.

Key terms and formulas

Junction rule: ΣI_enter = ΣI_leave, where I is in A. Loop rule: ΣΔV = 0, voltage changes in V. Across an ideal resistor going with current, ΔV = −IR; across a source from negative to positive terminal, ΔV = +ℰ. For a source with internal resistance include its Ir drop.

How to apply it

Choose current directions and label every loop traversal. Apply the junction rule at independent nodes, then write independent loop equations using one sign convention. Solve simultaneous equations and check both charge and voltage balances. For rapidly varying fields, simple lumped-circuit assumptions may fail.

Worked example

A junction carries I₁ = 2.0 A and I₂ = 1.5 A inward, and I₃ outward. Kirchhoff's junction rule gives I₃ = 2.0 + 1.5 = 3.5 A. In a loop with source ℰ = 12 V and resistors 2 Ω and 4 Ω in series, 12 − 2I − 4I = 0, so I = 2 A. Try it: currents 4 A and 1 A enter a junction; what total current leaves? (5 A).

Limits and common mistakes

The lumped-circuit rules assume charge does not accumulate significantly at nodes and changing magnetic flux through loops is negligible or explicitly accounted for. Sign consistency matters more than guessed direction. Do not count dependent loop equations twice.

Related theories

100. Work and Power of Electric Current

Origin

Joule investigated heating by electric currents in the 1840s; Lenz independently studied the connection. Their results linked electrical energy to heat and energy conservation.

Core idea

When charges move through a potential difference, electrical work is done. In an ordinary resistor, this electrical energy becomes internal energy and then heat transferred to surroundings. In other devices, input power may also become light, mechanical work or stored chemical energy. An electrical meter commonly measures energy in kilowatt-hours, not instantaneous power.

Key terms and formulas

A = UIt and P = A/t = UI, with A in J, U in V, I in A, t in s and P in W. For an ohmic resistor P = I²R = U²/R; Joule–Lenz heating Q = I²Rt if all electrical work turns into heat. 1 kWh = 3.6 · 10⁶ J.

How to apply it

Identify whether the task asks for power, energy or heat. Convert minutes to seconds for joules, or use hours with kilowatts for kWh. For an ohmic element choose the formula matching given quantities. Check circuit current ratings and never bypass fuses.

Worked example

A heater rated P = 1.5 kW runs for t = 2.0 h. Electrical energy E = Pt = 1.5 kW · 2.0 h = 3.0 kWh = 1.08 · 10⁷ J. Try it: a 60 W lamp operates for 5 h. Energy used? (0.30 kWh).

Limits and common mistakes

P = I²R and U²/R are resistor relations, not universal identities for motors or batteries. kWh measures energy, not power. Resistive heating can cause fire; use suitable fuses, conductors and electrical protection.

Related theories

Electric Current and Current Strength (#94); Ohm's Law for a Circuit Section (#96); Electromotive Force and Ohm's Law for a Complete Circuit (#98)

101. Ammeters, Voltmeters and Multimeters

Origin

Early galvanometers detected current by magnetic deflection in the nineteenth century. Later moving-coil instruments and electronic digital multimeters enabled quantitative voltage, current and resistance measurements.

Core idea

A current meter should alter the original current as little as possible, so an ideal ammeter has zero internal resistance. An ideal voltmeter has infinite resistance and draws no current. Real meters have finite input characteristics; placing an ammeter across a source can create an extremely dangerous low-resistance path. Resistance mode uses an internal test source and requires an unpowered circuit.

Key terms and formulas

Ohm's law connects measured quantities: R = U/I, where U is voltage in volts (V), I current in amperes (A), and R resistance in ohms (Ω). Real voltmeters typically have high input resistance; current ranges have limited maximum currents and fuse ratings. Voltage ranges specify AC or DC measurements separately.

How to apply it

Disconnect power before changing the wiring. Put an ammeter in series and a voltmeter across the component, select AC or DC, and begin with an appropriately high range. Check the red lead socket and polarity for DC. Compare measured I and U with R = U/I, accounting for meter loading. Never test mains voltage without suitable training and rated equipment.

Worked example

A low-voltage resistor shows U = 4.5 V, and the series ammeter reads I = 0.15 A. R = U/I = 4.5 V / 0.15 A = 30 Ω. Try it: a 20 Ω resistor has 3.0 V across it. What current is expected? (0.15 A).

Limits and common mistakes

Meters load real circuits; an ammeter is never placed directly across a source. Wrong lead sockets, AC/DC settings and energized resistance measurements are common hazards.

Related theories

Electric Current and Current Strength (#94); Ohm's Law for a Circuit Section (#96); Series and Parallel Connections (#97)

102. Charging and Discharging a Capacitor

Origin

Exponential electrical transients emerged from nineteenth-century research on capacitors and circuit laws. Modern electronics uses RC networks to set delays and filter signals.

Core idea

Current flows initially because a voltage difference exists across the resistor. As charge builds up on the capacitor plates, its voltage rises, leaving less voltage across the resistor and reducing the current. In discharge the capacitor supplies current, and its voltage falls without changing sign in a simple passive RC circuit.

Key terms and formulas

τ = RC with R in Ω, C in farads (F), τ in seconds (s). For charging from zero with an ideal constant source U₀, U_C(t) = U₀(1 − e⁻ᵗ⁄τ); for discharge from initial U₀, U_C(t) = U₀e⁻ᵗ⁄τ. At t = τ the respective fractions are 0.632 and 0.368; at 5τ charging is approximately 99.3% complete.

How to apply it

Convert kilo-ohms and microfarads to SI before multiplying. Identify whether charging or discharge occurs and the initial capacitor voltage. Evaluate t/τ and its exponential; never substitute t for τ. Treat the source and components as ideal unless leakage and internal resistance are specified. Capacitors can retain charge after power is disconnected.

Worked example

For R = 10 kΩ = 10,000 Ω and C = 100 μF = 0.000100 F, τ = 1.0 s. Charging from 0 V toward 12 V gives U_C(1.0 s) = 12 V(1 − e⁻¹) ≈ 7.59 V. Try it: what voltage remains after 1τ in discharge from 10 V? (3.68 V).

Limits and common mistakes

Exponential laws assume a linear ideal capacitor, resistor and source; leakage alters long-time behavior. Do not confuse 63% charged with 63% remaining, or milliseconds with seconds.

Related theories

Capacitance and Capacitors (#92); Electrical Resistance and Resistivity (#95); Work and Power of Electric Current (#100)

103. Current in Metals and Superconductivity

Origin

Drude's early twentieth-century electron model explained much ordinary metallic conduction. Kamerlingh Onnes discovered superconductivity in mercury in 1911; later quantum theories explained its behavior.

Core idea

A battery establishes an electric field, giving electrons a small average drift opposite the conventional current direction. Heating a normal metallic wire usually increases its resistance. A superconducting state emerges only below a critical temperature and within critical magnetic-field and current limits. It also expels magnetic flux in characteristic conditions, the Meissner effect.

Key terms and formulas

For a modest temperature range, R = R₀[1 + α(T − T₀)], with R and R₀ in Ω, T in °C or K differences, and temperature coefficient α in K⁻¹. This linear approximation breaks down over broad ranges and at phase transitions. Zero DC resistance does not mean every superconductor works without energy for cooling.

How to apply it

Identify the initial resistance and reference temperature. Convert the temperature change without adding 273.15 to a difference, multiply by α, and inspect whether resistance should rise. When discussing superconductors, always state the need for a suitable temperature, magnetic field, and current. Strong superconducting magnets must be handled professionally.

Worked example

A copper wire has R₀ = 10.0 Ω at 20 °C; take α = 0.0039 K⁻¹. At 70 °C, ΔT = 50 K and R = 10.0 Ω(1 + 0.0039 · 50) = 11.95 Ω. Try it: for ΔT = 100 K with the same wire, find R. (13.9 Ω).

Limits and common mistakes

The linear temperature law is approximate and fails near phase transitions. Electron drift is opposite conventional current, while superconductivity needs critical conditions, not merely any cold metal.

Related theories

Electric Current and Current Strength (#94); Electrical Resistance and Resistivity (#95); Magnetic Properties of Matter (#112)

104. Current in Electrolytes and Electrolysis

Origin

Michael Faraday formulated the quantitative laws of electrolysis in the 1830s. They helped connect electrical charge with the chemical amount of matter transformed.

Core idea

Dissociation creates mobile cations and anions. Cations move toward the cathode, anions toward the anode; the signs of electrodes depend on the electrochemical setup. Electrode reactions transfer electrons between the external circuit and ions. The required charge depends on the ion charge number and number of particles converted.

Key terms and formulas

m = (M/(zF))It = kIt, where deposited mass m is in kg when molar mass M is in kg/mol, charge number z is dimensionless, Faraday constant F ≈ 96,485 C/mol, I in A and t in s; k = M/(zF) in kg/C. Q = It in C. Current efficiency may be below 100%.

How to apply it

Identify the reaction and number z of electrons per deposited atom. Convert molar mass from g/mol to kg/mol and time to seconds. Calculate Q, then m; check whether all current goes into the desired deposition. Electrolytes and electrode products may be hazardous, so electrolysis needs appropriate supervision.

Worked example

For copper ions Cu²⁺, z = 2, M = 0.0635 kg/mol. With I = 2.0 A for 1000 s, Q = 2000 C; m = 0.0635 · 2000/(2 · 96,485) kg ≈ 0.000658 kg = 0.658 g. Try it: double the time at the same current. What mass deposits? (1.32 g).

Limits and common mistakes

Faraday deposition assumes a specified reaction and full current efficiency; competing reactions reduce actual yield. Avoid confusing molar mass with atomic mass or using grams instead of kilograms in SI.

Related theories

Amount of Substance and the Avogadro Constant (#58); Electric Current and Current Strength (#94); Work and Power of Electric Current (#100)

105. Current in Gases and Vacuum

Origin

Scientists investigated gas discharges and cathode rays in the nineteenth century. Thomson identified the electron in 1897, while studies of discharge tubes advanced plasma and vacuum electronics.

Core idea

Ionization can result from energetic particles, light, or a sufficiently strong electric field. A non-self-sustained discharge stops when the external ionization source is removed; self-sustained discharge maintains ionization through the discharge itself. Thermionic emission frees electrons from a heated metal surface. In a vacuum diode a positive anode collects them, while reverse polarity strongly suppresses current.

Key terms and formulas

Current I = Δq/Δt is measured in A and charge q in C. If N singly charged electrons reach the electrode in time t, the current magnitude is I = Ne/t, where e ≈ 1.602 · 10⁻¹⁹ C. Breakdown voltage is not a universal constant: it depends on gas, pressure, gap and electrode shape.

How to apply it

For counting-charge questions, convert the particle number to charge with e, then divide by the duration. For discharge identification, ask what causes ionization and whether it persists after the source is removed. Do not treat gas conduction like Ohmic conduction in a fixed metal resistor. Never experiment with high-voltage discharges or vacuum-tube supplies without specialist protection.

Worked example

A vacuum tube transfers 3.0 · 10¹⁵ electrons to its anode each second. I = Ne/t = 3.0 · 10¹⁵ · 1.602 · 10⁻¹⁹ C / 1.0 s ≈ 4.81 · 10⁻⁴ A = 0.481 mA. Try it: twice as many electrons per second gives what current? (0.961 mA).

Limits and common mistakes

Gas discharge is nonlinear and depends strongly on pressure and electrode spacing. Do not assume vacuum contains gas ions, or that breakdown voltage is constant across different geometries.

Related theories

Electric Charge and Its Conservation (#85); Electric Current and Current Strength (#94); The p–n Junction, Diodes and Transistors (#107)

106. Semiconductors and Their Conductivity

Origin

Semiconductor physics grew from studies of rectifiers and the development of quantum band theory in the early twentieth century. Controlled silicon doping enabled modern transistors and integrated circuits.

Core idea

An electron promoted into a conduction band leaves a hole in the valence band. Both can move as effective charge carriers. Pure silicon has equal electron and hole concentrations in thermal equilibrium. Donor dopants provide extra electrons, while acceptors produce holes; the crystal as a whole remains electrically neutral. Rising temperature normally creates more carriers in an intrinsic semiconductor.

Key terms and formulas

For a uniform sample, R = ρl/S where resistance R is in Ω, resistivity ρ in Ω·m, length l in m and cross-sectional area S in m². Conductivity σ = 1/ρ in S/m. In a simplified carrier model σ = e(nμₙ + pμₚ), with n and p in m⁻³ and mobilities μ in m²/(V·s).

How to apply it

First identify whether electrons or holes are majority carriers, then choose the appropriate direction of conventional current. For resistance calculations use the actual material resistivity at the given temperature; do not assume it is constant during heating or illumination. A photoresistor responds to light-generated carriers, whereas a thermistor responds strongly to temperature.

Worked example

A semiconductor bar has ρ = 2.0 Ω·m, l = 0.020 m and S = 1.0 · 10⁻⁴ m². R = ρl/S = 2.0 · 0.020/(1.0 · 10⁻⁴) Ω = 400 Ω. Try it: what happens when S doubles at fixed ρ and l? (R = 200 Ω).

Limits and common mistakes

Resistivity depends on temperature, illumination and doping. Do not confuse a mobile positive hole with an actual positive particle or mistake n-type material for an overall negatively charged crystal.

Related theories

Electrical Resistance and Resistivity (#95); Current in Metals and Superconductivity (#103); The p–n Junction, Diodes and Transistors (#107)

107. The p–n Junction, Diodes and Transistors

Origin

The point-contact transistor was demonstrated at Bell Labs in 1947; junction transistors followed. Semiconductor diodes and transistors became building blocks of integrated circuits.

Core idea

Near the boundary, electrons and holes diffuse and recombine, leaving a depletion layer containing fixed charged dopant ions and a built-in electric field. Forward voltage lowers the effective barrier; reverse bias raises it. A diode's forward voltage is not a strict universal constant. In a bipolar transistor a small base current can control a larger collector current; field-effect transistors use a gate voltage.

Key terms and formulas

In a simple LED circuit, R = (U_s − U_LED)/I where source voltage U_s and LED drop U_LED are in V, current I in A, and series resistance R in Ω. This is an approximate design equation for a known forward drop. The ideal diode approximation allows current one way and blocks it the other, but actual devices have leakage, breakdown and current limits.

How to apply it

Draw the diode orientation and identify anode and cathode before connecting a low-voltage source. Include a current-limiting resistor for LEDs and check the manufacturer's ratings. For switching problems distinguish control terminal from the load-current path. Never connect an LED directly across an uncontrolled power source.

Worked example

A 5.0 V source powers an LED with an approximate 2.0 V forward drop at 0.010 A. The resistor needs 5.0 V − 2.0 V = 3.0 V, so R = 3.0 V/0.010 A = 300 Ω. Try it: at the same voltage drop, what resistor gives 0.020 A? (150 Ω).

Limits and common mistakes

A diode is not a perfect one-way switch; breakdown, leakage and overheating are real. Do not use one universal LED forward voltage or neglect current limiting and transistor terminal ratings.

108. Magnetic Field and Magnetic Induction

Origin

People used lodestones as compasses long before modern physics. In the nineteenth century, Oersted linked magnetism with electric currents, and Faraday developed the field picture.

Core idea

A bar magnet has north and south poles. Outside the magnet, conventional field lines point from north toward south and continue through the magnet to form closed loops. A compass's north-seeking end points along the local B direction. Isolated magnetic monopoles have not been observed. A magnetic field affects a stationary permanent magnet by torque and moving charges through magnetic force.

Key terms and formulas

B is magnetic induction with SI unit tesla (T). For a straight wire at right angles to a uniform field, B = F/(Il), where F is force in N, I current in A and l the wire length in m; this relation applies when the force is perpendicular and the wire section is straight. Earth's surface field is roughly 25–65 μT depending on location.

How to apply it

Sketch the magnet and test compass direction in a region away from metallic interference. Mark closed field-line loops, with closer lines indicating stronger fields qualitatively. For quantitative problems, identify force, current, length and orientation before solving for B. Avoid confusing Earth's geographic and magnetic north or treating poles as independent charges.

Worked example

A 0.20 m straight wire carrying 2.0 A perpendicular to a uniform field experiences a 0.040 N magnetic force. B = F/(Il) = 0.040 N/(2.0 A · 0.20 m) = 0.10 T. Try it: if current doubles, what force acts at the same B and l? (0.080 N).

Limits and common mistakes

Field-line density gives only a qualitative picture. Do not confuse the tesla with the weber or mistake a single pole for an isolated magnetic charge.

Related theories

Magnetic Field of an Electric Current (#109); The Ampère Force and Electric Motors (#110); Magnetic Flux (#113)

109. Magnetic Field of an Electric Current

Origin

In 1820 Hans Christian Oersted showed that a current deflects a compass needle. Ampère subsequently analysed the magnetic effects of currents and helped establish electrodynamics.

Core idea

Use the right-hand grip rule: point your thumb along conventional current, then your curled fingers show the magnetic field around the wire. For a solenoid, curled fingers follow current around the windings and thumb shows the field inside. The field grows with current, while the straight-wire field falls with distance. A ferromagnetic core may greatly strengthen a coil's field.

Key terms and formulas

For a very long straight wire in vacuum, B = μ₀I/(2πr), where B in T, I in A, distance r in m, μ₀ ≈ 1.256637 · 10⁻⁶ N/A². For an ideal long solenoid in air B ≈ μ₀nI, where n = N/l is turns per metre. Both formulas assume their respective ideal geometries.

How to apply it

Identify wire or solenoid geometry before choosing a formula. Draw current direction and apply the right-hand rule for B. Convert centimetres to metres; check that doubling r halves B near a long wire. Do not apply the long-solenoid formula close to its ends. Disconnect circuits before rearranging electromagnet coils.

Worked example

A long straight wire carries I = 5.0 A. At r = 0.010 m, B = 1.256637 · 10⁻⁶ · 5.0/(2π · 0.010) T = 1.0 · 10⁻⁴ T = 100 μT. Try it: at r = 0.020 m, find B. (50 μT).

Limits and common mistakes

Wire and solenoid expressions require idealized geometries and roughly uniform materials. Applying the right-hand rule to electron drift instead of conventional current reverses the answer.

Related theories

Magnetic Field and Magnetic Induction (#108); The Ampère Force and Electric Motors (#110); Magnetic Properties of Matter (#112)

110. The Ampère Force and Electric Motors

Origin

André-Marie Ampère studied interactions between electric currents during the 1820s. Faraday and other inventors built early electric-motor demonstrations during the nineteenth century.

Core idea

Moving charges within a wire feel magnetic forces; these are transmitted to the wire. In a uniform field, a straight wire has force perpendicular to both conventional current and B. Parallel wires attract if their currents flow in the same direction and repel if opposite. A motor's commutation or electronic control maintains a useful turning moment as the rotor rotates.

Key terms and formulas

F = BIl sin α, where F in N, B in T, I in A, wire length l in m and α the angle between current and magnetic field. For a single rectangular loop, moment magnitude M = BIA sin θ, where A in m² and θ is the angle between loop normal and B; N turns multiply the result by N.

How to apply it

Draw the current arrow and magnetic-field direction, then apply the left-hand motor rule or vector-force rule to determine force direction. Compute the sine of the angle, noting that zero degrees gives zero force. For motor problems check whether the angle is to the wire or to the loop normal. Do not handle energized motor windings or rotating parts carelessly.

Worked example

A 0.30 m wire carries I = 4.0 A perpendicular to B = 0.20 T. F = 0.20 T · 4.0 A · 0.30 m = 0.24 N. Try it: if the wire makes a 30° angle with B, what is F? (0.12 N).

Limits and common mistakes

The expression assumes a straight wire in a uniform B. Use the angle to current for force, but to the loop normal for torque; parallel wires interact through their fields.

Related theories

Magnetic Field and Magnetic Induction (#108); Magnetic Field of an Electric Current (#109); The Lorentz Force and Charged Particles in a Magnetic Field (#111)

111. The Lorentz Force and Charged Particles in a Magnetic Field

Origin

Hendrik Lorentz formulated the electromagnetic force on charges in the late nineteenth century, extending the understanding of particle motion in electromagnetic fields.

Core idea

The force direction is given by the cross product of velocity and magnetic field for a positive charge and reversed for a negative charge. A particle with velocity parallel to B experiences no magnetic force. With a perpendicular component, the force acts as a centripetal force; an added parallel component produces a helix. A magnetic field alone performs no work on the charge.

Key terms and formulas

Magnitude F = |q|vB sin α, with charge q in C, speed v in m/s, B in T. For perpendicular entry r = mv/(|q|B), where mass m in kg and radius r in m; period T = 2πm/(|q|B), independent of speed in the nonrelativistic approximation. If an electric field is also present, include electric force qE.

How to apply it

Choose the sign of q and mark v and B directions. For circular motion equate |q|vB = mv²/r, then solve for the unknown. Check unit conversions and whether relativistic speeds or particle collisions invalidate the simple model. Do not confuse the magnetic force with a force along the direction of motion.

Worked example

A proton with m = 1.67 · 10⁻²⁷ kg and q = 1.60 · 10⁻¹⁹ C moves at v = 2.0 · 10⁶ m/s perpendicular to B = 0.50 T. r = mv/(qB) ≈ 0.0418 m = 4.18 cm. Try it: double B. What is r? (2.09 cm).

Limits and common mistakes

The circular-radius and period formulas assume uniform B and nonrelativistic speed. Magnetic force changes direction, not kinetic energy; a negative charge reverses the force direction.

Related theories

Centripetal Acceleration (#19); Magnetic Field and Magnetic Induction (#108); The Ampère Force and Electric Motors (#110)

112. Magnetic Properties of Matter

Origin

Faraday studied magnetic responses of materials in the nineteenth century. Pierre Curie's work in the 1890s linked magnetism with temperature; twentieth-century physics explained magnetic domains and microscopic moments.

Core idea

Magnetization arises mainly from electron magnetic moments. Diamagnetic response generally points opposite the applied field, while paramagnetic response weakly follows it. Ferromagnets contain regions called domains with aligned moments; domain growth and rotation produce strong magnetic responses. A magnetic hysteresis loop shows that the response depends on field history, allowing permanent magnets and memory devices.

Key terms and formulas

For a linear isotropic medium, B = μ₀μᵣH, where B in T, H in A/m, relative permeability μᵣ dimensionless and μ₀ ≈ 1.256637 · 10⁻⁶ H/m. This simple constant-μᵣ relation is generally unsuitable for ferromagnets because of saturation and hysteresis. Curie temperature T_C is material-specific.

How to apply it

First classify the material and determine whether the field is weak or strong. If μᵣ is given for a linear material, calculate B from H; otherwise use a measured magnetization curve. For ferromagnets distinguish initial magnetization, remanence and saturation. Never assume every metal is ferromagnetic or that a magnet remains unchanged after heating.

Worked example

A linear material with μᵣ = 2.0 is exposed to H = 1000 A/m. B = μ₀μᵣH ≈ 1.256637 · 10⁻⁶ · 2.0 · 1000 T = 2.51 · 10⁻³ T. Try it: if H halves within the same linear regime, find B. (1.26 mT).

Limits and common mistakes

A constant relative permeability is invalid for strongly nonlinear ferromagnets. Do not classify all metals as ferromagnets or ignore temperature, hysteresis and saturation.

Related theories

Magnetic Field and Magnetic Induction (#108); Magnetic Field of an Electric Current (#109); Self-Induction, Inductance and Magnetic Field Energy (#117)

113. Magnetic Flux

Origin

Faraday developed magnetic flux concepts while investigating induction in 1831. Later mathematical field theory put this idea into modern quantitative form.

Core idea

Picture a loop enclosing a surface and an imaginary arrow normal to that surface. When B is parallel to the normal, flux has maximum positive magnitude; when B lies in the surface plane, flux is zero. Reversing the chosen normal reverses the sign without changing the physical configuration. The magnetic field is a vector, so its direction matters as much as its strength.

Key terms and formulas

Φ = BS cos α for constant uniform B and flat surface S, with flux Φ in webers (Wb), B in T, area S in m², and α between B and surface normal; 1 Wb = 1 T·m². More generally Φ = ∫ B·dS over a surface. The sign depends on the normal direction chosen.

How to apply it

Draw the surface, add the perpendicular normal, and label the angle between normal and B. Convert cm² to m²; do not confuse angle to the surface with angle to the normal. Compute signed flux and check that the maximum magnitude is BS. Decide whether B, S or orientation changes between the initial and final situations.

Worked example

A loop encloses S = 0.020 m² in a uniform B = 0.30 T, with α = 60° to the normal. Φ = 0.30 T · 0.020 m² · cos 60° = 0.0030 Wb. Try it: rotate the loop so its normal is parallel to B. Find Φ. (0.0060 Wb).

Limits and common mistakes

Φ = BS cos α needs uniform B and a flat area. The angle is measured to the surface normal, not the plane; a negative flux indicates orientation, not a negative field strength.

Related theories

Magnetic Field and Magnetic Induction (#108); Electromagnetic Induction and Faraday's Law (#114); Lenz's Rule (#115)

114. Electromagnetic Induction and Faraday's Law

Origin

In 1831 Michael Faraday observed electromagnetic induction; Joseph Henry independently investigated related effects. Faraday's law was later expressed in mathematical form within electromagnetic field theory.

Core idea

Induction depends on how rapidly the magnetic flux changes, not on the magnitude of flux alone. No flux change means no induced EMF in a stationary circuit, even when the field is strong and constant. The minus sign in the law specifies the induced orientation according to Lenz's rule, expressing opposition to the change that creates the current.

Key terms and formulas

For one turn, ℰ = −ΔΦ/Δt as an average, where ℰ is EMF in V, flux Φ in Wb and time t in s. For a coil of N identical tightly coupled turns, ℰ = −NΔΦ/Δt. For instantaneous changes use the corresponding time derivative. EMF can exist in an open loop without a sustained current.

How to apply it

Mark a consistent surface normal and the positive direction around the loop. Calculate initial and final flux through each turn, then ΔΦ and elapsed time. Multiply by N, keep sign if direction matters, and use |ℰ| when asked only for magnitude. If resistance R is known for a closed loop, estimate current I = |ℰ|/R. Do not connect induction coils to unsafe voltage sources.

Worked example

A 100-turn coil has flux per turn increasing from 0.0010 Wb to 0.0030 Wb in 0.20 s. |ℰ| = 100(0.0030 − 0.0010) Wb/0.20 s = 1.0 V. Try it: if the change takes 0.10 s, find |ℰ|. (2.0 V).

Limits and common mistakes

Changing flux, not merely a nonzero magnetic field, induces EMF. The average formula uses a finite interval; rapid variation requires derivatives. An open circuit can have EMF without current.

Related theories

Magnetic Flux (#113); Lenz's Rule (#115); Induced EMF in a Moving Conductor (#116)

115. Lenz's Rule

Origin

Heinrich Lenz formulated the direction rule in 1834, shortly after Faraday's discovery of induction. It explains the negative sign in Faraday's law.

Core idea

A changing external flux drives a current whose own field acts against the flux change. If the original flux increases into the page, the induced field points out of the page; if it decreases into the page, the induced field points into the page. Conducting surfaces can sustain eddy currents that oppose relative motion, causing magnetic braking and heating.

Key terms and formulas

For a fixed orientation, ℰ = −NΔΦ/Δt, where ℰ in V, flux Φ in Wb, time t in s and N turns. The minus sign encodes direction rather than making the EMF magnitude negative. The induced magnetic field is chosen to resist the change of flux, not always to point opposite the external field.

How to apply it

Draw the external B through the loop and decide whether its flux grows or shrinks. Draw the induced field that opposes that specific change. Then use the right-hand rule around the loop to determine clockwise or counterclockwise current as viewed from the chosen side. If the conductor is open, an EMF can exist but the closed-path induced current does not.

Worked example

A field into the page through a loop increases from 0.10 T to 0.30 T. The induced field points out of the page, so the induced current is counterclockwise as viewed on the page. Try it: the into-page field decreases. Which direction is the induced current? (clockwise).

Limits and common mistakes

Opposition is to the change of flux, not always to the external field itself. The chosen viewing side controls clockwise labels; an open loop has no circulating current.

Related theories

Magnetic Flux (#113); Electromagnetic Induction and Faraday's Law (#114); Induced EMF in a Moving Conductor (#116)

116. Induced EMF in a Moving Conductor

Origin

Faraday's nineteenth-century experiments established motion-based electromagnetic induction. The magnetic force on charges provides a microscopic explanation of the generated voltage.

Core idea

Imagine a rod sliding along conducting rails in a uniform magnetic field normal to their plane. Electrons feel the magnetic Lorentz force, causing charge separation. The rod and rails enclose a changing area, so the same EMF follows from Faraday's flux law. Closing the rails with a resistor allows current and produces a magnetic force opposing the slide.

Key terms and formulas

For straight rod length l moving at velocity v, perpendicular to both rod and a uniform field B, |ℰ| = Blv, where ℰ in V, B in T, l in m and v in m/s. If the velocity forms angle α to B while rod orientation remains appropriate, |ℰ| = Blv sin α. General geometries require the velocity cross product integrated along the conductor.

How to apply it

Draw v, B and rod direction, then use the magnetic force on positive charges to find the higher-potential end. Confirm the perpendicular geometry before using Blv. If the rod is in a closed circuit, find I = ℰ/R for total resistance R. A force must supply mechanical work to maintain speed against electromagnetic braking.

Worked example

A rod of length l = 0.40 m slides at v = 3.0 m/s through B = 0.50 T perpendicular to its plane. |ℰ| = 0.50 T · 0.40 m · 3.0 m/s = 0.60 V. Try it: what EMF at half the speed? (0.30 V).

Limits and common mistakes

Blv assumes specified perpendicular geometry and a uniform field. Sliding contact resistance and changing speed alter actual current; magnetic force on charges is not along velocity.

Related theories

The Lorentz Force and Charged Particles in a Magnetic Field (#111); Electromagnetic Induction and Faraday's Law (#114); Lenz's Rule (#115)

117. Self-Induction, Inductance and Magnetic Field Energy

Origin

Joseph Henry and Michael Faraday studied electromagnetic self-induction in the nineteenth century. Henry's name is used for the SI unit of inductance.

Core idea

As current increases, the coil's field strengthens, inducing an EMF directed against the increase. As current decreases, the induced EMF attempts to keep current flowing. In a linear inductor the magnetic flux linkage is proportional to current; thus inductance depends on geometry, turns and core material. Magnetic energy stores the work required to build the current.

Key terms and formulas

ℰ = −LΔI/Δt for constant L, where EMF ℰ in V, inductance L in henries (H), current I in A and time t in s. For a linear inductor, energy E = LI²/2 in J, and flux linkage NΦ = LI in Wb·turn. 1 H = 1 V·s/A.

How to apply it

Identify whether current rises or falls, then use the negative sign for direction and absolute value for the EMF magnitude. Convert milliseconds to seconds and millihenries to henries. For energy questions square the current before multiplying by L/2. Avoid assuming L stays constant in a saturating magnetic core. Never open a high-current inductive circuit without protection against voltage spikes.

Worked example

An inductor of L = 0.20 H increases current from 0 A to 3.0 A in 0.10 s. |ℰ| = LΔI/Δt = 0.20 H · 3.0 A/0.10 s = 6.0 V. Final stored energy E = 0.20 H · (3.0 A)²/2 = 0.90 J. Try it: energy at 2.0 A? (0.40 J).

Limits and common mistakes

Linear inductance can change with core saturation. The induced EMF resists current change, not current itself; high-voltage spikes on opening may be dangerous.

Related theories

Magnetic Flux (#113); Electromagnetic Induction and Faraday's Law (#114); Lenz's Rule (#115)

118. Oscillatory Motion: Amplitude, Period and Frequency

Origin

Galileo investigated pendulum motion around the start of the seventeenth century. Huygens later developed pendulum clocks and refined the understanding of periodic motion.

Core idea

Oscillations require a restoring influence and inertia, so a disturbed system can pass through equilibrium rather than simply stop there. For ideal free oscillations, energy alternates between kinetic and potential forms. Friction reduces amplitude in real systems; a sustained periodic drive can maintain motion. The phase tells where the oscillator is within its cycle.

Key terms and formulas

T = t/N, ν = N/t = 1/T, ω = 2π/T = 2πν, where period T in seconds (s), elapsed time t in s, cycle count N dimensionless, frequency ν in hertz (Hz) and angular frequency ω in rad/s. Amplitude A uses the same unit as displacement, usually m. These definitions apply to periodic motion.

How to apply it

Choose a clear event, such as passage through equilibrium in the same direction, to mark a complete cycle. Count several cycles to reduce timing uncertainty, then divide total time by N. Determine amplitude from the equilibrium position, not from one turning point to the other. Distinguish ordinary frequency ν from angular frequency ω.

Worked example

A pendulum completes 15 cycles in 30.0 s. T = 30.0 s/15 = 2.00 s; ν = 15/30.0 s = 0.500 Hz; ω = 2π/2.00 s ≈ 3.14 rad/s. Try it: if 12 cycles last 24 s, find T. (2.0 s).

Limits and common mistakes

A real damped oscillator is not exactly periodic forever. A full cycle returns to the same state and direction; amplitude is half the peak-to-peak distance.

Related theories

Harmonic Oscillations (#119); Spring and Simple Pendulums (#120); Energy in Oscillations and Damping (#121)

119. Harmonic Oscillations

Origin

Harmonic motion was clarified through seventeenth-century work on pendulums and springs, particularly by Hooke and Huygens. It became central to wave theory and mechanical vibration analysis.

Core idea

An oscillator reaches its largest displacement at a turning point, where velocity is momentarily zero and acceleration is largest toward equilibrium. At equilibrium displacement and acceleration are zero while speed is largest. The phase sets the initial position and direction. Real systems are approximately harmonic only for certain amplitudes and small damping.

Key terms and formulas

x = A cos(ωt + φ₀), v = −Aω sin(ωt + φ₀), a = −ω²x, where x and amplitude A in m, angular frequency ω in rad/s, time t in s, phase φ₀ in radians, velocity v in m/s and acceleration a in m/s². Maximum speed v_max = Aω and acceleration magnitude a_max = Aω².

How to apply it

Identify amplitude and period, convert period to ω = 2π/T, then use the phase to determine the sign of displacement and velocity. For maximum quantities use Aω and Aω². Verify that acceleration always points toward equilibrium. Do not confuse amplitude with total peak-to-peak displacement or use degrees inside a radian-based expression without conversion.

Worked example

A harmonic oscillator has A = 0.050 m and T = 2.0 s. ω = 2π/2.0 s = π rad/s; v_max = Aω ≈ 0.157 m/s; a_max = Aω² ≈ 0.493 m/s². Try it: at x = +0.020 m, what acceleration is predicted? (−0.197 m/s²).

Limits and common mistakes

The sinusoidal model assumes linear restoring behavior and negligible damping. Acceleration is not maximal at equilibrium, and ω has rad/s rather than Hz.

Related theories

Uniform Circular Motion: Period, Frequency and Angular Velocity (#18); Oscillatory Motion: Amplitude, Period and Frequency (#118); Spring and Simple Pendulums (#120)

120. Spring and Simple Pendulums

Origin

Hooke established the proportional relation for elastic spring force in the seventeenth century. Huygens studied pendulums and their clocks, developing practical uses of nearly periodic motion.

Core idea

For a spring oscillator, stronger stiffness increases the restoring force and shortens the period; heavier mass increases inertia and lengthens it. For a simple pendulum, gravity provides a restoring torque, and at small angles sin θ ≈ θ makes its motion approximately harmonic. Large angles lengthen the actual pendulum period beyond the simple formula.

Key terms and formulas

Spring: T = 2π√(m/k), with mass m in kg, spring constant k in N/m and T in s. Simple pendulum: T = 2π√(l/g), with length l in m and acceleration g in m/s². Rearranging gives g = 4π²l/T². The pendulum approximation requires a pointlike bob, light inextensible string, small amplitude and negligible damping.

How to apply it

Identify the oscillator type before choosing its formula. For a pendulum measure length from pivot to the bob's center of mass and time multiple complete oscillations. Use SI units, g ≈ 9.8 m/s², and verify that doubling spring mass raises T by √2 while quadrupling pendulum length doubles T. Avoid applying the small-angle pendulum equation to wide swings.

Worked example

For a simple pendulum of l = 0.980 m near Earth's surface, T = 2π√(0.980 m / (9.8 m/s²)) = 2π√(0.100 s²) ≈ 1.99 s. Try it: if its length is multiplied by four, what is T? (3.97 s).

Limits and common mistakes

The simple pendulum formula fails at large angles and sizable damping. Measure length to the bob center, not its bottom; mass does not enter the ideal pendulum period.

Related theories

Elastic Force and Hooke's Law (#28); Oscillatory Motion: Amplitude, Period and Frequency (#118); Harmonic Oscillations (#119)

121. Energy in Oscillations and Damping

Origin

Energy conservation was formulated in the nineteenth century by several scientists including Joule and Helmholtz. Studies of damped springs and pendulums led to mathematical models of energy loss.

Core idea

For a freely oscillating ideal spring, total energy stays constant while elastic potential energy and kinetic energy alternate. With damping, the amplitude decreases and energy is transferred to the surroundings. Weak damping usually produces an exponentially shrinking amplitude, but a very strong damping can prevent repeated crossings of equilibrium.

Key terms and formulas

E = kA²/2 = kx²/2 + mv²/2, with spring stiffness k in N/m, amplitude A and displacement x in m, mass m in kg and speed v in m/s. For viscous damping F_d = −bv, b is measured in kg/s. These equations assume a linear spring, a fixed equilibrium and no external drive.

How to apply it

Draw the turning points and equilibrium. For an ideal spring evaluate total energy at maximum extension, then subtract spring energy at a chosen displacement to find kinetic energy. With friction do not set mechanical energies at two times equal; include dissipated energy. Distinguish amplitude from instantaneous displacement.

Worked example

A spring has k = 200 N/m and amplitude A = 0.10 m. E = 200 N/m · (0.10 m)² / 2 = 1.0 J. At x = 0.060 m, spring energy is 0.36 J and kinetic energy is 0.64 J. Try it: energy at x = A? (1.0 J potential, zero kinetic).

Limits and common mistakes

With strong friction, total mechanical energy is not conserved; energy becomes internal energy. Do not treat displacement as amplitude or assume every damped motion remains sinusoidal.

Related theories

Oscillatory Motion: Amplitude, Period and Frequency (#118); Harmonic Oscillations (#119); Spring and Simple Pendulums (#120)

122. Forced Oscillations and Resonance

Origin

Galileo described sympathetic oscillations; nineteenth-century vibration theory and later engineering clarified driven resonance. Resonance is not the complete explanation of every structural failure: aerodynamic instabilities also matter.

Core idea

A driven oscillator receives energy from an external source each cycle. Close to its natural frequency the energy transfer can balance damping at a relatively large amplitude. More damping makes the peak lower and broader. Away from resonance the amplitude usually becomes smaller. The steady oscillation follows the driving frequency, not necessarily the free frequency.

Key terms and formulas

For an ideal spring oscillator ω₀ = √(k/m), with k in N/m, m in kg and angular frequency ω₀ in rad/s. f₀ = ω₀/(2π) in Hz. A damped, driven model is m ẍ + b ẋ + kx = F₀ cos(ωt), where F₀ is forcing amplitude in N, b in kg/s and ω in rad/s.

How to apply it

Compute the natural frequency from the mass and stiffness. Compare the driving frequency using the same units, and check whether damping is small or large. Avoid claiming that the highest displacement must occur at exactly ω₀ for every damped model. Keep clear whether frequency is in hertz or radians per second.

Worked example

For k = 100 N/m and m = 1.0 kg, ω₀ = √(100/1.0) rad/s = 10 rad/s and f₀ = 10/(2π) Hz ≈ 1.59 Hz. A 1.6 Hz drive is near resonance. Try it: if mass becomes 4.0 kg, what is f₀? (0.796 Hz).

Limits and common mistakes

The ideal resonance estimate ignores the shift caused by damping. Do not equate structural aeroelastic instability with simple forced resonance, or confuse hertz with radians per second.

Related theories

Oscillatory Motion: Amplitude, Period and Frequency (#118); Spring and Simple Pendulums (#120); Energy in Oscillations and Damping (#121)

123. Mechanical Waves

Origin

Huygens and later wave theorists developed ways to describe propagation. Nineteenth-century studies of elasticity and sound established transverse and longitudinal mechanical wave models.

Core idea

Each part of a medium interacts with adjacent parts, allowing a disturbance to propagate. In a transverse wave displacement is perpendicular to propagation; in a longitudinal wave it is parallel. Individual particles often oscillate around equilibrium while energy travels farther. Mechanical waves require a medium and cannot travel in a perfect vacuum.

Key terms and formulas

Wave speed v = λf = λ/T, with wavelength λ in m, frequency f in Hz, period T in s and speed v in m/s. Frequency is set by the source; speed depends on medium conditions. Amplitude describes the maximum displacement, not the distance the wave covers.

How to apply it

Identify the medium and the direction of propagation. Compare the particle-motion direction to distinguish wave type. Use λ = v/f if speed and frequency are known, and check that a wave with higher frequency has a shorter wavelength in the same medium. Do not confuse the particle speed with the propagation speed.

Worked example

A string wave moves at v = 12 m/s and its frequency is f = 4.0 Hz. Its wavelength is λ = v/f = 12 m/s / 4.0 Hz = 3.0 m. Try it: what is λ at 6.0 Hz in the same string? (2.0 m).

Limits and common mistakes

Wave speed is not particle speed, and mechanical waves cannot propagate through vacuum. A real medium may disperse or absorb waves; do not assume every wave is transverse.

Related theories

Oscillatory Motion: Amplitude, Period and Frequency (#118); Wavelength, Frequency and Wave Speed (#124); Interference, Diffraction and Standing Waves (#125)

124. Wavelength, Frequency and Wave Speed

Origin

Wave researchers including Huygens, Young and Fourier helped establish the description of periodic disturbances using wavelength and frequency. Their methods now apply to sound, water waves and electromagnetic waves.

Core idea

Wavelength is the distance between neighboring points in the same phase, such as two neighboring crests. Frequency counts complete oscillations per second. The period is the duration of one cycle. In a fixed medium, speed usually depends on the medium rather than the source frequency, although dispersive media are exceptions.

Key terms and formulas

v = λf, f = 1/T and λ = vT. Units: speed v in m/s, wavelength λ in m, frequency f in Hz = s⁻¹ and period T in s. Angular frequency ω = 2πf in rad/s; wave number k = 2π/λ in rad/m.

How to apply it

Identify whether the problem gives a period, frequency or wavelength. Convert ms to s and kHz to Hz. Calculate the missing quantity with v = λf. If a wave enters another medium, its frequency normally stays unchanged while speed and wavelength may change.

Worked example

Sound travels at v = 340 m/s and has f = 170 Hz. λ = v/f = 340 m/s / 170 Hz = 2.0 m. The period is T = 1/170 Hz ≈ 0.00588 s. Try it: at f = 680 Hz, what is λ? (0.50 m).

Limits and common mistakes

A dispersive medium may give different speeds at different frequencies. Do not equate period with frequency or change frequency automatically at a stationary boundary.

Related theories

Mechanical Waves (#123); Sound Waves: Pitch, Loudness and Timbre (#126); Electromagnetic Waves (#133)

125. Interference, Diffraction and Standing Waves

Origin

Young demonstrated wave interference with light in the early nineteenth century, while earlier and contemporary studies of water and sound explored wave overlap. Standing waves became important in musical acoustics.

Core idea

At constructive interference, wave crests meet crests; at destructive interference, a crest meets a trough. Diffraction becomes conspicuous when an opening or obstacle is comparable to a wavelength. In a standing wave nodes have zero displacement at all times, while antinodes attain maximum amplitude. Energy is not steadily transported along an ideal standing wave.

Key terms and formulas

For coherent in-phase sources, path difference Δ = mλ gives maxima and Δ = (m + 1/2)λ gives minima, m = 0, 1, 2, ... . For a string fixed at both ends, L = nλ/2, fₙ = nv/(2L), n = 1, 2, 3, ... ; L and λ in m, v in m/s, f in Hz.

How to apply it

First determine whether two traveling waves overlap or a reflected wave forms a standing pattern. For two sources compare path difference with wavelength, including any initial phase difference. For a fixed string count half-wavelengths between endpoints; nodes occur at both fixed ends.

Worked example

A string fixed at both ends is 0.60 m long and wave speed is 120 m/s. For its first harmonic λ₁ = 2L = 1.20 m, f₁ = v/λ₁ = 120 m/s / 1.20 m = 100 Hz. Try it: what is the second harmonic? (200 Hz).

Limits and common mistakes

Simple interference maxima assume coherent waves and known relative phase. Fixed-string harmonics require ideal stationary endpoints. Never assume two arbitrary sources make stable fringes.

Related theories

Mechanical Waves (#123); Wavelength, Frequency and Wave Speed (#124); Interference of Light (#147)

126. Sound Waves: Pitch, Loudness and Timbre

Origin

Musical acoustics grew from ancient observations of vibrating strings. Helmholtz studied sound perception and tone quality in the nineteenth century, linking complex tones to their harmonics.

Core idea

A vibrating source creates alternating compressions and rarefactions in air. A higher fundamental frequency generally gives a higher pitch. Sound intensity is power per unit area; perceived loudness also depends on frequency and listener sensitivity. Timbre is shaped by the spectrum of harmonics and the way sound starts and fades.

Key terms and formulas

I = P/S, with sound power P in W, area S in m² and intensity I in W/m². Sound level β = 10 log₁₀(I/I₀) dB, with reference I₀ = 10⁻¹² W/m² in air; this is an intensity level, not a direct measure of perceived loudness. λ = v/f.

How to apply it

Determine whether a question concerns pitch, sound intensity or tone quality. Use frequency for pitch, intensity ratios for decibels, and harmonics for timbre. For doubling intensity, add about 3 dB, not 10 dB. Protect hearing from prolonged high sound levels.

Worked example

For I = 10⁻⁶ W/m² and I₀ = 10⁻¹² W/m², β = 10 log₁₀(10⁶) dB = 60 dB. Try it: if intensity rises tenfold, what is the new level? (70 dB).

Limits and common mistakes

Loudness is a human perception, not identical to intensity or the decibel number. Sound needs a medium, and prolonged strong sound can damage hearing.

Related theories

Mechanical Waves (#123); Wavelength, Frequency and Wave Speed (#124); The Doppler Effect (#127)

127. The Doppler Effect

Origin

Christian Doppler proposed the effect in 1842; later acoustic experiments established it clearly. Astronomers use a related effect to study motions of light-emitting objects.

Core idea

With a stationary observer, an approaching sound source emits successive crests from positions closer together, decreasing the observed wavelength and increasing frequency. Motion of the observer also changes how frequently crests are encountered. Signs matter, and the familiar sound formula assumes motion along a common line through a stationary medium.

Key terms and formulas

For sound f′ = f(v + vₒ)/(v − vₛ) if observer velocity vₒ is toward the source and source velocity vₛ is toward the observer. f and f′ in Hz; v, vₒ and vₛ in m/s. For a stationary observer and receding source use f′ = fv/(v + |vₛ|). This classical model differs from relativistic Doppler shift for light.

How to apply it

Draw source, observer and propagation direction. Mark explicitly who approaches whom. Use medium-frame velocities and confirm that approach raises the observed pitch. Avoid applying the ordinary sound equation to light in vacuum.

Worked example

A siren emits f = 500 Hz and approaches a stationary person at 20 m/s through still air with v = 340 m/s. f′ = 500 · 340/(340 − 20) Hz ≈ 531 Hz. Try it: what if it recedes at 20 m/s? (472 Hz).

Limits and common mistakes

The classical equation assumes a stationary medium and motion along one line. Use relative signs consistently; light in vacuum needs a relativistic treatment.

Related theories

Wavelength, Frequency and Wave Speed (#124); Sound Waves: Pitch, Loudness and Timbre (#126); Electromagnetic Waves (#133)

128. The LC Circuit and Electromagnetic Oscillations

Origin

Thomson derived the oscillation period of an LC circuit in the nineteenth century. Later radio engineers used resonant circuits to select frequencies.

Core idea

A charged capacitor initially stores energy in its electric field. On connection to an inductor, charge moves, generating a magnetic field. Induction opposes rapid changes of current, and energy flows back toward the capacitor. Real circuits contain resistance, so unpowered oscillations decay unless energy is supplied.

Key terms and formulas

Thomson formula: T = 2π√(LC), f = 1/(2π√(LC)); inductance L in henries (H), capacitance C in farads (F), T in s and f in Hz. Energies: E_C = CU²/2, E_L = LI²/2 with U in V and I in A. This assumes lumped, linear components and negligible resistance for the ideal period.

How to apply it

Convert mH and μF to henries and farads before multiplying. Check the square root in Thomson's formula. For ideal energy questions, set the maximum electric energy equal to the maximum magnetic energy. Do not treat the coil as a purely resistive element.

Worked example

A circuit has L = 0.10 H and C = 100 μF = 1.0 · 10⁻⁴ F. T = 2π√(0.10 · 1.0 · 10⁻⁴) s ≈ 0.0199 s; f ≈ 50.3 Hz. Try it: if C becomes four times larger, what happens to f? (It halves to about 25.2 Hz).

Limits and common mistakes

Real coils and capacitors have losses, and sustained oscillations require replenishing energy. Use the ideal LC period only when parasitic resistance is negligible.

Related theories

Capacitance and Capacitors (#92); Self-Induction, Inductance and Magnetic Field Energy (#117); Harmonic Oscillations (#119)

129. Alternating Current and Generators

Origin

Faraday discovered electromagnetic induction in 1831. Later alternating-current generators and power systems developed through work by many engineers, including Tesla and Westinghouse.

Core idea

When a conducting loop rotates in a magnetic field, the magnetic flux through it varies. Faraday's law produces an EMF whose polarity alternates each half turn. A sinusoidal source drives a sinusoidal current only in circuits with suitable linear components; current may be shifted in phase relative to voltage.

Key terms and formulas

For uniform rotation of an ideal coil, e(t) = E₀ sin(ωt), E₀ = NBAω, with N turns, field B in T, area A in m², angular speed ω in rad/s and EMF e in V. Frequency f = ω/(2π) in Hz and period T = 1/f in s.

How to apply it

Convert rotations per second to hertz and angular speed to radians per second. Identify the number of turns and area of one turn. Use the amplitude equation rather than treating every instant as a peak. Work only with safe low-voltage demonstrations; mains power can be lethal.

Worked example

A 100-turn coil of area 0.010 m² rotates at f = 50 Hz in B = 0.10 T. ω = 2π · 50 ≈ 314 rad/s, E₀ = 100 · 0.10 T · 0.010 m² · 314 rad/s ≈ 31.4 V. Try it: what if rotation frequency doubles? (Amplitude about 62.8 V).

Limits and common mistakes

Only the ideal generator gives a perfectly sinusoidal EMF. Frequency is not voltage amplitude, and household AC must never be used for an unprotected school experiment.

Related theories

Electromagnetic Induction and Faraday's Law (#114); The LC Circuit and Electromagnetic Oscillations (#128); Effective Values of Current and Voltage (#130)

130. Effective Values of Current and Voltage

Origin

RMS measurements grew from alternating-current engineering in the late nineteenth century. They allowed power calculations that do not depend on the instant at which a sinusoidal voltage is sampled.

Core idea

Alternating current varies during a cycle, so an ordinary time average of a symmetric sine wave is zero. Heating depends on the square of current, which stays nonnegative. Taking the average of that square and its square root gives a meaningful effective current or voltage.

Key terms and formulas

For a sinusoid U_rms = U₀/√2 and I_rms = I₀/√2; U₀ and I₀ are amplitudes in V and A. A resistor dissipates P_avg = U_rms I_rms = I_rms²R = U_rms²/R, with P in W and R in Ω. For nonsinusoidal waveforms, calculate RMS from the actual squared waveform, not simply divide peak by √2.

How to apply it

Identify whether a stated number is peak, peak-to-peak or RMS. For a sine wave, divide peak by √2 or multiply RMS by √2. Power formulas using RMS apply directly to pure resistance; with reactive loads account for phase and power factor. Household mains is dangerous even when a figure is described as an average.

Worked example

For a 230 V RMS sinusoidal supply, U₀ = 230 V · √2 ≈ 325 V. A 100 Ω resistor would dissipate P = (230 V)²/100 Ω = 529 W, well beyond a typical small resistor rating. Try it: RMS of a 10 V peak sinusoid? (7.07 V).

Limits and common mistakes

Peak/√2 holds for a sine wave, not arbitrary AC. In a reactive circuit average power also depends on phase. Never mistake RMS voltage for a safe peak.

Related theories

Alternating Current and Generators (#129); Resistors, Capacitors and Inductors in AC Circuits (#131); Transformers and Power Transmission (#132)

131. Resistors, Capacitors and Inductors in AC Circuits

Origin

AC circuit analysis grew through nineteenth-century work by engineers and mathematicians including Steinmetz, who developed practical complex-number methods for sinusoidal power circuits.

Core idea

For a resistor, voltage and current are in phase. In a pure capacitor, current leads voltage by a quarter cycle. In a pure inductor, current lags voltage by a quarter cycle. In a series RLC circuit, inductive and capacitive reactances oppose each other and cancel at the resonance frequency.

Key terms and formulas

X_L = ωL and X_C = 1/(ωC), measured in Ω; ω = 2πf in rad/s, L in H, C in F. Series impedance magnitude Z = √(R² + (X_L − X_C)²) in Ω, with I_rms = U_rms/Z in A. Series resonance ω₀ = 1/√(LC), if lumped ideal components are used.

How to apply it

Convert f to angular frequency and all units to SI. Calculate X_L and X_C separately, subtract in the correct order, then combine with R geometrically. Compare whether current leads or lags according to which reactance dominates. Do not add reactances as plain resistances.

Worked example

A series circuit has f = 50 Hz, R = 30 Ω, L = 0.10 H and C = 100 μF. ω ≈ 314 rad/s, X_L ≈ 31.4 Ω, X_C ≈ 31.8 Ω, Z ≈ √(30² + (31.4 − 31.8)²) Ω ≈ 30.0 Ω. Try it: what is Z at exact resonance? (30 Ω).

Limits and common mistakes

These impedance expressions assume steady sinusoidal operation with linear components. Do not add reactances as ordinary positive resistances or omit phase shift.

Related theories

132. Transformers and Power Transmission

Origin

Faraday established induction in 1831; practical transformers and alternating-current networks developed in the late nineteenth century.

Core idea

Changing current in the primary winding creates changing magnetic flux through the core. The flux induces voltage in the secondary winding. If the secondary has more turns, its voltage is higher, while its current is lower for approximately conserved power. Real transformers have copper losses, core losses and leakage. They require changing flux rather than steady DC operation.

Key terms and formulas

Ideal transformer U₁/U₂ = N₁/N₂ and U₁I₁ = U₂I₂, with voltage U in V, current I in A and turns N dimensionless. Efficiency η = P_out/P_in. Wire heating loss P_loss = I²R with I in A and wire resistance R in Ω.

How to apply it

Compare turns first to decide step-up or step-down action. Determine the ideal secondary voltage, then use power balance to estimate current. In real devices adjust for efficiency; do not claim step-up increases energy. Never dismantle or handle mains-connected transformers.

Worked example

A transformer has N₁ = 1000 turns, N₂ = 100 turns and U₁ = 230 V AC. U₂ = 230 V · 100/1000 = 23 V. For an ideal 2.0 A secondary load, P₂ = 23 V · 2.0 A = 46 W and I₁ = 46 W / 230 V = 0.20 A. Try it: double N₂. What is U₂? (46 V).

Limits and common mistakes

The ideal ratio neglects copper, core and leakage losses. A transformer cannot produce extra energy and does not transform a steady DC supply continuously.

Related theories

Electromagnetic Induction and Faraday's Law (#114); Alternating Current and Generators (#129); Effective Values of Current and Voltage (#130)

133. Electromagnetic Waves

Origin

Maxwell predicted electromagnetic waves through his field equations in the 1860s. Hertz produced and detected radio waves in experiments during the late 1880s, confirming the prediction.

Core idea

A changing electric field is related to a changing magnetic field, allowing a wave to propagate. In a plane wave in vacuum the electric and magnetic fields are mutually perpendicular and each is perpendicular to propagation. Their changing pattern travels at the speed of light. Electromagnetic waves need no material medium.

Key terms and formulas

In vacuum c = λf, where c = 299 792 458 m/s exactly, wavelength λ in m and frequency f in Hz. Field amplitudes obey E₀ = cB₀, with E₀ in V/m and B₀ in T, for a plane wave in vacuum. In materials propagation speed depends on their electromagnetic properties.

How to apply it

Identify the medium and frequency. Use c for vacuum, or the stated phase speed for a material. Convert MHz and GHz to Hz, then find wavelength. Do not treat mechanical and electromagnetic waves as both requiring matter.

Worked example

For f = 100 MHz = 1.00 · 10⁸ Hz, λ = c/f ≈ 3.00 · 10⁸ m/s / 1.00 · 10⁸ Hz = 3.00 m. Try it: what is the vacuum wavelength at 1.0 GHz? (0.300 m).

Limits and common mistakes

A plane wave in vacuum is an ideal model; media may absorb and disperse fields. Electromagnetic waves do not need matter, unlike sound waves.

Related theories

Wavelength, Frequency and Wave Speed (#124); The Electromagnetic Spectrum (#135); Light: Its Nature and Speed (#136)

134. Principles of Radio Communication

Origin

Several pioneers contributed to early wireless communication. Popov demonstrated radio reception in the 1890s, and Marconi developed practical wireless telegraphy systems; assigning the entire invention to one person oversimplifies history.

Core idea

In amplitude modulation, the carrier amplitude follows a signal; in frequency modulation, the instantaneous carrier frequency varies. Antennas convert time-varying electrical currents into waves and incoming waves into electrical signals. Tuning selects the desired frequency, while demodulation reconstructs the message. Radar transmits waves and receives echoes.

Key terms and formulas

Radio wavelength in free space is λ = c/f, with c ≈ 3.00 · 10⁸ m/s, f in Hz and λ in m. A simple echo-ranging estimate is d = cΔt/2, where Δt is round-trip delay in s and d in m. This assumes propagation approximately at c and a reflected signal.

How to apply it

Identify carrier frequency before calculating wavelength. If measuring a radar echo, remember that the signal travels to the target and back. Distinguish carrier frequency from the lower information-signal frequency. Use approved transmitter equipment and frequencies.

Worked example

An FM station transmits near f = 100 MHz = 1.00 · 10⁸ Hz. λ = c/f ≈ 3.00 · 10⁸ m/s / 1.00 · 10⁸ Hz = 3.00 m. Try it: a radar echo returns after 20 μs; how far away is the reflector? (3.0 km).

Limits and common mistakes

Range and reception depend on terrain, antennas, attenuation and interference, not wavelength alone. Echo time must be halved for distance; emission is subject to radio regulations.

Related theories

Interference, Diffraction and Standing Waves (#125); Electromagnetic Waves (#133); The Electromagnetic Spectrum (#135)

135. The Electromagnetic Spectrum

Origin

Discoveries of infrared by Herschel in 1800, ultraviolet by Ritter in 1801 and X-rays by Röntgen in 1895 expanded the known spectrum beyond visible light. Gamma radiation was studied after the discovery of radioactivity.

Core idea

Moving from radio toward gamma radiation, frequency and photon energy increase while wavelength decreases. Radio supports communication; microwaves serve communication and heating; infrared is associated with thermal emission; visible light supports sight. Some ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma radiation can ionize matter and require appropriate protection.

Key terms and formulas

In vacuum c = λf and photon energy E = hf, where c = 299 792 458 m/s, wavelength λ in m, frequency f in Hz, Planck constant h = 6.62607015 · 10⁻³⁴ J·s and energy E in J. Band boundaries are approximate, and frequency alone does not specify biological harm.

How to apply it

Convert nm or μm into meters; compute frequency from wavelength, then energy if needed. Identify risks by photon energy, intensity and exposure rather than assuming every UV photon ionizes matter. Never expose eyes or skin to intense ultraviolet, X-ray or gamma sources.

Worked example

Green light with λ = 500 nm = 5.00 · 10⁻⁷ m has f = c/λ ≈ 6.00 · 10¹⁴ Hz and E = hf ≈ 3.98 · 10⁻¹⁹ J. Try it: what is the frequency at 600 nm? (5.00 · 10¹⁴ Hz).

Limits and common mistakes

Spectrum boundaries overlap by convention. Biological risk depends on photon energy, dose and exposure. Not all ultraviolet light is ionizing and nonionizing exposure can also harm tissue.

Related theories

Electromagnetic Waves (#133); Light: Its Nature and Speed (#136); Thermal Radiation and Planck's Hypothesis (#154)

136. Light: Its Nature and Speed

Origin

Newton developed a corpuscular model; Huygens developed a wave explanation. Rømer estimated light's finite speed from astronomical observations in 1676, and Fizeau measured it by terrestrial methods in 1849. Modern quantum physics unites wave and particle behavior.

Core idea

Light travels in vacuum without a supporting material. Wave descriptions explain interference and diffraction; photons explain discrete energy exchange. Its frequency stays the same when crossing a stationary boundary, but speed and wavelength can change in transparent materials. A light-year measures distance, not time.

Key terms and formulas

Speed in vacuum c = 299 792 458 m/s exactly; in a medium v = c/n where n is refractive index, and v = λf. A light-year is the distance traveled by light in one Julian year, approximately 9.46 · 10¹⁵ m. Speed v in m/s, wavelength λ in m and frequency f in Hz.

How to apply it

Choose vacuum or the specified medium and convert units carefully. For a travel-time problem use t = d/v. For a medium use v = c/n, not the vacuum speed blindly. Do not treat a light-year as a unit of time.

Worked example

Take Earth–Sun distance d = 1.50 · 10¹¹ m. The light-travel time is t = d/c ≈ 1.50 · 10¹¹ m / 3.00 · 10⁸ m/s = 500 s ≈ 8.33 min. Try it: time for light to travel 300 km in vacuum? (About 1.00 ms).

Limits and common mistakes

Use c only for vacuum. A light-year is distance, not time; classical ray optics does not explain all interference or quantum effects.

Related theories

Electromagnetic Waves (#133); The Electromagnetic Spectrum (#135); Refraction of Light and Snell's Law (#140)

137. Rectilinear Propagation of Light, Shadows and Eclipses

Origin

Ray optics developed from ancient studies of shadows and vision. Astronomers explained eclipse geometry long before modern wave theory; geometric optics remains a useful approximation for everyday objects.

Core idea

A point light source casts a sharp geometric shadow. A source with visible size allows some rays to reach points near the shadow edge, forming a penumbra. During a solar eclipse the Moon's shadow falls on Earth; during a lunar eclipse Earth shadows the Moon. Light can diffract near tiny obstacles, so perfectly sharp rays are only an approximation.

Key terms and formulas

For similar triangles in a point-source arrangement, shadow size/object size = distance(source, screen)/distance(source, object). All distances are measured in m. A solar eclipse requires an approximately aligned new Moon; a lunar eclipse requires an approximately aligned full Moon, but the Moon's orbit is tilted so eclipses do not occur every month.

How to apply it

Draw the source, obstacle and screen to scale where possible. Trace limiting rays from source edges to locate umbra and penumbra. Use similar triangles for numerical sizes. Never observe the Sun directly without certified solar filters.

Worked example

A point lamp is 1.0 m from a 0.20 m tall object, and a screen is 3.0 m from the lamp. Similar triangles give shadow height h = 0.20 m · 3.0/1.0 = 0.60 m. Try it: if the screen is 2.0 m from the lamp, how high is the shadow? (0.40 m).

Limits and common mistakes

Straight rays are an approximation when diffraction is negligible. Extended sources give penumbrae, and lunar orbital tilt prevents an eclipse at every new or full Moon.

Related theories

Light: Its Nature and Speed (#136); Reflection of Light and Plane Mirrors (#138); Refraction of Light and Snell's Law (#140)

138. Reflection of Light and Plane Mirrors

Origin

Reflection laws were studied in antiquity and later became standard parts of geometric optics. The plane-mirror model follows directly from straight-ray construction and equal-angle reflection.

Core idea

The incident ray, reflected ray and normal lie in one plane. Their angles to the normal are equal, not their angles to the mirror surface. Reflected rays from a plane mirror diverge as if they came from a point behind it; no light actually gathers at that virtual image point.

Key terms and formulas

Law of reflection: θᵢ = θᵣ, both angles in degrees or radians measured relative to the normal. For a plane mirror, image distance dᵢ equals object distance dₒ in magnitude, and lateral magnification m = +1. A mirror rotated by angle α rotates the reflected direction by 2α for a fixed incoming ray.

How to apply it

Draw the normal at the point where light hits the mirror, not along the mirror itself. Measure both angles from the normal. Extend reflected rays backward with dashed construction lines to locate the virtual image. Do not put the plane-mirror image on the mirror surface.

Worked example

A person stands 1.5 m from a plane mirror. The virtual image appears 1.5 m behind it, so the person-to-image separation is 1.5 m + 1.5 m = 3.0 m. Try it: separation for a person 2.0 m from the mirror? (4.0 m).

Limits and common mistakes

The law assumes specular reflection from a smooth interface. Rough materials scatter light; image position is virtual, not a physical point where rays meet.

Related theories

Light: Its Nature and Speed (#136); Rectilinear Propagation of Light, Shadows and Eclipses (#137); Spherical Mirrors (#139)

139. Spherical Mirrors

Origin

Curved mirrors have been used since antiquity, while systematic image construction was refined through geometric optics. Their applications include telescopes, cosmetic mirrors and road mirrors.

Core idea

For paraxial rays near the principal axis, a spherical mirror behaves approximately as though parallel rays meet at a single focal point. A concave mirror has positive focal length under the common real-is-positive convention; a convex mirror has negative focal length. Rays far from the axis suffer spherical aberration and do not converge at precisely one point.

Key terms and formulas

Mirror equation 1/f = 1/dₒ + 1/dᵢ and magnification m = −dᵢ/dₒ, with focal length f and distances dₒ, dᵢ in m; use dᵢ positive for a real image and negative for a virtual image. For paraxial spherical mirrors f ≈ R/2, with radius R in m.

How to apply it

Identify concave or convex geometry, choose a consistent distance-sign convention, then use the mirror equation. Check whether the calculated image is real or virtual. Draw a parallel ray and a ray through the center of curvature. Do not use f = R/2 accurately for rays far from the principal axis.

Worked example

A concave mirror has f = 0.20 m, with an object dₒ = 0.60 m away. 1/dᵢ = 1/0.20 − 1/0.60 = 3.33 m⁻¹, so dᵢ = 0.30 m, m = −0.30/0.60 = −0.50. Try it: image for dₒ = 0.40 m? (dᵢ = 0.40 m).

Limits and common mistakes

The spherical-mirror model assumes paraxial rays. Off-axis rays have aberration; always apply one consistent sign convention for real and virtual images.

Related theories

Reflection of Light and Plane Mirrors (#138); Lenses and Optical Power (#142); Image Formation by Thin Lenses (#143)

140. Refraction of Light and Snell's Law

Origin

Snell determined the sine-law relationship in the seventeenth century; earlier geometric results were also known to Ibn Sahl. Refraction explains lenses and apparent shifts of underwater objects.

Core idea

At a boundary, the phase of the electromagnetic wave must match along the interface. This gives a change in propagation direction when phase speed changes. If light strikes along the normal there is no change of direction, although its speed and wavelength may change. Dispersion makes refractive index depend on wavelength.

Key terms and formulas

Snell's law n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂; n = c/v where c is vacuum light speed and v is phase speed in the material. Angles θ₁, θ₂ are measured from the normal, indices n are dimensionless, speeds are in m/s. At fixed frequency λ₂/λ₁ = v₂/v₁ = n₁/n₂.

How to apply it

Draw the interface and normal, then label which medium has greater refractive index. Solve for sin θ₂ and ensure its value does not exceed one. Use degrees consistently and remember light frequency does not change at the boundary. Do not confuse refraction with reflection.

Worked example

Light goes from air n₁ = 1.00 into glass n₂ = 1.50 at θ₁ = 30°. sin θ₂ = (1.00/1.50) sin 30° = 1/3; θ₂ ≈ 19.5°. Try it: angle in glass for normal incidence? (0°).

Limits and common mistakes

Snell’s law presumes locally uniform media and a boundary. Refractive index can depend on color; total internal reflection may occur when light goes into a lower-index medium.

Related theories

Light: Its Nature and Speed (#136); Reflection of Light and Plane Mirrors (#138); Total Internal Reflection (#141)

141. Total Internal Reflection

Origin

The critical-angle condition follows from Snell's refraction law, developed in the seventeenth century. Later optical-fibre technology exploited repeated internal reflections to transmit light.

Core idea

At the critical angle the refracted ray would travel along the boundary. Above it light is reflected back into the higher-index medium. An evanescent field still extends a short distance across the interface, so total reflection does not mean the field there is strictly zero.

Key terms and formulas

n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂; sin θc = n₂/n₁ when n₁ > n₂. Angles θ are measured from the normal; refractive indices n are dimensionless. For glass to air, n₁ ≈ 1.50 and n₂ ≈ 1.00.

How to apply it

Identify the incident medium and compare both indices. Compute θc = arcsin(n₂/n₁); compare the actual incidence angle with θc. Draw the normal, not the surface, as the angle reference.

Worked example

For glass n₁ = 1.50 and air n₂ = 1.00, sin θc = 1.00/1.50 = 0.667; θc ≈ 41.8°. At 50°, total internal reflection is possible. Try it: is there a critical angle for light from air into glass? (No.)

Limits and common mistakes

Only higher-to-lower-index transitions have a critical angle; it does not apply to air-to-glass. Surface coatings and close interfaces may permit frustrated total internal reflection.

Related theories

Refraction of Light and Snell's Law (#140); Light: Its Nature and Speed (#136); Optical Instruments (#145)

142. Lenses and Optical Power

Origin

Lensmaking predates modern science; seventeenth-century geometrical optics clarified image formation. Spectacles became widely used long before detailed physical models of lenses.

Core idea

The principal axis passes through the optical centre. The focal length is the signed distance from the optical centre to the principal focus. A positive focal length describes a converging lens in air; a negative one describes a diverging lens.

Key terms and formulas

D = 1/f, with focal length f in metres and optical power D in dioptres (m⁻¹). For thin lenses in contact, Dtotal = D₁ + D₂. Lens shape and refractive index determine f; formulas assume the paraxial approximation.

How to apply it

Identify lens type from the sign of f. Convert centimetres to metres before taking the reciprocal. If thin lenses are in contact, add powers first and invert once.

Worked example

A converging lens has f = +0.25 m, so D = 1/(0.25 m) = +4.0 dpt. A lens of −2.0 dpt in contact gives Dtotal = +2.0 dpt, ftotal = +0.50 m. Try it: power of f = −0.50 m? (−2.0 dpt.)

Limits and common mistakes

Power addition assumes thin lenses in contact, not separated thick lenses. Keep the sign of the diverging lens negative and convert cm into m.

Related theories

Refraction of Light and Snell's Law (#140); Image Formation by Thin Lenses (#143); The Eye and Vision Correction (#144)

143. Image Formation by Thin Lenses

Origin

Geometric ray constructions developed with seventeenth-century optics; later lens equations provided a compact calculation method used in cameras and spectacles.

Core idea

A ray through the optical centre travels approximately straight; a ray parallel to the axis passes through the far focal point or appears to diverge from a near focus; a ray directed through the near focus emerges parallel. Real images can be projected; virtual images cannot be projected directly.

Key terms and formulas

Use signed distances 1/f = 1/dₒ + 1/dᵢ, with positive f for converging lenses and negative f for diverging lenses; dₒ > 0 for a real object, dᵢ > 0 for a real image and dᵢ < 0 for a virtual image. Magnification m = −dᵢ/dₒ; lengths are in metres.

How to apply it

Sketch the three rays first. Choose and state the sign convention. Rearrange 1/dᵢ = 1/f − 1/dₒ, then use m to find image size and orientation.

Worked example

A converging lens has f = +0.10 m and an object at dₒ = 0.30 m. 1/dᵢ = 10 m⁻¹ − 3.33 m⁻¹ = 6.67 m⁻¹; dᵢ = +0.15 m. m = −0.15/0.30 = −0.50, so the image is inverted and half-size. Try it: where is an object at 2f imaged? (At 2f on the other side.)

Limits and common mistakes

The thin-lens model is paraxial and ignores aberrations. State your sign convention: a negative image distance indicates a virtual image, not a negative physical distance.

Related theories

Lenses and Optical Power (#142); Reflection of Light and Plane Mirrors (#138); The Eye and Vision Correction (#144)

144. The Eye and Vision Correction

Origin

Early spectacles developed over centuries. Nineteenth-century physiological optics connected eye anatomy, focusing and optical defects to geometric imaging.

Core idea

The cornea supplies most focusing power; the crystalline lens changes shape with accommodation. In a myopic relaxed eye, distant images tend to form in front of the retina, so a diverging correction is used. Hyperopia often requires a converging correction. Astigmatism involves direction-dependent focusing.

Key terms and formulas

Lens power D = 1/f in dpt for f in metres. An approximate relaxed-eye distance correction for a far point R metres away is D ≈ −1/R, ignoring vertex distance and other eye details. A conventional near point for a young normal eye is about 0.25 m.

How to apply it

Decide whether the problem concerns distance or near vision. Identify far or near point and lens sign, then calculate dioptres. Clinical prescriptions must be determined by an eye-care professional.

Worked example

If a person's far point is 0.50 m, the approximate distance-glasses power is D = −1/(0.50 m) = −2.0 dpt. Try it: positive or negative lens for simple hyperopia? (Positive, converging.)

Limits and common mistakes

Eye optics is more complex than a single thin lens. Far-point estimates do not replace eye examination, and age changes accommodation.

Related theories

Lenses and Optical Power (#142); Image Formation by Thin Lenses (#143); Optical Instruments (#145)

145. Optical Instruments

Origin

The early seventeenth century saw rapid development of telescopes and microscopes. Later improved lenses, mirrors and photography expanded their applications.

Core idea

A magnifier uses a converging lens and an object inside its focal length to create a larger virtual image. A compound microscope combines an objective and eyepiece. A refracting telescope uses lenses; a reflecting telescope uses a curved mirror to collect light.

Key terms and formulas

For relaxed-eye viewing, an ideal magnifier has angular magnification M ≈ 0.25 m/f, where f is focal length in metres and 0.25 m is conventional near-point distance. Telescope angular magnification magnitude is |M| ≈ fobjective/feyepiece for a simple astronomical telescope.

How to apply it

Identify whether the instrument produces a real intermediate image or a virtual final image. Draw the objective, eyepiece and light path, then calculate angular rather than linear magnification when appropriate.

Worked example

A magnifier with f = 0.050 m has M ≈ 0.25 m/0.050 m = 5 for relaxed-eye viewing. Try it: which part of a telescope chiefly gathers light? (The objective or primary mirror.)

Limits and common mistakes

Angular and linear magnification are different. Simple formulas assume ideal thin lenses and relaxed-eye viewing; aberrations and diffraction limit real instruments.

Related theories

Lenses and Optical Power (#142); Image Formation by Thin Lenses (#143); Polarization of Light (#149)

146. Dispersion of Light and Color

Origin

Isaac Newton demonstrated with prisms in the 1660s that white sunlight contains a range of colors, rather than a prism creating those colors from nothing.

Core idea

Refractive index usually depends on wavelength, so different spectral components emerge at different angles. A rainbow arises from refraction, internal reflection and dispersion in water droplets. Pigments subtract wavelengths from incident light, whereas an RGB display adds light from its pixels.

Key terms and formulas

n(λ) = c/vphase(λ). In vacuum, c = λν, where wavelength λ is in metres and frequency ν in hertz. Visible wavelengths are roughly 380–750 nm; the exact perceived boundaries are gradual.

How to apply it

Identify whether light sources are being mixed or whether pigments filter light. For a prism, apply Snell's law separately to each wavelength. For frequency conversion, first express nm as metres.

Worked example

For green light λ = 500 nm = 5.00 · 10⁻⁷ m, ν = c/λ ≈ (3.00 · 10⁸ m/s)/(5.00 · 10⁻⁷ m) = 6.00 · 10¹⁴ Hz. Try it: does frequency change when light enters glass? (No.)

Limits and common mistakes

Different displays and pigments do not share the same color-mixing rule. A medium changes wavelength but not frequency at a stationary interface.

Related theories

Refraction of Light and Snell's Law (#140); The Electromagnetic Spectrum (#135); Light: Its Nature and Speed (#136)

147. Interference of Light

Origin

Thomas Young demonstrated optical interference in the early nineteenth century. Thin-film interference and Newton's rings also became important evidence for wave optics.

Core idea

Constructive interference occurs when two coherent waves arrive in phase; destructive interference occurs when their phase difference is an odd half-cycle. Coherence means the phase relation stays sufficiently stable during observation. Film reflections can add an extra phase reversal.

Key terms and formulas

For coherent in-phase sources, maxima occur at path difference Δ = kλ, minima at Δ = (k + 1/2)λ, where integer k gives interference order and λ is wavelength. For small double-slit angles, fringe spacing Δy ≈ λL/d, where L is screen distance and d is slit spacing, both in metres.

How to apply it

Identify whether the waves arrive initially in phase and check for reflection phase changes. In Young's setup convert nanometres and millimetres to metres, then use Δy = λL/d.

Worked example

With λ = 600 nm, L = 2.0 m and d = 0.30 mm, Δy = (6.00 · 10⁻⁷ m)(2.0 m)/(3.0 · 10⁻⁴ m) = 0.0040 m = 4.0 mm. Try it: phase relation for a dark fringe from equal-amplitude beams? (Opposite phase.)

Limits and common mistakes

Fringe formulas require sufficient coherence and small angles. Film reflections may introduce a half-cycle phase change, reversing bright and dark conditions.

Related theories

Light: Its Nature and Speed (#136); Dispersion of Light and Color (#146); Diffraction of Light and the Diffraction Grating (#148)

148. Diffraction of Light and the Diffraction Grating

Origin

Nineteenth-century wave optics, especially work by Fresnel and Fraunhofer, explained diffraction patterns. Engineered gratings later became central to optical spectroscopy.

Core idea

Each grating opening sends out waves; only certain directions make adjacent contributions reinforce one another. Narrow apertures also impose a resolution limit on optical instruments. The colors reflected from a CD result partly from its closely spaced tracks acting as a grating.

Key terms and formulas

For normal incidence, d sin θ = mλ, where grating period d and wavelength λ are in metres, diffraction order m is an integer and θ is the angle to the normal. For a circular aperture the approximate first-dark-ring angle is θ ≈ 1.22λ/D for small angles, with diameter D in metres.

How to apply it

Compute d from line density, converting lines per millimetre into reciprocal metres. Solve sin θ = mλ/d and verify that its magnitude does not exceed one.

Worked example

A grating has 500 lines/mm, so d = 1/(5.00 · 10⁵ m⁻¹) = 2.00 · 10⁻⁶ m. For λ = 500 nm and order m = 1, sin θ = 0.250; θ ≈ 14.5°. Try it: can order m = 5 occur here? (No, because sin θ would exceed one.)

Limits and common mistakes

The grating formula assumes normal incidence and coherent illumination. Not every order exists: |mλ/d| must be at most 1. Aperture size sets resolution limits.

Related theories

Light: Its Nature and Speed (#136); Interference of Light (#147); Optical Instruments (#145)

149. Polarization of Light

Origin

Étienne-Louis Malus investigated polarized light in 1808, and nineteenth-century wave theory explained it as evidence that light waves are transverse.

Core idea

In linearly polarized light, the electric field oscillates along one fixed direction perpendicular to propagation. A polarizer transmits the component along its transmission axis. Rotating an analyzer changes transmitted intensity; polarization also enables some display and 3D-viewing systems.

Key terms and formulas

Malus's law for ideal linearly polarized input is I = I₀ cos² θ, with θ the angle between polarization and analyzer axis and intensity I in W/m². An ideal first polarizer transmits half the intensity of unpolarized light.

How to apply it

Determine whether incoming light is polarized. If initially unpolarized, halve its intensity at an ideal first polarizer. Then use the squared cosine of the angle between successive axes.

Worked example

Unpolarized light has I₀ = 100 W/m². After an ideal first polarizer I₁ = 50 W/m². A second at 60° gives I₂ = 50 W/m² · cos²60° = 12.5 W/m². Try it: second analyzer at 90°? (0 W/m² ideally.)

Limits and common mistakes

Malus’s law assumes ideal polarizers and linearly polarized input. Unpolarized light first loses half its intensity in an ideal polarizer; real filters absorb more.

Related theories

Electromagnetic Waves (#133); Light: Its Nature and Speed (#136); Interference of Light (#147)

150. Postulates of Special Relativity

Origin

Albert Einstein formulated special relativity in 1905. The earlier Michelson–Morley experiment found no expected ether-wind effect, motivating changes to classical ideas about light propagation.

Core idea

Space and time coordinates depend on the observer's inertial frame, but the spacetime description is consistent across frames. Ordinary Galilean addition would incorrectly predict different vacuum light speeds. Changing frames also changes which spatially separated events share a time coordinate.

Key terms and formulas

c = 299 792 458 m/s exactly in vacuum. For relative speed v, the Lorentz factor γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²) is dimensionless and applies for |v| < c. At v much smaller than c, γ is close to 1.

How to apply it

First identify two inertial observers and their relative speed. Compare v/c before choosing classical or relativistic relations. Distinguish the measured speed of light in vacuum from a light signal's speed in matter.

Worked example

For v = 0.60c, γ = 1/√(1 − 0.60²) = 1/0.80 = 1.25. Try it: does an inertial observer measure vacuum light traveling at c + v? (No, at c.)

Limits and common mistakes

The postulates apply to inertial frames in flat spacetime; accelerated observers and gravity need more care. The constant c is vacuum speed, not light speed in glass.

Related theories

Relative Motion and Velocity Addition (#14); Time Dilation and Length Contraction (#151); Relativistic Velocity Addition (#152)

151. Time Dilation and Length Contraction

Origin

Einstein derived these consequences of special relativity in 1905. Measurements using particle lifetimes and precision clocks have repeatedly confirmed relativistic timing; GPS navigation also requires corrections from general relativity.

Core idea

Proper time Δτ is measured by one clock present at both events. Proper length L₀ is measured in the object's rest frame. An observer seeing the clock and rod move obtains a longer interval between clock ticks and a shorter parallel length; transverse dimensions do not contract.

Key terms and formulas

γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²); Δt = γΔτ; L = L₀/γ. Speeds v and c are in m/s; times in seconds and lengths in metres. These relations compare inertial frames with constant relative speed.

How to apply it

Identify which clock is at rest with respect to both measured events. For length, establish which frame contains the stationary rod and measure its endpoints simultaneously in the observing frame. Use the same γ consistently.

Worked example

At v = 0.80c, γ = 1/√(1 − 0.64) = 5/3 ≈ 1.67. A rod with L₀ = 3.0 m has observed parallel length L = 3.0 m/(5/3) = 1.8 m. Try it: observed interval for proper time 6.0 s? (10.0 s.)

Limits and common mistakes

Do not confuse proper time with time measured in an arbitrary frame. Length contraction acts along motion; acceleration and gravity need additional analysis.

Related theories

Postulates of Special Relativity (#150); Relativistic Velocity Addition (#152); Mass–Energy Equivalence (#153)

152. Relativistic Velocity Addition

Origin

Einstein's 1905 special relativity replaced Galilean velocity addition at speeds approaching light speed. The rule follows from Lorentz transformations between inertial frames.

Core idea

If a spacecraft moves relative to Earth and launches a probe forward, Earth's observed probe speed is not the simple sum of speeds measured in separate frames. The denominator accounts for how time and space coordinates transform together.

Key terms and formulas

For collinear speeds, u = (u′ + v)/(1 + u′v/c²), where u′ is object velocity in the moving frame, v is that frame's velocity relative to the observer, and u is the observed velocity. All velocities use m/s and signed directions.

How to apply it

Choose a common positive axis. Convert velocities to fractions of c, including minus signs for opposite directions. Substitute into the denominator and verify |u| < c for massive objects.

Worked example

A ship moves at v = 0.60c and emits a probe forward at u′ = 0.50c relative to it. u = (0.60c + 0.50c)/(1 + 0.60 · 0.50) = 1.10c/1.30 ≈ 0.846c. Try it: what if u′ = c? (u = c.)

Limits and common mistakes

The stated expression covers velocities along one axis. At low speeds the correction is tiny; for transverse motion use full relativistic transformation rules.

Related theories

Relative Motion and Velocity Addition (#14); Postulates of Special Relativity (#150); Time Dilation and Length Contraction (#151)

153. Mass–Energy Equivalence

Origin

Einstein established mass–energy equivalence in 1905, within special relativity. Later studies of nuclear reactions illustrated the practical significance of differences in rest mass.

Core idea

Rest energy is intrinsic to a system. Total energy includes rest and motion contributions; a body's mass need not increase with speed in modern terminology. Energy and momentum together satisfy a relativistic relation that also describes photons.

Key terms and formulas

E₀ = mc², with rest mass m in kg and rest energy E₀ in joules. E² = (pc)² + (mc²)², where momentum p is in kg·m/s. For a change of rest mass Δm, released energy magnitude may be ΔE = |Δm|c² if the complete energy balance is accounted for.

How to apply it

Identify whether a problem asks for rest energy or released energy. For nuclear conversion use the mass difference, not the full initial mass. Convert grams to kilograms and keep units until the result.

Worked example

For Δm = 1.0 mg = 1.0 · 10⁻⁶ kg, ΔE = Δmc² ≈ (1.0 · 10⁻⁶ kg)(3.00 · 10⁸ m/s)² = 9.0 · 10¹⁰ J. Try it: rest energy of 2.0 mg? (1.8 · 10¹¹ J.)

Limits and common mistakes

Do not assume the whole mass of nuclear fuel is converted into energy. Released energy comes from a mass difference; the complete system conserves total energy and momentum.

Related theories

Postulates of Special Relativity (#150); Photons (#155); Nuclear Forces, Mass Defect and Binding Energy (#164)

154. Thermal Radiation and Planck's Hypothesis

Origin

In 1900 Max Planck introduced energy quanta to reproduce blackbody spectra. This marked an essential beginning of quantum physics.

Core idea

Hotter blackbodies emit more power per area and reach maximum spectral emission at shorter wavelengths. Discrete energy exchanges were a radical departure from classical continuous energy assumptions. Real surfaces have emissivity below or close to unity, depending on wavelength.

Key terms and formulas

Photon or oscillator energy quantum E = hν, with h = 6.626 070 15 · 10⁻³⁴ J·s and frequency ν in Hz. Wien's displacement law λmaxT ≈ 2.898 · 10⁻³ m·K. Ideal blackbody radiant exitance M = σT⁴, with σ ≈ 5.67 · 10⁻⁸ W/(m²·K⁴); T must be in kelvins.

How to apply it

Identify whether the question concerns the peak wavelength, individual quantum energy or total emitted power. Convert Celsius to kelvins, then choose the appropriate relation.

Worked example

For a blackbody at T = 5800 K, λmax = (2.898 · 10⁻³ m·K)/(5800 K) ≈ 5.00 · 10⁻⁷ m = 500 nm. Try it: peak wavelength at 2900 K? (About 1000 nm.)

Limits and common mistakes

Ideal blackbody laws are not exact for every real surface. Use absolute temperature in K; avoid interpreting peak wavelength as the only emitted wavelength.

Related theories

The Electromagnetic Spectrum (#135); Photons (#155); The Photoelectric Effect (#156)

155. Photons

Origin

Planck introduced energy quanta in 1900; Einstein argued for light quanta in 1905. Later experiments, including those on radiation pressure and photon scattering, strengthened the photon description.

Core idea

Higher-frequency light has more energetic photons. Increasing intensity at unchanged frequency means more photons per second, not greater energy per photon. A single photon in vacuum moves at c and has momentum along its direction of propagation.

Key terms and formulas

E = hν = hc/λ, p = E/c = h/λ. Use h = 6.626 · 10⁻³⁴ J·s, c = 3.00 · 10⁸ m/s, frequency ν in Hz, wavelength λ in metres, energy E in joules and momentum p in kg·m/s.

How to apply it

Convert wavelength into metres. Determine one-photon energy with hc/λ, then divide beam power by photon energy to count photons per second. Never confuse beam power in W with photon energy in J. Avoid looking into laser beams.

Worked example

At λ = 500 nm, E ≈ (6.626 · 10⁻³⁴ J·s)(3.00 · 10⁸ m/s)/(5.00 · 10⁻⁷ m) = 3.98 · 10⁻¹⁹ J. A 1.0 mW beam delivers ≈ (1.0 · 10⁻³ J/s)/(3.98 · 10⁻¹⁹ J) = 2.5 · 10¹⁵ photons/s. Try it: does doubling power at fixed λ double photons/s? (Yes.)

Limits and common mistakes

A photon is not a tiny classical ball; wave effects remain important. Count rate depends on incident power and photon energy; protect eyes from lasers.

Related theories

Thermal Radiation and Planck's Hypothesis (#154); The Photoelectric Effect (#156); Wave–Particle Duality and de Broglie Waves (#157)

156. The Photoelectric Effect

Origin

Heinrich Hertz observed related photoelectric phenomena in 1887, and Oleksandr Stoletov studied photocurrent laws. Einstein explained the effect using light quanta in 1905.

Core idea

One photon transfers energy to one electron in the simplest model. The work function is the minimum energy required to leave the surface. Remaining energy becomes electron kinetic energy; a stopping potential can measure its maximum value. Internal photoeffects underlie many semiconductor sensors and solar cells.

Key terms and formulas

hν = A + Kmax; Kmax = eUstop = (1/2)mvmax² in a nonrelativistic model; threshold ν₀ = A/h and λ₀ = hc/A. Here A is work function in J, e = 1.602 · 10⁻¹⁹ C, Ustop in volts, m electron mass in kg.

How to apply it

Convert work function to joules or calculate consistently in eV. Find photon energy, subtract A and check if the result is positive. If negative, there is no external emission in the single-photon model.

Worked example

A surface has A = 2.0 eV and photons E = 3.0 eV. Kmax = 3.0 eV − 2.0 eV = 1.0 eV, hence Ustop = 1.0 V. Try it: E = 1.5 eV on this surface? (No photoelectrons in the basic model.)

Limits and common mistakes

The one-photon metal model has exceptions under intense multiphoton illumination and in semiconductors. Light intensity above threshold controls electron count more directly than maximum kinetic energy.

Related theories

Photons (#155); Thermal Radiation and Planck's Hypothesis (#154); Wave–Particle Duality and de Broglie Waves (#157)

157. Wave–Particle Duality and de Broglie Waves

Origin

Louis de Broglie proposed matter waves in 1924. Davisson and Germer observed electron diffraction in 1927, confirming this central prediction.

Core idea

A moving quantum particle is described by a wavefunction, not by a miniature material wave rolling alongside a classical ball. The de Broglie wavelength depends on momentum, so increasing momentum shortens the wavelength. Electron diffraction reveals crystal spacing.

Key terms and formulas

λ = h/p, with h = 6.626 · 10⁻³⁴ J·s, momentum p in kg·m/s and wavelength λ in metres. For nonrelativistic motion p ≈ mv; at higher speeds use relativistic momentum.

How to apply it

Decide whether speeds are low enough for p = mv. Convert all inputs to SI, calculate p and then h/p. Compare the result with atomic spacings near 10⁻¹⁰ m.

Worked example

An electron with nonrelativistic momentum p = 6.63 · 10⁻²⁴ kg·m/s has λ = (6.626 · 10⁻³⁴ J·s)/(6.63 · 10⁻²⁴ kg·m/s) ≈ 1.00 · 10⁻¹⁰ m = 0.100 nm. Try it: what happens to λ if p doubles? (It halves.)

Limits and common mistakes

Use relativistic momentum if v is close to c. A de Broglie wave is a quantum description, not mechanical vibration of surrounding space.

Related theories

Photons (#155); The Photoelectric Effect (#156); The Uncertainty Principle (#158)

158. The Uncertainty Principle

Origin

Werner Heisenberg formulated the uncertainty principle in 1927. It emerged from the mathematical structure of quantum mechanics and wave-packet descriptions.

Core idea

Position uncertainty Δx and momentum uncertainty Δp are statistical spreads for many identically prepared systems. Making one spread smaller generally requires the conjugate spread to become larger. The effect is negligible for many macroscopic motions because Planck's constant is so small.

Key terms and formulas

For standard deviations, ΔxΔp ≥ ħ/2, where ħ = h/(2π) ≈ 1.055 · 10⁻³⁴ J·s. Δx is in metres and Δp in kg·m/s, so their product has units J·s. This inequality is not an exact equality for arbitrary states.

How to apply it

Identify the specified position spread and solve for the minimum compatible momentum spread. Use Δp ≥ ħ/(2Δx); do not confuse spread with average momentum or attribute it solely to measurement disturbance.

Worked example

For an electron localized to Δx = 1.0 nm = 1.0 · 10⁻⁹ m, Δp ≥ (1.055 · 10⁻³⁴ J·s)/(2.0 · 10⁻⁹ m) ≈ 5.3 · 10⁻²⁶ kg·m/s. Try it: halve Δx; what happens to the lower bound? (It doubles.)

Limits and common mistakes

The inequality concerns statistical spreads in a quantum state. It does not imply a measurement apparatus secretly pushes every particle; equality applies only to special states.

Related theories

Wave–Particle Duality and de Broglie Waves (#157); Photons (#155); Bohr's Postulates and Energy Levels (#160)

159. Rutherford's Nuclear Model of the Atom

Origin

Around 1909, Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden carried out gold-foil scattering studies under Ernest Rutherford. Rutherford proposed the nuclear atom in 1911, replacing Thomson's diffuse positive-charge picture.

Core idea

Alpha particles are positively charged helium nuclei. Strong deflection indicates encounter with a compact, positively charged region. Electrons occupy the surrounding atomic region; the classical picture of electrons simply orbiting the nucleus cannot explain atomic stability.

Key terms and formulas

Typical atomic radius is about 10⁻¹⁰ m and nuclear radius is about 10⁻¹⁵ m, a ratio of roughly 10⁵. Coulomb repulsion between an alpha particle and the nucleus becomes strong at short separations; scattering depends on impact parameter and particle energy.

How to apply it

Compare the expected scattering for diffuse positive charge with the observed rare large-angle events. For size problems, divide atomic radius by nuclear radius, keeping the same units.

Worked example

If an atom has radius 1.0 · 10⁻¹⁰ m and its nucleus radius 1.0 · 10⁻¹⁵ m, the ratio is (1.0 · 10⁻¹⁰ m)/(1.0 · 10⁻¹⁵ m) = 1.0 · 10⁵. Try it: is the nucleus about one hundred thousand times smaller in radius? (Yes.)

Limits and common mistakes

The nuclear model does not explain stable electron energies or line spectra. Classical orbiting electrons would radiate, so quantum ideas are necessary.

Related theories

Electric Charge and Its Conservation (#85); Bohr's Postulates and Energy Levels (#160); Structure of the Atomic Nucleus and Isotopes (#163)

160. Bohr's Postulates and Energy Levels

Origin

Niels Bohr proposed his atomic model in 1913 to explain the hydrogen spectrum while addressing the instability of Rutherford's classical atomic picture.

Core idea

Hydrogen energy levels are negative relative to a separated electron and proton. A transition downward emits a photon whose energy equals the decrease in atomic energy; an upward transition absorbs the needed energy. Modern quantum mechanics replaces classical electron orbits with orbitals and probability distributions.

Key terms and formulas

For hydrogen Eₙ ≈ −13.6 eV/n², where n = 1, 2, 3… and 1 eV = 1.602 · 10⁻¹⁹ J. For emission hν = Eupper − Elower > 0 and λ = hc/(Eupper − Elower). Use hc ≈ 1240 eV·nm for convenient wavelength calculations.

How to apply it

Identify initial and final principal quantum numbers. Calculate both signed level energies, subtract lower from upper for emitted photon energy, then convert to wavelength. Do not confuse energy sign with photon energy.

Worked example

For hydrogen transition n = 3 to n = 2, E₃ ≈ −13.6/9 eV = −1.51 eV; E₂ = −13.6/4 eV = −3.40 eV. Eγ = −1.51 eV − (−3.40 eV) = 1.89 eV; λ ≈ 1240/1.89 nm = 656 nm. Try it: energy of n = 1? (−13.6 eV.)

Limits and common mistakes

Bohr’s simple formulas describe hydrogen and hydrogen-like one-electron ions with adjustments, not arbitrary many-electron atoms. An electron is not a planet on a classical path.

Related theories

Rutherford's Nuclear Model of the Atom (#159); Photons (#155); Emission and Absorption Spectra (#161)

161. Emission and Absorption Spectra

Origin

In the early nineteenth century Joseph von Fraunhofer mapped dark lines in sunlight. Gustav Kirchhoff and Robert Bunsen connected characteristic lines to chemical elements in the 1850s and 1860s. Quantum energy levels later explained why each atom has distinctive spectral lines.

Core idea

Electrons in isolated atoms can occupy discrete energy states. An atom emits a photon when its energy decreases and absorbs one when a photon supplies the difference between two allowed states. Dense hot materials usually create broad continuous spectra. A low-density excited gas often creates an emission-line spectrum. Light crossing cooler gas may show an absorption spectrum superimposed on a continuous background.

Key terms and formulas

The photon relation is ΔE = hν = hc / λ. Here ΔE is energy difference in joules (J), h ≈ 6.626 · 10⁻³⁴ J·s, ν is frequency in hertz (Hz), c ≈ 3.00 · 10⁸ m/s and λ is wavelength in metres (m). A line at a given wavelength identifies a transition, not necessarily one unique element without comparison against other lines. Spectral-line shifts can also reflect motion.

How to apply it

Identify whether the spectrum is continuous, emission or absorption. Measure several line wavelengths, not just one, and compare them with known reference spectra. For astronomy, allow for Doppler shifts before matching elements. When assessing composition, remember that emission strength depends on excitation, temperature and density as well as abundance.

Worked example

A spectral line has wavelength 656 nm = 6.56 · 10⁻⁷ m. Its photon energy is E = hc / λ ≈ (6.626 · 10⁻³⁴ J·s)(3.00 · 10⁸ m/s)/(6.56 · 10⁻⁷ m) ≈ 3.03 · 10⁻¹⁹ J ≈ 1.89 eV. Try it: what frequency corresponds to 500 nm? (Answer: 6.00 · 10¹⁴ Hz.)

Limits and common mistakes

An element can show many lines, and different ionisation states create different spectra. A single dark band is not proof of a particular substance. Real stars have broadened and blended lines; a simple isolated-atom diagram is not enough to infer abundance. Never confuse a continuous spectrum with a continuous range of atomic energies.

Related theories

Bohr's Postulates and Energy Levels (#160); Photons (#155); Dispersion of Light and Color (#146)

162. Lasers

Origin

Albert Einstein described stimulated emission in 1917. Theodore Maiman demonstrated the first working laser with a ruby crystal in 1960, building on earlier maser research. The acronym LASER refers to light amplification by stimulated emission of radiation.

Core idea

An excited atom can emit a photon spontaneously, with essentially random direction and phase. A passing photon of the right energy can instead stimulate emission of another photon in the same mode. To achieve net optical amplification, a gain medium must be pumped so that the relevant upper state has greater population than the lower state. A resonator can provide feedback, while an output mirror lets some light escape.

Key terms and formulas

Photon energy follows E = hν = hc / λ, with h = 6.626 · 10⁻³⁴ J·s, frequency ν in Hz, c ≈ 3.00 · 10⁸ m/s and wavelength λ in m. Pumping supplies energy to the laser medium; a population inversion is needed for gain on the laser transition. Coherence means a predictable phase relation over a suitable time or distance, not that all lasers are perfectly monochromatic.

How to apply it

First identify the gain medium, pumping mechanism and optical cavity. Distinguish laser output power in watts (W) from energy in joules (J); for an interval t, output energy is Q = Pt. Select wavelength and beam properties for the application. Follow laser classification and eye-protection requirements instead of estimating safety from apparent brightness.

Worked example

A low-power laser emits P = 2.0 mW = 0.0020 W for t = 30 s. Its emitted energy is Q = Pt = 0.0020 W · 30 s = 0.060 J. Try it: how much energy does a 1.0 mW beam emit in 10 s? (Answer: 0.010 J.)

Limits and common mistakes

Laser beams are not perfectly parallel and their spectral widths are not literally zero. Not every source with narrow-band light is a laser. Optical power alone does not establish eye safety because wavelength, exposure and beam geometry matter. Avoid direct or reflected exposure; powerful lasers may also burn skin or ignite materials.

Related theories

Bohr's Postulates and Energy Levels (#160); Photons (#155); The Photoelectric Effect (#156)

163. Structure of the Atomic Nucleus and Isotopes

Origin

Ernest Rutherford established the nuclear character of atoms in the early twentieth century. James Chadwick discovered the neutron in 1932. Nuclear isotope notation developed as proton numbers and neutron numbers became measurable.

Core idea

Each proton carries charge +e and each neutron has zero net electric charge. A nucleus with Z protons has charge +Ze. The atom is electrically neutral when the surrounding electron count equals Z. Isotopes have the same Z but different neutron counts and therefore different masses and sometimes different stability. Chemical behaviour is dominated by electrons, so isotopes of one element usually have similar chemistry.

Key terms and formulas

The mass number A = Z + N is a count, not a mass measured in kilograms. Nuclides may be written as ¹⁴₆C, where the upper number is A and the lower is Z. Nuclear charge Q = Ze with e ≈ 1.602 · 10⁻¹⁹ C. Nuclear radius is approximately R = r₀A^(1/3), where r₀ ≈ 1.2 · 10⁻¹⁵ m; this is an empirical approximation for nuclei.

How to apply it

Read the element symbol or periodic-table atomic number to obtain Z. Use the upper index for A, then compute N = A − Z. When comparing isotopes, keep Z fixed and vary N. Estimate sizes with the radius formula only as an order-of-magnitude model; never substitute A directly for atomic mass in precision calculations.

Worked example

For ²³₁₁Na, Z = 11 and A = 23. Therefore N = 23 − 11 = 12 neutrons; a neutral atom also has 11 electrons. Try it: how many neutrons are in ¹⁸₈O? (Answer: 18 − 8 = 10.)

Limits and common mistakes

Mass number is not an exact atomic mass: binding energy creates a mass defect. Nuclei are not uniform hard spheres, and the radius law is approximate. Do not confuse isotopes (different neutrons) with ions (different electrons), or assume all isotopes are radioactive.

Related theories

Rutherford's Nuclear Model of the Atom (#159); Nuclear Forces, Mass Defect and Binding Energy (#164); Radioactivity: Alpha, Beta and Gamma Radiation (#165)

164. Nuclear Forces, Mass Defect and Binding Energy

Origin

Einstein established mass–energy equivalence in 1905. Measurements of atomic and nuclear masses later established mass defects. Nuclear force models developed during the twentieth century to explain the strong attraction between nearby nucleons despite electric repulsion between protons.

Core idea

Nucleons attract at nuclear distances through the residual strong interaction, which is short-ranged and saturating. A stable bound nucleus has lower energy than its free constituents. Energy must be supplied to pull it apart. Average binding energy per nucleon increases strongly among light nuclei and peaks around the iron-nickel region, then tends to fall for much heavier ones. This shape explains energy release from reactions toward more tightly bound products.

Key terms and formulas

For bare nucleons and a nucleus, Δm = Zmₚ + Nmₙ − m_nucleus. If using neutral atomic masses, include hydrogen-atom masses consistently to account approximately for electrons: Δm ≈ Zm_H + Nmₙ − m_atom. Binding energy Eᵦ = Δmc². One unified atomic mass unit corresponds to about 931.5 MeV/c², so Eᵦ(MeV) ≈ Δm(u) · 931.5. Binding energy per nucleon is Eᵦ / A.

How to apply it

Choose one mass convention and do not mix bare nuclear and neutral atomic masses without electron corrections. Find Z and N, subtract the product mass from the free-constituent mass, then multiply by c² or 931.5 MeV per u. Divide by A only when asked for average binding energy per nucleon. Compare initial and final total mass to determine reaction energy.

Worked example

A hypothetical nucleus has Δm = 0.010 u. Its binding energy is Eᵦ ≈ 0.010 · 931.5 MeV = 9.315 MeV ≈ 9.3 MeV. Try it: what is Eᵦ for Δm = 0.020 u? (Answer: 18.6 MeV.)

Limits and common mistakes

More binding energy per nucleon does not automatically imply that a particular reaction proceeds without a barrier. The nuclear force is not just a classical short-range force; quantum physics is essential. The mass defect of a nucleus must not be confused with a loss of total mass-energy of an isolated system.

Related theories

Mass–Energy Equivalence (#153); Structure of the Atomic Nucleus and Isotopes (#163); Nuclear Fission and Nuclear Reactors (#168); Nuclear Fusion (#169)

165. Radioactivity: Alpha, Beta and Gamma Radiation

Origin

Henri Becquerel discovered spontaneous uranium radiation in 1896. Marie and Pierre Curie studied radioactivity and identified new radioactive elements. Later experiments distinguished alpha, beta and gamma radiation and established nuclear transformation rules associated with Frederick Soddy and Kazimierz Fajans.

Core idea

Alpha decay ejects two protons and two neutrons from an unstable nucleus. Beta-minus decay converts a neutron into a proton while emitting an electron and an electron antineutrino; beta-plus converts a proton into a neutron and emits a positron and neutrino. Gamma emission releases excitation energy without changing proton or nucleon counts. Radioactivity is a property of unstable nuclei, not a consequence of ordinary heating.

Key terms and formulas

In alpha decay, A decreases by 4 and Z by 2. In β⁻ decay, A is unchanged and Z increases by 1; in β⁺ decay, A is unchanged and Z decreases by 1. In gamma emission, A and Z remain unchanged. Nuclear charge number Z and mass number A are counts without SI units. Alpha radiation has charge +2e, beta particles charge −e or +e and gamma photons charge zero.

How to apply it

Identify the emitted radiation and write the parent nucleus with mass number A and charge number Z. Apply the corresponding change to both numbers, then determine the daughter element from its new Z. Check conservation of charge and nucleon number. For protection, rely on established radiation-safety guidance and avoid handling unknown sources.

Worked example

In alpha decay, ²³⁸₉₂U changes its mass number to 238 − 4 = 234 and charge number to 92 − 2 = 90. The daughter is ²³⁴₉₀Th, with an alpha particle emitted. Try it: what Z results from β⁻ decay of a nucleus with Z = 6? (Answer: Z = 7; A stays the same.)

Limits and common mistakes

Penetration depends on radiation energy and shielding material; simple labels such as “gamma always passes through everything” are wrong. Beta decay also includes a neutrino or antineutrino, which cannot be omitted from the full physical account. External exposure and contamination inside the body pose different risks. Radiation cannot be judged safe by whether it can be seen or felt.

Related theories

Structure of the Atomic Nucleus and Isotopes (#163); The Law of Radioactive Decay and Half-Life (#166); Ionizing Radiation: Dose and Protection (#170)

166. The Law of Radioactive Decay and Half-Life

Origin

Ernest Rutherford and Frederick Soddy developed the exponential description of radioactive decay in the early twentieth century. Willard Libby later established radiocarbon dating using the decay of carbon-14.

Core idea

Each unstable nucleus has an approximately constant probability of decay per unit time when its physical state is unchanged. Consequently, the number of surviving nuclei decreases exponentially. Activity counts decays per second, so it decreases at the same rate as the number of undecayed nuclei. Half-life belongs to a particular radionuclide and usually is not changed by ordinary temperature or pressure.

Key terms and formulas

N(t) = N₀ · 2^(−t/T) = N₀e^(−λt), where N and N₀ are numbers of nuclei, t and T are times in the same unit, and decay constant λ = ln 2 / T has unit s⁻¹ when T is in seconds. Activity A = λN is in becquerels (Бq in SI notation Bq), where 1 Bq = 1 decay/s. For a pure sample, remaining mass m follows the same decay fraction as N.

How to apply it

Determine elapsed time t and half-life T. Compute the number of half-lives t/T, then multiply the initial sample amount by 2^(−t/T). For activity, calculate λ first in s⁻¹ and use A = λN. Distinguish the remaining parent isotope from stable or radioactive daughter products that may remain in the sample.

Worked example

A radioactive sample begins with 80 mg of the parent nuclide. After two half-lives the remaining parent mass is m = 80 mg · 2⁻² = 20 mg. Try it: how much parent remains from 64 mg after three half-lives? (Answer: 8 mg.)

Limits and common mistakes

The half-life does not mean every nucleus survives exactly T seconds. The activity of mixed isotopes cannot be described by one half-life without specifying components. Radiocarbon dating requires calibration and assumptions about initial carbon exchange; contamination can invalidate a result.

Related theories

Radioactivity: Alpha, Beta and Gamma Radiation (#165); Ionizing Radiation: Dose and Protection (#170); Structure of the Atomic Nucleus and Isotopes (#163)

167. Nuclear Reactions

Origin

Rutherford reported the first artificial nuclear transmutation in 1919, converting nitrogen through alpha-particle bombardment. James Chadwick discovered the neutron in 1932, making later nuclear-reaction studies more effective.

Core idea

A projectile such as a proton, neutron or alpha particle can interact with a target nucleus and rearrange its constituents. A nuclear reaction may create new nuclides and eject particles. Energy and momentum must be conserved, along with electric charge and nucleon number. The released kinetic energy, called the Q-value when positive, derives from a decrease in total rest mass.

Key terms and formulas

For a reaction, ΣA_before = ΣA_after and ΣZ_before = ΣZ_after for the usual reactions without baryon-number-changing processes. Reaction energy Q = (Σm_initial − Σm_final)c². Here m is total rest mass in kg, c ≈ 3.00 · 10⁸ m/s, and Q is in joules (J). With masses in u, Q(MeV) ≈ Δm(u) · 931.5. The sign of Q indicates exothermic (+) or endothermic (−) energy balance.

How to apply it

Write all incoming and outgoing nuclei with A and Z. Add each column separately and solve for the unknown particle or nuclide. Check mass and charge conservation, then use accurately matched mass data to calculate Q if asked. A positive Q does not imply a negligible activation or Coulomb barrier.

Worked example

Consider ¹⁴₇N + ⁴₂He → ¹⁷₈O + X. Initial A is 14 + 4 = 18, so X has A = 18 − 17 = 1. Initial Z is 7 + 2 = 9, so X has Z = 9 − 8 = 1: X is ¹₁H, a proton. Try it: which particle has A = 1 and Z = 0? (Answer: a neutron.)

Limits and common mistakes

Conservation of A and Z is necessary but not sufficient to establish a reaction channel; energy, momentum and quantum rules matter too. Do not use ordinary chemical molecular masses as if they were exact nuclear masses. Nuclear reactions should never be attempted outside authorised facilities.

Related theories

Structure of the Atomic Nucleus and Isotopes (#163); Nuclear Forces, Mass Defect and Binding Energy (#164); Nuclear Fission and Nuclear Reactors (#168)

168. Nuclear Fission and Nuclear Reactors

Origin

Otto Hahn and Fritz Strassmann reported key uranium-fission chemical evidence in 1938; Lise Meitner and Otto Frisch explained the process in 1939. The first controlled self-sustaining chain reaction was achieved in Chicago in 1942. The 1986 Chornobyl disaster exposed severe reactor-design and operational failures.

Core idea

When a suitable heavy nucleus, such as uranium-235, absorbs a neutron, it may become unstable and split into two main fragments, releasing energy and additional neutrons. A sustained chain reaction needs enough neutrons from one generation to cause fissions in the next. A moderator can slow neutrons in thermal reactors, while control rods absorb neutrons to regulate reactivity. Coolant removes heat from the core.

Key terms and formulas

An illustrative energy scale is about 200 MeV released per uranium-235 fission, approximately 3.2 · 10⁻¹¹ J because 1 eV = 1.602 · 10⁻¹⁹ J. Reactor power P = E / t is measured in watts (W); electricity output is smaller than the heat release because conversion efficiency is less than 100%. The neutron multiplication factor k describes chain-reaction growth: k = 1 is steady critical operation, k < 1 subcritical, k > 1 supercritical.

How to apply it

In a textbook calculation, convert energy per fission from MeV to joules, multiply by the number of fissions, and divide by elapsed time for average thermal power. Separate thermal power from electrical output. For conceptual diagrams, identify fuel, moderator if present, control elements, cooling system and containment. Reactor design and operation require specialised licensed professionals.

Worked example

If 1.0 · 10¹⁸ fissions each release approximately 200 MeV, then energy per fission is 200 · 10⁶ eV · 1.602 · 10⁻¹⁹ J/eV ≈ 3.20 · 10⁻¹¹ J. Total energy ≈ 1.0 · 10¹⁸ · 3.20 · 10⁻¹¹ J = 3.20 · 10⁷ J. Try it: what energy is released by 2.0 · 10¹⁸ such fissions? (Answer: 6.4 · 10⁷ J.)

Limits and common mistakes

Not all reactors use a moderator, and critical operation is not the same as a dangerous uncontrolled event. Energy per fission varies with reaction products. Simplified diagrams omit delayed neutrons and important safety systems. Chornobyl cannot be explained as an inevitable result of nuclear power: specific design and operational factors mattered. Never attempt to handle nuclear material.

Related theories

Nuclear Forces, Mass Defect and Binding Energy (#164); Radioactivity: Alpha, Beta and Gamma Radiation (#165); Nuclear Reactions (#167); Ionizing Radiation: Dose and Protection (#170)

169. Nuclear Fusion

Origin

Arthur Eddington proposed that stellar energy could come from nuclear processes in the 1920s; Hans Bethe later explained key stellar fusion pathways. Controlled fusion research expanded in the mid-twentieth century. ITER is an international tokamak project designed to study burning-plasma conditions rather than to sell electricity.

Core idea

Light nuclei are positively charged and repel electrically, so approaching closely enough for the strong interaction requires high energies or quantum tunnelling. In the Sun gravity confines hot plasma, and fusion proceeds mainly through proton-proton chains. Experimental fusion systems may use deuterium and tritium. A tokamak holds ionised gas, called plasma, using magnetic fields to reduce contact with vessel walls.

Key terms and formulas

For deuterium-tritium fusion, ²₁H + ³₁H → ⁴₂He + ¹₀n + 17.6 MeV. The released energy is Q ≈ 17.6 · 10⁶ eV · 1.602 · 10⁻¹⁹ J/eV ≈ 2.82 · 10⁻¹² J per reaction. Plasma temperature is measured in kelvins (K) or energy units such as keV; 1 eV corresponds to about 1.16 · 10⁴ K for kT. Confinement time and density are also essential.

How to apply it

Balance mass number and charge number on both sides of a fusion equation. Convert the reaction energy to joules and multiply by the number of reactions. In conceptual questions, distinguish the stellar gravitational confinement from laboratory magnetic or inertial confinement, and separate scientific plasma gain from net power delivered to the electricity grid.

Worked example

One deuterium-tritium fusion reaction releases approximately 17.6 MeV. Converting units gives 17.6 · 10⁶ · 1.602 · 10⁻¹⁹ J ≈ 2.82 · 10⁻¹² J. Try it: roughly how much energy comes from 1.0 · 10¹² such reactions? (Answer: 2.82 J.)

Limits and common mistakes

High temperature alone does not make a practical power plant: density, confinement, losses and materials determine viability. Net plasma energy gain is not the same as net electricity production. Fusion reactions can produce energetic neutrons, so activation and shielding still matter. Simplified “two nuclei combine into one” descriptions may omit emitted particles.

Related theories

Nuclear Forces, Mass Defect and Binding Energy (#164); Nuclear Reactions (#167); Nuclear Fission and Nuclear Reactors (#168)

170. Ionizing Radiation: Dose and Protection

Origin

Radiation measurements developed after the discoveries of X-rays and radioactivity in the 1890s. The gray and sievert became standard units for absorbed and radiation-weighted dose. International bodies including the ICRP and IAEA developed frameworks for radiation protection.

Core idea

Alpha, beta, gamma and other ionizing radiations can ionize atoms and damage molecules including DNA. The same deposited energy can have different biological consequences depending on radiation type, irradiated tissue and exposure conditions. A Geiger counter detects certain ionising events or count rates; it does not automatically report a reliable personal effective dose. A cloud chamber reveals tracks of charged particles through condensation.

Key terms and formulas

Absorbed dose D = E_dep / m, where E_dep is deposited energy in joules (J), mass m is in kilograms (kg), and 1 gray (Gy) = 1 J/kg. Equivalent dose for a tissue and radiation type is H = w_R D, where w_R is a dimensionless radiation weighting factor; H is in sieverts (Sv). Effective dose adds tissue weighting to assess stochastic risk. A typical global average natural-background effective dose is a few mSv per year, with large geographic variation.

How to apply it

For an absorbed-dose exercise, divide energy deposited by irradiated mass and use Gy. To discuss biological impact, check whether the question asks for absorbed, equivalent or effective dose, since they are different quantities. In a real protection setting, reduce unnecessary exposure time, maximise distance where relevant and use shielding appropriate to the radiation type. Follow official radiological advice.

Worked example

Radiation deposits E_dep = 0.002 J in tissue of mass m = 1.0 kg. The absorbed dose is D = 0.002 J / 1.0 kg = 0.002 Gy = 2.0 mGy. Try it: what absorbed dose results from 0.003 J deposited in 1.5 kg? (Answer: 0.002 Gy.)

Limits and common mistakes

Gy and Sv are not interchangeable and a Geiger counter reading is not automatically a dose in sieverts. Equivalent dose is not a simple prediction of injury to one person, and risk also depends on dose rate, organ and exposure. Time-distance-shielding guidelines must be applied with source type and contamination pathways in mind; do not handle unknown radioactive materials.

Related theories

Radioactivity: Alpha, Beta and Gamma Radiation (#165); The Law of Radioactive Decay and Half-Life (#166); Nuclear Fission and Nuclear Reactors (#168)

171. Elementary Particles and Fundamental Interactions

Origin

Particle physics developed through the discovery of the electron in 1897, the proposal of quarks in 1964 and many accelerator experiments. The ATLAS and CMS collaborations at CERN announced a new particle consistent with the Higgs boson in 2012.

Core idea

Elementary particles have no currently observed internal structure. Six quark flavours and six lepton flavours form three generations. Protons contain two up quarks and one down quark, while neutrons contain one up and two down quarks. Each charged particle has an antiparticle with opposite charge. Photons mediate electromagnetic interactions, gluons the strong interaction and W and Z bosons the weak interaction. The Higgs field is associated with masses of elementary particles.

Key terms and formulas

The elementary charge has magnitude e ≈ 1.602 · 10⁻¹⁹ C. Up-type quarks carry charge +2e/3, down-type quarks −e/3, and electrons −e; neutrinos have zero electric charge. For a proton, Q = 2(+2e/3) + (−e/3) = +e. For a neutron, Q = (+2e/3) + 2(−e/3) = 0. Quarks also possess colour charge, which is not visual colour.

How to apply it

To analyse particle composition, list each constituent and add its electric charge in units of e. Distinguish the strong interaction binding quarks inside nucleons from its residual form between nucleons. Identify which of the four interactions governs an example, such as electromagnetic forces in atoms or weak processes in beta decay. Remember that the Standard Model does not explain all cosmological observations.

Worked example

A proton contains two up quarks of charge +2e/3 each and one down quark of charge −e/3. Its charge is Q = +2e/3 + 2e/3 − e/3 = +e = +1.602 · 10⁻¹⁹ C. Try it: what is the charge of a neutron with one up and two down quarks? (Answer: 0 C.)

Limits and common mistakes

Protons and neutrons are composite, not elementary. The Standard Model does not incorporate gravity as a quantum field theory and does not by itself explain dark matter. Antiparticles are not particles with negative mass. The Higgs field does not account for all the mass of ordinary matter: most proton mass comes from strong-interaction energy.
